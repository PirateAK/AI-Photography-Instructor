## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT

**Welcome**

The Intermediate flash lesson used light attached to the camera — which means the light always comes from where you stand. Take the flash off the camera and the angle becomes a creative decision for the first time. This is the step that turns flash from convenience into craft.

**Why off-camera: the angle becomes yours**

On-camera flash hits the subject square in the face — flat, harsh, and shadowless in the wrong way. The same flash a meter to the side produces modeling: a shadow side, a highlight side, shape. Off-camera light is how a photograph stops looking like a snapshot of an event and starts looking like a lit portrait.

**The kit and the sync basics**

The minimum kit: a flash with manual power control, a way to trigger it off-camera (a wireless trigger, or your camera's optical slave system), and a light stand. Sync is the one technical fact to verify: the fastest shutter speed that works with flash varies by camera — beyond it, black bands appear. Start at or below your camera's sync speed and let aperture and ISO handle brightness.

**Modifiers: size and diffusion at work**

A bare flash is a small, hard source: crisp shadows, punchy contrast. Put it behind an umbrella or a softbox and the source becomes larger — soft shadows, gradual falloff. The Intermediate lesson's rule holds and scales: bigger relative to the subject equals softer. A modifier also costs light, so power rises to compensate — one lever at a time, as always.

**Demonstration: one light, four positions**

One subject, one flash, one modifier — four frames. At 45 degrees: classic modeling, the portrait standard. At 90 degrees: dramatic split light. Above and slightly behind: rim and depth. Below: the horror-movie look — deliberately wrong, to prove the point. Same power, same subject; only the angle changed. Then the modifier comparison: bare versus umbrella, same position — watch the shadow edges.

**Building your first two-light habit**

When one light feels natural, the next step is a second light doing one job: a reflector or a second flash for fill, at lower power than the key. Key sets the look; fill softens what the key created. Two lights, two jobs — that habit scales all the way to studio work.

**Learner practice**

Build the one-light setup: a portrait at 45 degrees with a modifier, then move the light through three positions and compare. Change one lever at a time.

**Recap**

Off-camera flash turns the light's angle into a decision. Verify sync, choose a modifier for the shadow quality you want, and learn positions by moving one light through its arc. Key first, fill second — that's the whole studio in miniature.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE

Build the one-light portrait:

1. Flash on a stand (or held by a helper) at 45 degrees, modified. Shoot.
2. Move the light through three positions — 90 degrees, behind for rim, above — shooting at each.
3. Compare the four frames: where did the shadows go, and which position serves the face?
4. Add fill: a reflector opposite the key, or a second flash at lower power. Shoot once more.

Write down the position and power of each frame. If you're using a phone: most phones can't fire an external flash — practice the position lesson with a lamp instead; the levers are identical.

## 7. RECAP

Off-camera flash makes the light's angle yours. Verify sync, use a modifier to buy softness, and learn positions by moving one light through its arc — 45 for modeling, 90 for drama, behind for rim. Key sets the look; fill softens the key. That habit scales to any studio.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK

- Sync speed varies by camera and flash system; state the actual value and avoid universal claims.
- Trigger systems differ (radio, optical, cable) — demonstrate only the one filmed.
- TTL versus manual flash power behave differently; this lesson teaches manual — say so if TTL is mentioned.
- Inverse-square falloff is approximate in the narration; keep the "one lever at a time" discipline in all demonstrations.
- Verify stated flash power and positions against the actual setup footage.
