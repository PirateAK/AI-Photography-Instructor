# Advanced Lesson 3 — Multiple-Light Portraiture

Class: Advanced
Prerequisite: Advanced lessons on off-camera flash

## 1. LEARNING OUTCOME

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT

**Welcome**

Studio portraiture looks complicated and isn't. It's one key light doing the main work, plus up to three assistants each with a single job. Build it one light at a time and name each light's job aloud — that discipline is the whole lesson.

**The key: the light that decides**

The key is the brightest light and defines the portrait's character: its position sets the shadow pattern, its size sets the softness, its power sets the exposure everything else obeys. Every other light is measured AGAINST the key, never independently.

**Fill, rim, background: three supporting roles**

Fill lifts the shadow side the key created — a lower-power flash, a reflector, or nothing at all if you like the contrast. A rim (or hair light) sits behind and above, separating the subject from the background with an edge of light. A background light does exactly one thing: controls the tone or color of the backdrop independently of the subject. Three jobs, three lights — or fewer, if a reflector or the wall does the work.

**The ratio: how lights talk to each other**

Portrait lighting is ratios. A 2:1 key-to-fill ratio is gentle; 4:1 is dramatic; 8:1 is severe. You don't need a meter to start — you need the habit of changing one light's power and comparing frames. When the key alone looks right, add fill at two stops less, then judge. The ratio is the taste dial of portraiture.

**Demonstration: building the setup one light at a time**

Frame one: key only, 45 degrees, modified — a complete portrait already. Frame two: fill added at reduced power — shadows open, drama reduced. Frame three: rim added behind — the subject separates from the background. Frame four: background light raised — the backdrop lifts to gray. Four frames, four decisions, each one reversible. The final image isn't "better" than frame one — it's a different, more produced statement.

**The classic patterns, briefly**

The named patterns — Rembrandt, loop, butterfly, split — are simply key positions with recognizable shadow shapes. Learn them as landmarks, not laws: the position of the key produces the pattern, and the pattern produces the mood.

**Learner practice**

Build the setup from one light to three, photographing at each stage, and write down what each addition changed. Then choose which stage's portrait you actually prefer — the answer is taste, and it's allowed.

**Recap**

The key decides; fill opens the shadows; rim separates; background controls the backdrop. Ratios are the taste dial. Build one light at a time, name each job, and keep the frame you prefer — not the one with the most lights.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE

Build a portrait setup in four stages, photographing at each:

1. Key only — position and modifier chosen for the look.
2. Add fill at roughly two stops below the key.
3. Add a rim behind and above.
4. Adjust or add a background light.

Write one line per stage: what the light contributed. Then choose your favorite stage — more lights is a style, not a score.

If you're using a phone or have no flashes: the same build works with lamps and reflectors, and the phone as the camera. The roles, ratios, and review are identical.

## 7. RECAP

The key decides the character. Fill opens what the key closed. Rim separates the subject from the background. A background light controls the backdrop alone. Ratios are the taste dial — build one light at a time, name each job, and keep the portrait you prefer.
