## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT

**Welcome**

Composition so far has been about attention — what the eye finds. At the advanced level, composition carries MEANING: the same scene arranged differently tells a different story. This lesson is about the storytelling levers, and then about ordering images into a sequence.

**Gesture: the story in a body**

Gesture isn't just faces. Hands reveal what someone is doing; a shoulder's angle reveals tension or ease; a leaning body pulls the frame's energy in its direction. In street, documentary, and portrait work, the strongest frames usually contain a gesture mid-action — the moment the body tells the story the face might hide. Train yourself to see hands and posture first, faces second.

**Color as a narrative tool**

Color is not decoration; it is information. A single saturated element against muted surroundings says "this matters." A consistently warm palette says "nostalgia" before anyone reads the subject. And the ABSENCE of color — a drained, near-monochrome treatment — says "document" or "memory." Choose the palette the story needs, then hold it consistent across the sequence.

**Relationships: what talks to what in the frame**

Two elements in a frame start a conversation: the person and the sign they're walking past, the child and the shadow, the boat and the harbor it left. Advanced composition is managing those conversations — overlap, distance, and direction create relationships the viewer reads automatically. The question to ask before shooting: what in this frame should be RELATED, and what should be ISOLATED?

**Demonstration: one subject, several meanings**

One subject — a closed café. Frame one: wide, morning light, chairs stacked — "the day hasn't started." Frame two: tight on the door handle, hands entering — "someone arrives." Frame three: shot through the window from outside, reflections layered — "the observer outside." Same subject; three stories, told by viewpoint, layers, and gesture.

**The sequence: pictures in order**

A single photograph is a moment; a sequence is a sentence. Order changes meaning: the wide establishing frame first sets place, the detail second creates intimacy, the closer frame last lands the point. Reorder them and the story shifts. When assembling, look at the set as a reader, not a shooter: what did you feel first, and what do you want felt next?

**Learner practice**

Photograph one subject in several ways to tell different stories, then select three and arrange them into a short sequence. Write the story the sequence tells, in one sentence — and check the images actually tell it.

**Recap**

Gesture, color, and relationships carry meaning — arrange them deliberately. One subject, several stories, told by viewpoint and layering. And pictures in order form sentences: sequence is composition across time.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE

Photograph one subject — a place, an object, or a willing person — in several ways to tell different stories:

1. Vary viewpoint, layering, and gesture across frames.
2. Select the three strongest and arrange them into a sequence.
3. Reorder the sequence once and note how the meaning shifts.
4. Write one sentence stating the story your final sequence tells.

If you're using a phone: the storytelling levers are identical, and the gallery's ordering makes experimenting with sequences natural.

## 7. RECAP

Gesture tells stories in bodies; color tells them in mood; relationships tell them in the frame. One subject can carry several stories — and a sequence of images forms a sentence whose meaning changes with order. Compose for the story, then edit the story into a sequence.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK

- Eye-path and mood claims are craft conventions, not measured certainties; phrase as what photographers observe.
- The demonstration set must be genuinely one subject with meaning-changing variation, as narrated.
- Sequence reorder claims must be demonstrated with the actual images — verify the claimed meaning shift is visible.
- Example photos of gesture must genuinely depict the stated gesture; verify before publishing.
