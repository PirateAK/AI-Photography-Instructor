# Advanced Lesson 6 — Long Exposure and Night Photography

Class: Advanced
Prerequisite: Intermediate lessons on motion, natural light, and metering

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT

**Welcome**

At night the rules reverse. There isn't enough light for the meter's guesses to matter, autofocus struggles, and every shake shows. Night photography is planned photography — and that planning is learnable.

**Stability: the tripod is the exposure**

When exposures stretch to seconds, the tripod isn't an accessory — it IS the exposure. Solid legs, weighted if windy, and a delayed shutter or remote so your finger never touches the camera during the frame. Mirrorless shooters get an extra gift: electronic first-curtain or fully electronic shutters eliminate the internal slap. The steadier the platform, the longer the exposure you can trust.

**Focusing in the dark**

Autofocus needs contrast, and darkness hides contrast. The reliable method: autofocus once on a bright edge or a light at the same distance as your subject, then switch the lens to manual and leave it there. Live view magnification — zoomed into the brightest thing in frame — confirms focus on the actual sensor. Compose after focus locks, and don't bump the ring.

**Exposure at night: math, tests, and patience**

Night exposure is test-driven. Start somewhere reasonable — a wide aperture, ISO high enough for a short test frame — take the shot, read the histogram, and adjust. Once the test frame is right, scale: drop ISO to where noise is acceptable and stretch the shutter by the same stops. Seconds-long exposures are normal; minutes exist for star fields, and the 500 rule (500 divided by focal length gives a rough star-point ceiling in seconds) is a starting approximation, not a law.

**Demonstration: moving lights at night**

A road at dusk, camera locked down. Thirty seconds: headlights and taillights paint continuous ribbons through the frame while the buildings stand still and sharp. The meter contributed nothing here — the first test frame told me the exposure was close, the histogram confirmed no clipping in the lit windows, and patience did the rest.

**Night honesty: noise, and when to go home**

Long exposures at high ISO accumulate noise and heat. Accept noise where the image earns it; expose to keep ISO as low as the shutter allows; and know when the scene is done — night photography rewards a few considered frames over a hundred hopeful ones. The battery also drains faster in the cold; carry a spare.

**Learner practice**

Photograph a night scene — moving lights, a lit building, or a street — using the full method: tripod, pre-focused, test-driven exposure, final frame.

**Recap**

At night, the tripod is the exposure and focus happens once, deliberately, on the live-view zoom. Exposure is test-driven — high ISO to preview, then scale down and stretch. Moving lights paint with time. Accept the noise the image earns, and quit while the frames are considered.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE

Photograph one night scene using the full method:

1. Tripod set solid; delayed shutter or remote ready.
2. Focus once on a light at subject distance; switch to manual and leave it.
3. Test frame at high ISO; read the histogram; scale to the final exposure.
4. Final frame — moving lights or a lit building — and review enlarged.

Write down the test values and the final values, and the stop-distance between them.

If you're using a phone: night mode does the stacking for you, but the discipline transfers — brace the phone, focus deliberately by tapping, and compare what the automatic stack produced with a manual Pro-mode attempt.

## 7. RECAP

Night reverses the rules: the tripod is the exposure, focus happens once on the magnified live view, and metering gives way to test frames scaled into final exposures. Moving lights paint with time. Accept the noise the image earns — and stop when the frames are considered.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK

- The 500 rule is a rough approximation that varies by sensor size and acceptable star trailing; present it as a starting point only.
- Long-exposure noise and hot pixels vary by camera, temperature, and exposure length; describe only the demonstrated behavior.
- State actual test and final values for every frame; night exposure values are scene-specific, never universal.
- Electronic shutter trade-offs (rolling shutter, star trailing differences) exist; if mentioned, present as model-dependent.
- Safety: night locations must be legal and safe; do not demonstrate from roadways or private property.
