# Advanced Lesson 10 — Image Selection and Sequencing

Class: Advanced
Prerequisite: Intermediate lesson on building a consistent editing workflow

## 1. LEARNING OUTCOME

By the end of this lesson, learners can assess a larger set of images, select the strongest work against stated criteria, and arrange it into a coherent series whose order shapes the viewing experience.

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT

**Welcome**

Anyone can press the shutter. The edit — assessing a whole set, choosing the strongest, ordering them — is where photographers are actually made. This lesson takes a forty-frame set down to a ten-image story, and the method matters more than the photographs.

**First pass: ruthless and fast**

The first pass is fast and merciless: every frame that is technically dead — misfocused, accidentally blurred, hopelessly exposed — goes immediately, without sentiment. Speed is the point: a slow first pass lets attachment grow around weak frames. Downvote with the thumb, not the heart.

**Second pass: criteria replace mood**

The survivors get the deliberate pass, and mood alone is no longer the test. State your criteria for THIS set — sharpness where it matters, the gesture that carries the story, the light that serves the mood, no duplicated compositions. Then judge each frame against the criteria, not against how you felt when you pressed the shutter. Two near-identical frames? Keep one — the better one, not the first one.

**Sequencing: order is meaning**

A series is read in order, and order is meaning. The opening frame sets the place or the tension; middles develop it — varied in framing and rhythm so the sequence breathes rather than repeats; the closing frame lands the feeling or opens the question. Reorder and the story shifts — the Advanced storytelling lesson's rule, applied across time. Read the sequence as a viewer who knows nothing, and fix the frames where attention stalls.

**Demonstration: forty frames to a ten-image story**

Here's a real set: forty frames from one morning walk. First pass: forty to twenty-two — the technically dead gone in minutes. Second pass: twenty-two to ten, judged against the stated criteria — the morning's light, the harbor's activity, no duplicate compositions. Sequencing: the wide harbor establishing frame opens; three activity frames build the middle; the detail — rope, gull, coffee — punctuates; and the closing frame is the empty dock, the morning ended. Ten images, one sentence: "the harbor wakes." The cut thirty weren't failures — they were the process.

**Killing your darlings**

The hardest cut is the frame you love that the set doesn't need — technically lovely, out of place. Keep a darlings folder. Cut it from the sequence anyway. The series is the artwork here, and the darling that breaks its rhythm weakens everything it touches. It hurts exactly as much as it should, and it's still right.

**Learner practice**

Take a set of at least thirty images from one shoot and edit it to a ten-image (or fewer) story, using two passes and stated criteria. Write the story's one-sentence summary — then verify the sequence tells it.

**Recap**

First pass ruthless and fast; second pass judged against stated criteria, not mood. Order is meaning — opening sets, middles develop, closing lands. And the darlings you love but the set doesn't need go to a folder of their own. The edit is where photographers are made.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE

Take a set of at least thirty images from a single shoot — this week's gallery is a fine source:

1. First pass: cut the technically dead. Fast.
2. Second pass: state the criteria for this set, then judge every survivor against them.
3. Sequence the survivors into a story with a deliberate opening and closing.
4. Reorder once and observe the meaning shift.
5. Write the one-sentence story, and count the darlings you cut.

If you're using a phone: the gallery and the Progress screen are your editing table — the process is identical at phone scale.

## 7. RECAP

First pass ruthless and fast; second pass against stated criteria — not mood. Order is meaning: the opening sets, the middles develop with varied rhythm, the closing lands. Darlings that break the rhythm go to their own folder. The edit is where photographers are made.
