# Advanced Lesson 11 — Developing a Personal Style

Class: Advanced
Prerequisite: Advanced lessons on composition and storytelling, and image selection

## 1. LEARNING OUTCOME

By the end of this lesson, learners can identify recurring visual choices in their own body of work — in light, color, subject, and composition — and name the deliberate choices they will carry forward as the beginnings of a personal style.

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT

**Welcome**

Personal style is the thing every photographer wants and most go about backwards — chasing a look instead of finding their own. The honest sequence: style is FOUND in the work you've already made, then CHOSEN as a deliberate direction. This lesson does both.

**The audit: patterns you didn't plan**

Lay out a large sample of your recent work — thirty images or more, across different shoots. Ignore which ones you like best; look for what repeats. You will find patterns you never planned: you favor side light over flat light; your frames run darker than the average; you shoot tighter than most; a particular color keeps arriving. These patterns exist whether or not you approve of them — and they are the raw material of style.

**The four columns: light, color, subject, composition**

Sort the audit into four columns. Light: soft or hard, front or back, golden or gray? Color: saturated or muted, warm or cool, dominant hues? Subject: what do you photograph when no one assigns it — people, places, small details, vast emptiness? Composition: tight or wide, centered or displaced, layered or minimal? The columns overlap, and the overlaps are the fingerprint.

**Demonstration: auditing a real set**

Thirty of my frames on the wall. The light column: overwhelmingly side and backlight — almost nothing flat-lit. Color: muted, with one repeated accent. Subject: small human figures inside large spaces — over and over. Composition: layered, rarely minimal. Reading the columns together: my found style is "small people in big, moody, layered spaces." Some of that was intention; most of it was instinct repeating. Now it can be chosen.

**From pattern to intention**

Found patterns become style when you keep some deliberately and prune others. The ones you love become commitments: chase that light, keep that palette, remain that subject. The ones you dislike become assignments: practice the light you avoid, the framing you never try — not to change your voice, but to choose it with the full range available. Style grows at the edges, not by doubling down on the center.

**The imitation trap**

Studying photographers you admire is education; imitating them is detention. If you copy a look, you inherit its ceiling. Borrow TECHNIQUE freely — the audit method itself, the editing order, the priority-first habit — but let the patterns come from your own frames. The fastest route to a generic portfolio is a borrowed style; the slowest, surest route to yours is the audit.

**Learner practice**

Audit thirty of your own images across the four columns, then write three deliberate commitments — light, color, subject — you will carry into your next shoot. Shoot for those commitments; audit again afterward.

**Recap**

Style is found in your patterns, then chosen as intention. Audit across light, color, subject, and composition; keep the patterns you love as commitments; practice at the edges you avoid. Technique can be borrowed — voice can't, and shouldn't be.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE

Audit your own work:

1. Assemble thirty or more images across different shoots — the Progress screen's history is a legitimate source.
2. Sort into the four columns: light, color, subject, composition.
3. Name three patterns you found — at least one you didn't plan.
4. Write three commitments for your next shoot, and one edge you'll practice that you usually avoid.
5. Shoot for the commitments; audit the new set against them.

If you're using a phone: the audit works identically — phones produce style too, and the four columns don't care what shot the file.

## 7. RECAP

Style is found, then chosen. Audit thirty of your images across light, color, subject, and composition; name the patterns you never planned. Keep the loved patterns as commitments; practice at the edges you avoid. Borrow technique freely — the voice comes from your own frames.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK

- Pattern claims must be verifiable against the actual image set shown; do not narrate patterns the frames don't support.
- Style development is presented as a long process — one audit begins it, not completes it.
- Do not name or disparage other photographers' styles as imitation examples; the imitation trap is discussed generally.
- The four columns are a teaching structure, not a taxonomy; say so if asked.
- Any prior-work statistics (how many images, shoots) must match the demonstration set.
