# Advanced Lesson 12 — Ethics, Permission, and Responsible Photography

Class: Advanced
Prerequisite: Advanced lessons on storytelling and image selection

## 1. LEARNING OUTCOME

By the end of this lesson, learners can identify the ethical questions in photographing, editing, and sharing images — and articulate a reasoned position on consent, access, representation, and manipulation for their own practice.

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT

**Welcome**

The Advanced class taught power: over light, over focus, over narrative, over what gets cut. Power raises the question this lesson asks — not with fixed answers, because the answers vary by context and culture, but with the habit of ASKING before taking, editing, and sharing.

**Consent and permission: people, places, moments**

People: laws differ by country, but the ethical question precedes the law — does this person want to be photographed, and would they recognize themselves in the result? Street photography's candid tradition is real, and so is the responsibility that shadows it: a photograph taken in public can still be an unkindness. Private property, events, children, and vulnerable situations raise the bar further: when in doubt, ask. Places: some locations — museums, temples, military sites, private land — set their own rules, and permission is part of the craft, not an obstacle to it. Moments: even with consent to photograph, consent to PUBLISH is a separate decision worth its own thought.

**Representation: what the frame claims**

A photograph is a selection, and selection is a claim. The frame that shows the protest's one angry sign claims a mood; the frame showing the calm majority claims another. Both may be true; neither is complete. Ask: does this image represent the moment fairly? Am I photographing this person WITH agency or AT them? Am I presenting someone's dignity or my own cleverness? The worst outcomes rarely come from malice — they come from never asking.

**Editing and publication: the honesty lines**

The Intermediate finishing lesson drew two lines — believable and honest — and at the advanced level they sharpen. Believable: no plastic skin, no impossible physics, no tell-tale halos. Honest: removing a wrapper is finishing; removing a person is a claim that they weren't there. Caption and context are part of the image: a truthful photograph published under a false caption becomes a false photograph. And AI-assisted tools have raised the stakes — if your software invents content, you own the invention. Label heavy manipulation as illustration, not photography.

**Demonstration: four scenarios, weighed**

Four real situations, reasoned aloud. One: a street portrait of a stranger in an unflattering moment — is publication fair? Two: a documentarian edits out a background bystander who didn't consent — finishing or fabrication? Three: a dramatic sky replaced in a landscape sold as art — is it labeled? Four: a viral photo of a child in distress — consent, dignity, and news value collide. None has a one-line answer. Each gets: who is affected, what is claimed, what would I defend publicly?

**Your practice's written positions**

The lesson's deliverable is personal: write your own positions — when you ask permission, what you won't manipulate, how you caption, what you won't publish. Written positions hold under pressure; improvised ones bend.

**Learner practice**

Take three of your own photographs — past or new — and write the ethical review of each: who appears, what the frame claims, what was edited, and whether you'd defend it publicly. Adjust your publication decisions to match.

**Recap**

Advanced skills carry advanced responsibilities. Ask before taking when people are involved; examine what the frame claims; hold the believable and honest lines through editing and captioning. Write your positions down — the photograph may be instant, but the responsibility is deliberate.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE

Choose three of your own photographs — past or new — and write an ethical review of each:

1. Who appears, and did they meaningfully consent (to the photo AND to its publication)?
2. What does the frame claim about the moment — and is the claim fair?
3. What was edited, and does it stay inside the believable/honest lines?
4. Would you defend this image publicly, to the person in it?

Adjust your publication decisions to match your answers. Then write your standing positions: when you ask, what you won't manipulate, how you caption.

If you're using a phone: none of this changes — phones photograph people constantly and publish instantly, which raises the stakes rather than lowering them.

## 7. RECAP

Advanced skills carry advanced responsibilities. Consent precedes the shutter when people are involved — and publication consent is separate. Selection is a claim; hold it fairly. Editing stays believable and honest, captions included. Write your positions — deliberate responsibility is the advanced photographer's real signature.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK

- Photography law varies by country and jurisdiction; the lesson teaches ethical reasoning, NOT legal advice — say so explicitly on camera.
- Scenarios are illustrative and staged/consented; no real third parties are depicted or discussed without consent.
- AI-tool claims (what current software can invent or remove) must match the tools actually demonstrated, not headlines.
- Do not present one cultural standard as universal — consent norms and street-photography traditions vary; acknowledge it.
- The written-positions deliverable is the demonstration: verify it appears in the footage.
