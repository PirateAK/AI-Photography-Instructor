# Beginner Photography Class Lesson 1 - How a Camera Works

## 1. LEARNING OUTCOME
By the end of this lesson, the student can trace the exact path of light through a camera body, identify the physical components on both DSLR and mirrorless designs, and explain the basic roles of the lens, aperture, shutter, and sensor.

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome to MuskegMan Photography and our flagship Beginner Photography Class. I'm so glad you're here. Today, in Lesson 1, we are answering a very simple question: how does a camera actually work? 

Now, if you've ever picked up a camera, looked at all the buttons, dials, and glass, and felt a tiny wave of intimidation—take a deep breath. You are in the right place. Every fancy camera, every smartphone in our pockets, and every vintage film body shares one single, beautiful secret. They all do the exact same job. They collect light and trap it. 

Think of a camera as a light-tight box. If there is no light, you get pure black. If there is too much light, you get pure white. Photography is simply the art of controlling that light to tell your story. 

To understand any camera—whether it's a massive professional DSLR, a sleek modern mirrorless, or the phone in your hand—you just need to follow the light. Let's trace it together. 

Every photograph begins with light bouncing off your subject. That light travels through the air and hits the front of your camera. That brings us to our first stop: the lens. 

The lens is the glass cylinder on the front of the camera. Its job is to gather all those scattered rays of light and bend them so they form a sharp, focused picture. Without the lens, your camera would just record a blurry wash of brightness. Think of the lens like a pair of eyeglasses for the camera, pulling the scene into sharp focus. 

Once the light passes through the glass elements inside the lens, it hits our second stop: the aperture. 

The aperture is a circular opening inside the lens made of overlapping metal blades. It acts just like the iris of your eye. In bright sunlight, your eye's pupil shrinks down to let in less light. In a dark room, your pupil opens wide. The aperture does the exact same thing. It meters how much light is allowed to pass through on its way into the camera body. 

After squeezing through the aperture, the light reaches our third stop: the shutter. 

The shutter is a set of tiny physical curtains sitting right in front of the camera's recording surface. When you press the shutter button on top of your camera, those curtains fly open for a split second, and then snap shut. The shutter times exactly how long the sensor gets to see that light. We might set it to stay open for one-thousandth of a second on a bright Alaska afternoon, or for two full seconds to capture the northern lights at night. 

Finally, the light hits our fourth and final stop: the sensor. 

The sensor is a rectangular silicon chip sitting deep inside the camera body. It is covered in millions of tiny light-sensitive wells called pixels. When the light hits these pixels, they measure how bright and what color that light is, turning photons into digital data. That data is what your camera saves onto your memory card as a photograph. 

Lens, aperture, shutter, sensor. Memorize that order. If you can trace that path, you understand the beating heart of photography. 

Now, how that light travels inside the camera body can vary depending on what type of camera you own. Let's look at our two main camera families: DSLR and mirrorless. 

Let's start with the DSLR, which stands for Digital Single-Lens Reflex. That word "reflex" is your clue. 

Inside a DSLR, right behind the lens, sits a physical mirror angled at forty-five degrees. When you look through the optical viewfinder—that little eyepiece on the back of the camera—you aren't looking at a tiny TV screen. You are looking at a system of glass prisms and mirrors that bounces the light straight from your lens up to your eye. You see the real world, optically, in real time. 

So what happens when you press the shutter button? That mirror has to get out of the way. With a loud, satisfying *clack*, the mirror instantly flips up out of the light path, the shutter curtains open and close to expose the sensor, and then the mirror drops right back down so you can see your next shot. If you listen closely when you shoot with a DSLR, you can actually hear that mechanical mirror flipping up and down. 

Now let's look at the second major design: the mirrorless camera. 

As the name implies, mirrorless cameras skip the mirror entirely. When light enters a mirrorless camera, it passes straight through the lens, right past where the mirror used to be, and hits the sensor continuously. 

So how do you see what you're shooting if there's no mirror bouncing the light to an optical viewfinder? Simple. The sensor is always awake, streaming a live video feed to either the electronic viewfinder held up to your eye or the LCD screen on the back of the camera body. 

Think of it like looking at a tiny computer monitor. Because the sensor is always reading the light, what you see on a mirrorless screen is a real-time preview of your exposure. If your image is going to be too bright or too dark, the mirrorless screen will often show you that before you even take the picture. 

Both designs—DSLR and mirrorless—take incredible photos. Neither one is inherently better than the other. They just solve the problem of viewing the scene in two different ways. 

And what about your smartphone? Does it follow these rules? Absolutely. 

Your smartphone has a tiny lens on the back. Behind that lens, it has a digital aperture—though on most phones, it's fixed at one size—and an electronic shutter that times the exposure. Finally, it hits a tiny digital sensor. Because phones are so thin, they skip all the physical mirrors and prisms of a DSLR, using software and digital screens just like a mirrorless camera. The parts are smaller, but the journey of the light is identical. 

Designs vary between models, but following the light is the map that works on all of them. 

Before we wrap up today, I want to give you your first small win. You don't need to memorize every button on your camera right now. Your small win for today is simply picking up your camera, locating the lens on the front, finding the shutter button on top, and saying out loud: lens, aperture, shutter, sensor. 

In our next lesson, we are going to dive deeper into that second stop on our map: the aperture. You're going to learn how changing the size of that opening completely changes the mood of your images. 

Thank you for joining me for Lesson 1. Keep practicing, be kind to yourself, and I'll see you in the next lesson.

## 7. RECAP
- Photography begins with light, and every camera functions as a light-tight box that collects and traps it.
- The universal path of light is always the same: **Lens** (focuses the light) $\rightarrow$ **Aperture** (meters the volume) $\rightarrow$ **Shutter** (times the exposure) $\rightarrow$ **Sensor** (records the final image).
- **DSLR cameras** use a physical mirror to bounce light up to an optical viewfinder, flipping out of the way when you take a picture.
- **Mirrorless cameras** skip the mirror entirely, streaming a continuous live view directly from the sensor to your screen or electronic viewfinder.
- Different camera bodies may arrange their parts differently, but the journey of the light remains your reliable map on every device.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
1. **Question:** What is the correct order of the four core stops light makes as it travels through a camera?
   - *Answer:* Lens, aperture, shutter, sensor.
2. **Question:** How does a DSLR viewfinder show you the scene before you take a picture?
   - *Answer:* A physical mirror angled at 45 degrees reflects the light coming through the lens up into an optical viewfinder system.
3. **Question:** Do mirrorless cameras use a physical mirror to bounce light to the viewfinder?
   - *Answer:* No. Mirrorless cameras let light hit the sensor directly at all times, generating a live digital preview on an electronic screen or viewfinder.
