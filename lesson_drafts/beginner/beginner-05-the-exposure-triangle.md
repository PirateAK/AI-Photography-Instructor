## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back. Today we put M mode to work, and I promise, by the end of this lesson you'll stop fearing that manual dial. Exposure is simply how bright or dark your photo looks, and three camera settings control it together. Think of them as a team of three friends holding a bucket of water. If one changes how much water they pour, the others have to adjust to keep the bucket balanced.

Let’s meet our first friend: Aperture. Aperture is the hole inside your lens that opens and closes to let light through, measured in f-numbers like f/2.8 or f/8. Here is the trickiest part for every beginner, so listen closely: a smaller f-number means a *wider* opening, which lets in *more* light and brightens your photo. A larger f-number means a *narrower* opening, letting in *less* light and darkening your photo. 

Aperture doesn't just change brightness, though. It also controls your depth of field, which is the zone of sharpness from front to back in your image. Want that creamy, blurry background behind a portrait? Dial in f/2.8. Want your whole landscape sharp from the wildflowers at your feet to the mountains miles away? Step up to f/11. Your small win right now on the simulator: tap your aperture down to f/2.8 and watch the background melt away.

Next up is Shutter Speed. This is the duration of time that your camera's curtain stays open to let light hit the sensor, measured in seconds or fractions of a second like 1/500th or 1/30th. A fast shutter speed, like 1/1000th of a second, freezes a speeding bicycle or a flying gull in mid-air because the sensor only peeks at the scene for a tiny fraction of a second. A slow shutter speed, like 1/4th of a second, leaves the curtain open longer, blurring a rushing waterfall into silky smooth glass. 

Just remember: faster shutter equals darker image and frozen action. Slower shutter equals brighter image and motion blur. Try setting your simulator shutter to 1/1000th right now and see how crisp that moving subject becomes.

That brings us to our third and final corner: ISO. ISO is your sensor’s electronic sensitivity to light, measured in numbers like 100, 400, 1600, or 6400. In the old film days, this was how sensitive your film stock was to the sun. On a bright Alaska afternoon, you set your ISO to 100 because light is plentiful. When the sun dips behind the mountains and twilight settles in, you raise your ISO to 3200 or 6400 to amplify the available light so your photos don't come out pitch black. 

There's a catch, of course. Pushing your ISO higher introduces digital noise, which looks like fine sand or color speckles scattered across your shot. Think of it like turning up the volume control on an old stereo: you get the music louder, but you also get a whole lot of static hiss in the background. Your small win: bump your ISO from 100 to 6400 on the simulator and check out the texture in the shadows.

So how do these three work together? There is no single universally "right" setting. Instead, you choose the creative look you want first. If you want a blurry background for a portrait, you lock in your aperture at f/2.8. If you're shooting sports, you lock in a fast shutter speed of 1/500th. Once you make that creative choice, you balance the other two dials around it until your exposure meter is sitting right in the middle at zero. 

Take a look at the live histogram on your screen right now—that little graph showing shadow, midtone, and highlight brightness. As you change your settings, watch the mountain of data shift left and right. When it spans gracefully across the center without smashing into the edges, you've nailed your exposure. Practice this on the simulator today, and I'll see you in the next lesson.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Make three versions of the scene in front of you using the camera simulator, changing one setting at a time while watching the live histogram make each trade visible:
1. Open your aperture two stops wider (e.g., from f/5.6 to f/2.8): watch brightness rise AND background blur grow.
2. Speed your shutter speed two stops faster (e.g., from 1/125s to 1/500s): notice how brightness falls and motion is frozen.
3. Raise your ISO two stops higher (e.g., from 200 to 800): watch brightness rise and subtle digital grain appear in the shadows.

## 7. RECAP
- Exposure is controlled by three working elements: aperture, shutter speed, and ISO.
- Aperture controls light and depth of field; a smaller f-number means a wider opening and more background blur.
- Shutter speed controls light and motion; a faster fraction of a second freezes action while a slower speed blurs it.
- ISO controls sensor sensitivity; higher numbers brighten dark scenes but introduce digital noise.
- Use the live histogram on your simulator to guide your balance and verify your exposure in real time.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
- **Question:** Does a smaller f-number (like f/2.8) let in more light or less light than a larger f-number (like f/11)?
- **Answer:** A smaller f-number represents a wider opening, which lets in more light.
- **Question:** If you switch your shutter speed from 1/60th of a second to 1/500th of a second, does your image become brighter or darker?
- **Answer:** Darker, because the sensor is exposed to light for a shorter duration.
- **Question:** What is the primary visual trade-off when raising your ISO from 100 to 6400 in low light?
- **Answer:** You gain brightness, but you invite digital noise (grain) into your image.
