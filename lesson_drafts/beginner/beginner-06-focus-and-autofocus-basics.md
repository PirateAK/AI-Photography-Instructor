## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to the MuskegMan Photography school. Today we are tackling focus and autofocus basics. 

Let's start with a hard truth we've all faced. You dial in your shutter speed, you balance your ISO, your exposure meter sits dead center at zero, and you take the shot. You look at the back of the camera, and it looks like a masterpiece. But then you get home, open the file on your computer, and realize the tip of your subject's nose is sharp, but their eyes are a soft, muddy blur. Ouch. 

Here is the golden rule for today: focus placement is the choice of which part of the scene appears sharp, and it is entirely separate from exposure. A perfectly exposed photo can still be sharp in all the wrong places. Exposure gives you light. Focus gives you intention. 

So, what is autofocus? Simply put, autofocus is your camera's motorized lens system working with electronic sensors to find maximum contrast and lock onto your subject. It is a wonderfully capable tool, but it is not a mind reader. Your camera has no idea if you want to emphasize the cute coffee mug on the table or the person sitting behind it. The camera’s job is to make things sharp; your job as the photographer is to guide it onto the intended subject. 

Now, camera models vary wildly. You might have a joystick, a touchscreen, or a directional pad to move your focus point. Don't worry about mastering every single setting on the market. Just learn yours. Open your manual, find out how to move that little focus square, and make it second nature. 

Let's look at how autofocus modes usually break down. You will typically see two main settings. First is Single Autofocus, often called AF-S or One-Shot. When you press your shutter button halfway down, the camera finds focus, locks onto it, and stays put. Use this for things that sit still, like a landscape, a bowl of fruit, or a sleepy puppy. Second is Continuous Autofocus, called AF-C or AI Servo. When you hold the shutter halfway down, the camera keeps tracking moving subjects, adjusting focus as they walk closer or farther away. Use this for running kids, flying birds, or anything with legs. 

Next up are focus area controls. Your camera gives you choices on how many focus points to use. You can use a single point—which gives you pinpoint accuracy—or you can let the camera use zone focusing, where a cluster of points works together to track a moving subject. For beginners, stick to a single, movable focus point. It puts you firmly in the driver's seat. 

Let's hop over to our camera simulator on screen right now to see how this works in practice. 

Here is a great trick to make focus placement obvious while you are learning. Open your aperture wider than your as-shot value first—try setting your lens to f/2.8 or f/1.8 instead of f/8. Why? Because a wide-open aperture creates a very thin depth of field, which is the zone of acceptable sharpness in front of and behind your subject. Thin depth of field makes your focus mistakes, and your focus successes, pop out instantly. 

Watch what happens on the simulator screen. When we drag our little focus dot onto the subject's eye, watch sharpness bloom right there. Eyelashes are crisp, the iris is clear. Now, let's take that exact same focus dot and drag it backward onto the tree trunk in the background. Suddenly, the subject goes completely soft. That is focus placement in action. You are deciding where the viewer's eye travels. 

In the field, your workflow should look like this: guide your autofocus point onto a person's eye or an important object detail, press the shutter halfway down to lock it, recompose if needed, and take the photo. 

But wait—we aren't done yet. Never trust the small screen on the back of your camera. A photo that looks sharp in playback can reveal missed focus the second you enlarge it. 

After you take a test shot, hit your playback zoom button. Zoom right in on the critical detail—the eyelashes, the texture of the fabric, the edge of the leaf. If the detail is sharp, high-five yourself, you nailed it. If focus missed, don't panic. Place that focus point more deliberately and shoot again. 

Let's lock in your small win for today. Before you put your camera away, pick a stationary object in your room—a houseplant, a lamp, a book. Set your aperture to your lens's widest setting, move your focus point manually to a specific corner of the object, take the shot, and zoom in to verify. You've got this!

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Open your camera, set your aperture wide open (like f/2.8 or f/4), and choose a nearby textured subject like a coffee mug or houseplant. Move your single focus point onto a tiny detail—like the rim of the handle or a specific leaf vein. Take the shot, immediately zoom in 100% on playback to check that exact detail, and repeat until you can land the focus point precisely where you want it every single time.

## 7. RECAP
- Focus placement is entirely separate from exposure; you can have a bright, well-lit photo that is sharp in the wrong place.
- Autofocus finds contrast and locks focus, but you must guide the focus point onto your intended subject.
- Use Single Autofocus (AF-S) for still subjects and Continuous Autofocus (AF-C) for moving ones.
- Wide apertures make depth of field thin, helping you clearly see where your focus lands.
- Never trust the small rear screen; always zoom in on playback to verify that critical details are sharp.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
1. **Question:** Is a perfectly exposed photo always guaranteed to be sharp?
   **Answer:** No. Exposure controls light, while focus placement controls which part of the image is sharp. They are completely separate decisions.
2. **Question:** Which autofocus mode should you generally choose for a stationary subject like a landscape or a bowl of fruit?
   **Answer:** Single Autofocus (AF-S or One-Shot), because it locks focus when the shutter button is pressed halfway down and holds it still.
3. **Question:** Why should you zoom in during image playback rather than relying on the initial review screen?
   **Answer:** The small rear screen makes almost everything look deceptively sharp; zooming in lets you inspect critical details like eyelashes or textures to confirm focus accuracy.
