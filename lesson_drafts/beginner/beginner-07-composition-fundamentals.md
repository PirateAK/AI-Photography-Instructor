## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to MuskegMan Photography’s beginner class. Today we are tackling lesson seven: composition fundamentals. 

Now, I want you to take a deep breath. There are no rigid laws in photography, only tools. Composition is simply how you arrange elements inside your frame. And honestly, it starts with what you choose to leave out. Every time you lift your camera, you are making a choice about what matters. The goal here is an intentional choice, not a correct one. Let’s dive in.

Our first tool is the classic rule of thirds. Think of your viewfinder split into a tic-tac-toe board by two vertical and two horizontal lines. Most cameras let you turn this grid display right on in your menu. The idea is simple: instead of dead-centering your subject every single time, try placing important elements off-center, right near those grid lines or where the lines intersect. Our eyes naturally gravitate toward those intersecting points. It creates energy. It creates breathing room. 

Now, does a centered subject never work? Of course it does! Symmetrical scenes, reflections, or powerful portraits look incredible right in the middle. But off-center placement gives your subject room to look into or move into. Try turning on your camera's grid overlay today for just one small win on your next walk.

Tool number two is balance. A subject doesn’t need to be right in the middle of the frame to feel settled. Imagine a seesaw. If you have a bright red kayak way over on the right third of your frame, you can balance it with something smaller on the left—like a cluster of dark rocks or an interesting patch of moss. Balance is about visual weight. It keeps your photo from feeling like it’s tipping over to one side. 

Our third tool is leading lines. These are paths, boardwalks, wooden railings, or even rows of spruce trees that naturally walk the viewer's eye right toward your main subject. When you spot a leading line in nature, crouch down and line it up so it starts near the bottom corner of your frame and points straight to your focal point. It’s like laying down a red carpet for whoever looks at your photo.

Let’s hop over to the camera simulator on screen to see how framing changes everything. Watch what happens when I switch our virtual lens from a wide 24-millimeter focal length to a tighter 85-millimeter portrait lens. The whole story of the image shifts because framing is literally what you choose to include and what you leave out. 

Now, let's look at the AI composition coach inside your app. If you run a photo through the coach with a style selected, it will give you instant notes on your balance and subject placement. Use it as a friendly critic, not a grader. 

Your small win today? Take three shots of one simple subject around your house or yard. Put it dead center once, slide it onto a third using your grid display the second time, and use a doorway or railing as a leading line for the third. You've got this.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
In the field, make three photos of one single subject: 
1. Centered in the frame.
2. Off-center along a rule-of-thirds grid line (turn on your camera's grid display in the menu).
3. Using a leading line or natural frame like a doorway or tree branches. 
Review all three on your screen and ask yourself: where does my eye land first? 
Next, open the app, run one of those photos through the AI coach with a style selected, and read its composition notes. Finally, use the app's lens selector tool to compare a wider versus a tighter framing of the same scene to see how framing alters the story.

## 7. RECAP
- Composition is an intentional choice about what to include and what to leave out, not a rigid rule.
- The rule of thirds places key elements off-center near grid intersections to create visual energy and breathing room.
- Balance uses visual weight so your frame feels settled without needing dead-center placement.
- Leading lines like paths, railings, and rows of trees walk the viewer's eye straight to your subject.
- The app's lens selector and AI composition coach help you evaluate your framing and balance choices instantly.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
1. **Question:** What is the primary purpose of turning on a camera's grid display?
   *Answer:* To help you easily align your subject along the rule of thirds lines and intersections.
2. **Question:** Does a subject always have to be off-center for a photo to look good?
   *Answer:* No, centered composition works wonderfully for symmetrical scenes, reflections, and portraits when used intentionally.
3. **Question:** What is a leading line?
   *Answer:* A natural or man-made element like a path, boardwalk, or railing that walks the viewer's eye toward the main subject.
