## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to MuskegMan Photography's AI Photography Instructor. Today we are diving into lighting basics: direction, softness, and mood. Before you ever touch a camera dial—whether you are shooting at ISO 200, f/4, or 1/250th of a second—light is already doing the heavy lifting in your frame. Light's direction and quality shape shadows, contrast, and mood before anything else happens. Let's start with a quick small win: step outside your door today, find any object, and just notice where the sun hits it. You are already learning to see like a photographer.

Let's talk about direction first. Light direction changes everything about a subject's shape. Imagine you are standing with your back to a window or the sun, pointing your camera straight at your friend's face. That is front light. Front light fills in shadows, which means it flattens features and is very forgiving on skin texture. It is bright, even, and safe. But it can also look a little flat and lifeless because you have removed depth.

Now, take two steps to the left so the light hits your friend from the side. That is side light. Side light sculpts. Because the light comes from an angle, it creates strong shadows on the opposite side of the nose, cheeks, and jaw. Shape and texture suddenly pop out at you. If you are photographing a rugged mountain face in Alaska or an old wooden cabin, side light brings out every single grain and cranny. 

Finally, put the light source directly behind your subject. That is backlight. Backlight rims your subject in a glowing halo of light, or it plunges them into a total silhouette if you expose for the bright sky behind them. Backlight is dramatic, moody, and cinematic. But it comes with a catch, and that brings us to our next small win: noticing how your camera's meter reacts to it.

When you point your camera at a backlit subject, the bright background tricks your camera's light meter—the built-in tool that measures brightness. The meter sees all that bright light and says, "Whoa, this scene is too bright!" So it darkens the whole image, turning your subject into a dark shadow. 

In the app's camera simulator, you can see that reaction directly right now. Look at the histogram guide on your screen. That graph shows you where the tones fall. When you shoot into the backlight, the histogram leans heavy to the right, meaning the frame is bright, while your subject sits dark in the middle. The rescue for this is exposure compensation—those plus and minus EV buttons—or switching to spot metering, which tells your camera to measure light only from your subject's face, not the sky behind them. Tap that minus EV button down to negative one (−1) or plus one (+1) and watch the whole shape slide until your subject looks right.

Next up is softness. Softness follows the source's apparent size. What does that mean? It means a big light source creates soft, gentle shadows with blurry, gradual edges. A small light source creates hard, crisp shadows with sharp, defined edges. 

Think of the midday sun on a clear summer day in Anchorage. The sun looks tiny in that massive sky. Because it is a small, distant source, it acts like a hard light. It casts harsh, raccoon-eye shadows under your subject's eyes and nose. Now, imagine an overcast, cloudy day. Those clouds act like a giant diffuser—a translucent material that scatters light. The clouds turn the entire sky into one giant softbox. Suddenly, those harsh shadows vanish, replaced by creamy, wrapping light that flatters everything it touches. 

Here is another small win: you do not have to wait for a cloudy day to get soft light. If you are indoors, move your subject right next to a large window, or place a sheer white curtain over it. You just made your light source bigger relative to your subject, and the shadows instantly soften.

Remember, moving the subject or your feet changes the light in the frame—and your exposure right along with it. If your portrait looks too contrasty and harsh, you don't necessarily need to dive into complex menu settings. Just take two steps to your right, turn your subject toward open shade, or wait for a cloud to drift over. Photography is simply drawing with light. Once you learn to read where the light is coming from and how soft it is, your camera settings will finally make sense because they are just serving the light.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
In the field practice: Find one subject (a friend, a pet, or even a houseplant). Take three distinct photos: one with front light, one with side light, and one with backlight. For each shot, note where the light is coming from, whether the shadow edges are hard or soft, and how the overall exposure looks. If your backlit shot turns your subject into a dark silhouette, open your camera simulator or app, use the exposure compensation (+/− EV) buttons or spot metering to rescue the subject's details, and watch how the histogram shifts.

## 7. RECAP
- Light direction shapes mood and texture: front light flattens, side light sculpts, and backlight creates dramatic silhouettes or rims.
- The camera's meter can be tricked by bright backlights, making your subject too dark; use +/− EV or spot metering to fix it.
- Softness depends on the apparent size of the light source: big sources or diffused clouds create gentle, gradual shadows, while small sources make crisp edges.
- Moving your feet or your subject instantly changes the lighting angle and exposure.
- Photography is drawing with light—always look at your light source before adjusting your camera dials.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
1. Question: What effect does front lighting typically have on a subject's features?
   Answer: Front lighting fills in shadows, flattening facial features and skin texture while providing an even, bright exposure.
2. Question: Why does a backlit subject often appear as a dark silhouette in your camera?
   Answer: The camera's light meter reads the bright background light and darkens the entire exposure to compensate, leaving the subject underexposed.
3. Question: What makes a light source produce soft shadows instead of hard ones?
   Answer: A larger apparent light source (or a diffuser like clouds or a window curtain) spreads the light rays, creating gentle shadows with gradual, soft edges.
