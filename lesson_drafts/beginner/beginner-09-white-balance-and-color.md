## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to the MuskegMan Photography school app. Today we are talking about color, and specifically, white balance. Have you ever taken a photo inside your house, and when you look at the back of your camera, everyone looks like they have a mild case of the orange-glow sunburn? Or maybe you step into the deep shadows on a bright day, and your skin tones look ghostly blue? That isn't a problem with your camera sensor. It is a problem with color temperature, and today, you are going to master it.

Let's start with a simple definition. White balance is the camera's rendering control. It is simply the tool your camera uses to make neutral objects—like a sheet of printer paper or a concrete sidewalk—actually look neutral, meaning white or gray, under whatever light source you happen to be standing under. 

Light sources have different colors. Scientists call this color temperature, measured in Kelvin. But you don't need to memorize a chart of numbers. Just think of it this way: indoor incandescent light bulbs lean very warm and yellow-orange. Shade outdoors leans cool and blue. Sunlight at noon is relatively neutral. Your eyes adjust to these shifts automatically. If you hold a white paper towel under an orange kitchen bulb, your brain says, "That's white." But your camera sensor doesn't have a brain. It just records the raw wavelengths of light. If left to its own devices, it records a heavy orange tint. We call that an unwanted color cast—a tint that shifts every color in your frame away from reality.

Let's look at the camera simulator on your screen right now. We have a still life set up under warm indoor lighting. Notice our settings: aperture is locked at f/4, shutter speed is 1/60th of a second, and ISO is set to 400. We are going to keep every single exposure setting identical, and only change one thing: the white balance.

Right now, the camera is set to AWB, which stands for Auto White Balance. AWB does a decent job most of the time because the camera's computer guesses what the scene should look like. But look closely at the white ceramic mug in the frame. It still looks a little bit creamy and warm. 

Now, let's tap the menu and switch our white balance preset to "Shade," which is usually represented by a little cloud icon. Watch the screen. Shade is designed to add warmth when you are standing in cold blue light. Because we applied it indoors under already warm lights, the image instantly swings heavily orange. It looks like a cozy campfire, but the whites are completely distorted. 

Let's change it again. This time, we will select "Tungsten" or "Incandescent," represented by a little lightbulb icon. Tungsten is designed to cool down warm indoor bulbs. Boom. Look at that. The orange cast vanishes. The white ceramic mug looks crisp and white again, and the background colors return to neutral. Notice what didn't change: our brightness. The exposure stayed completely steady at f/4, 1/60th, and ISO 400. White balance changes the color of the light, not the amount of light.

Here is the secret seasoned photographers know: preset names and exact results vary from one camera brand to another. One manufacturer's "Daylight" might look a fraction warmer than another's. So always judge the image on your LCD screen, not the little label on the menu. 

Auto white balance is handy, but it interprets the scene its own way. If you photograph a bright yellow sunflower against a green field, AWB might panic and try to cool the entire scene down because it thinks there is too much yellow warmth. Manual presets and custom settings hand that control right back to you.

And here is your big creative takeaway for today: a color cast isn't always a mistake. Sometimes, a deliberate warm or cool shift is a fantastic creative choice. If you are shooting an early morning landscape in the foggy Alaskan woods, cooling down your white balance pushes the blues and greens into a moody, atmospheric masterpiece. If you are shooting a golden-hour portrait, dialing in a slightly warmer white balance emphasizes that rich, sun-kissed glow. You are the artist, not the computer.

Before we wrap up, here is your small win for today: open your camera simulator right now, pick any scene, and cycle through AWB, Shade, and Tungsten just once. Notice how the colors shift without the picture getting brighter or darker. You've just unlocked color control. See you in the next lesson!

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Open the camera simulator. Keep all your exposure settings locked in place (for example: aperture f/4, shutter speed 1/60s, ISO 400). Cycle through the White balance settings: start on Auto White Balance (AWB), switch to Shade (which adds warmth), and then switch to Tungsten (which cools down the image to correct warm bulbs). Watch the colors swing across your screen while the brightness stays completely locked. Ask yourself: which version looks natural, and which version creates a better deliberate mood for the scene? 

*Field Version:* Take your real camera or phone to a room with a window. Place a piece of white paper near the glass, take a shot on AWB and a preset. Then move that same piece of paper under indoor overhead lights and take another shot on AWB and a preset. Note your light source, your setting, and how the colors changed.

## 7. RECAP
- Light sources have different natural colors; indoor bulbs lean warm, while shade leans cool.
- White balance is your camera's rendering control that makes neutral objects look truly neutral.
- Changing white balance shifts color temperature (measured in Kelvin), not your exposure brightness.
- Presets and manual settings give you creative control when Auto White Balance guesses incorrectly.
- An intentional warm or cool color cast is a powerful tool for building mood in your photography.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
1. **Question:** Does changing the camera's white balance setting alter the brightness (exposure) of the image?
   **Answer:** No. White balance changes the color tint or temperature of the light in the frame, not the amount of light hitting the sensor.
2. **Question:** What is an unwanted color cast?
   **Answer:** An overall tint (such as too orange indoors or too blue in the shade) that shifts every color in the frame away from what your eyes perceive as natural.
3. **Question:** Why might Auto White Balance (AWB) struggle with a scene dominated by a single bright color, like a field of yellow sunflowers?
   **Answer:** The camera's computer may incorrectly assume the dominant color is an unnatural cast and try to neutralize it, washing out the true colors of the scene.
