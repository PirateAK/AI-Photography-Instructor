# Commercial Photography Lesson 1 - Product on white: the commercial baseline

## 1. LEARNING OUTCOME
By the end of this lesson, the student can set up a professional two-light product station with a pure white background, configure their camera to Manual mode at base ISO 100 and f/8 for total front-to-back sharpness, and execute a consistent commercial shoot that meets modern e-commerce standards.

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to MuskegMan Photography. Today we are tackling the absolute bedrock of commercial work: the product-on-white shot. If you want to make a living with your camera, this is where you start. There is nothing artsy or mysterious about this lesson. We are after ruthless, repeatable consistency. By the end of our time together, you will know how to light, shoot, and deliver clean commercial product images that make clients happy and keep your invoices paid.

Let's start with a hard truth. When an e-commerce client hands you fifty pairs of running shoes, they do not want fifty different creative interpretations. They want identical lighting, razor-sharp focus from the front laces to the back heel, and a pure white background that drops right into their website without extra clipping work. Consistency *is* the product. If your exposures drift from shot to shot, your client has to spend hours in post-production fixing your mistakes. And trust me, they won't hire you twice if you cost them time.

So, how do we build the commercial baseline? It starts with your physical space. You need a stable surface, a seamless white sweep paper or acrylic table, and a controlled lighting environment. Turn off your overhead room lights. Ambient room light is the enemy of color consistency. We want 100 percent control over every photon hitting our subject.

Let's talk lighting. We are using a basic two-light setup. Light number one is your key light. That is your main illumination source. Place it at a forty-five-degree angle to the side of your product and slightly elevated, pointing down. Put a large softbox or diffusion panel on it to wrap the light smoothly around your product. Soft light is flattering; hard light creates harsh, distracting reflections unless you are shooting polished steel or glass. 

Light number two is your background light. If you want a pure white background, say an RGB value of 255, 255, 255, you cannot rely on light bouncing off the product. You need to light the background independently. Place a second strobe or speedlight behind your seamless sweep, pointing directly at the backdrop. Set that background light about one full stop brighter than your key light. This blows out the background to pure white while leaving your product properly exposed in the foreground.

Now, let's move to the camera. Turn your mode dial straight to Manual, or 'M'. No auto-exposure, no auto-white balance. We want zero automation. Put your camera on a sturdy tripod. Once your composition is locked, do not move that tripod. 

Let's dial in our settings. Set your ISO to your camera's base setting, which is almost certainly ISO 100. Base ISO gives you the cleanest dynamic range, the deepest color depth, and zero digital noise. Next, set your aperture to f/8. Why f/8? Because wide-open apertures like f/1.8 will leave the front of your product sharp and the back blurry. In commercial work, the entire product must be crisp. f/8 gives you deep depth of field—that's the zone of acceptable sharpness from front to back—while avoiding diffraction softening that can happen at f/22. 

What about shutter speed? You are shooting on a tripod with off-camera flashes or strobes, so ambient light is effectively zero. Set your shutter speed to your camera's maximum sync speed, usually 1/160th or 1/200th of a second. This ensures your flash syncs perfectly without any dark shutter-curtain bands across your frame. 

Let's talk focus. Switch your lens to manual focus, or use single-point autofocus to lock onto the front-most part of your product that faces the camera. If your product has text or a brand logo, focus right there. Once focus is locked, flip your lens switch to manual focus so the camera doesn't hunt for focus when you trigger the shutter. 

Let's walk through a live camera simulator demo right now. Notice what happens when we leave the camera on aperture priority. The meter reads the bright white background, panics, and underexposes our product, turning our crisp white sneakers into a muddy gray mess. But the moment we switch to manual mode—ISO 100, f/8, 1/160th second—and trigger our strobes, the background pops to pure white and the product details snap into sharp relief. That is the power of manual control.

Before you take your first frame, lock your white balance. Do not leave it on Auto White Balance, or AWB. If your flash power shifts slightly or ambient light leaks in, AWB will drift your colors across a series of product shots. Set your white balance manually to match your flash strobes, usually around 5600 Kelvin, or shoot a gray card right at the start of your session so you can batch-sync your color in post-production.

Workflow matters just as much as gear. Tether your camera directly to your laptop using a heavy-duty USB-C cable running into Capture One or Lightroom. When you shoot tethered, your client—or your own critical eye—can see every detail on a big screen instantly. You will spot stray dust specks, minor product alignment issues, or a crooked label before you pack up your gear. Dust is a killer in product photography. Keep a blower brush and a microfiber cloth right next to your station, and clean your product *before* you put it on set. Cleaning a speck of dust in ten seconds with a blower saves you ten minutes of cloning it out in Photoshop later.

Let's talk about the business side of product photography, because taking the photo is only half the job. How do you price a gig like this? Never charge hourly for commercial product work. Hourly rates penalize you for being fast and efficient. Instead, charge per SKU—that means per distinct product item—or charge a half-day or full-day production rate. 

A standard commercial baseline for a straightforward product-on-white shoot ranges from twenty-five to seventy-five dollars per finished SKU, depending on your market and the complexity of the item. If you are shooting fifty pairs of shoes, that is a tidy package fee. Always include a licensing clause in your contract. Your client is buying the right to use those images for their e-commerce store and marketing channels for a specific duration—say, one year or in perpetuity—but you retain the copyright. If they want to license those photos to a national magazine or a billboard campaign later, that triggers a secondary licensing fee. Make sure your contract protects you.

Let's review our main takeaways for today. One: Consistency is your primary product. Lock your camera to Manual, ISO 100, f/8, and a fixed shutter sync speed. Two: Separate your lighting. Use a key light for the product and an independent background light set one stop brighter to achieve pure white effortlessly. Three: Shoot tethered so you can catch dust and focus errors immediately on a large screen. Four: Price per SKU, not hourly, and protect your work with clear licensing terms.

You've got this. Practice the baseline setup we covered today, keep your exposures locked down, and I'll see you in the next lesson where we tackle reflective products like glass and metal.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Set up a small table with a white poster board curved up against a wall as a makeshift sweep. Place any household object—like a mug or a water bottle—on the sweep. Set your camera to Manual mode: ISO 100, f/8, and your flash sync shutter speed (or equivalent ambient setting if using desk lamps). Take three test shots, adjusting your lighting until the object is sharp from front to back and the background is bright and clean without harsh shadows. Review your images on a computer screen for absolute focus sharpness.

## 7. RECAP
- Consistency is the product: Manual mode, base ISO 100, and f/8 ensure identical exposures and deep depth of field across every shot.
- Separate your lighting: Use a dedicated background light set one stop brighter than your key light to easily achieve pure white backgrounds.
- Tether your workflow: Connecting your camera to a laptop lets you spot dust and focus errors instantly before packing up your gear.
- Price smart: Charge commercial clients per SKU or by day rate, never hourly, and always attach clear usage licensing terms to your contracts.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
- **Question 1:** Why do we shoot product-on-white work at f/8 instead of f/1.8?
  *Answer:* f/8 provides a deep depth of field, ensuring the entire product is sharp from front to back, whereas f/1.8 creates a shallow blur that leaves parts of the product out of focus.
- **Question 2:** How should you position your background light relative to your key light when aiming for a pure white background?
  *Answer:* The background light should be pointed directly at the backdrop and set approximately one full stop brighter than your key light to cleanly blow out the background.
- **Question 3:** What is the primary reason to avoid charging hourly for commercial product photography?
  *Answer:* Hourly pricing penalizes you for being fast and efficient; commercial work is best priced per SKU or by day/project rates.
