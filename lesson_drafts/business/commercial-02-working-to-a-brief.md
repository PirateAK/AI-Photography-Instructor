# Commercial Photography Lesson 2 - Working to a brief

## 1. LEARNING OUTCOME
By the end of this lesson, the student can translate a client's vague stylistic adjectives into precise camera settings, lighting choices, and framing decisions. They can execute a multi-look commercial shoot for a single product and deliver consistent, ready-to-edit assets that match a professional creative brief.

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to MuskegMan Photography's commercial series. Today we are talking about a moment every working pro knows intimately. Your client emails you a mood board and three adjectives: clean, premium, and bold. Then they hang up, and you are left staring at a stainless steel water bottle on a table, wondering what aperture turns an adjective into an invoice. 

Clients do not speak in f-stops. They speak in feelings. Our job is to bridge that gap. We translate their emotional words into mechanical actions: shutter speeds, light angles, and lens choices. By the end of this lesson, you will never look at a vague creative brief with dread again. You will see an instruction manual.

Let us start with look number one: "Clean." When a client asks for clean, they mean clinical, honest, and distraction-free. They want zero ambiguity. Think Apple product pages or high-end medical device brochures. 

In our camera simulator, let us dial in the settings for "Clean." We are locking ISO at 100 for maximum clarity and zero digital grain. Digital grain is that fine-art texture you might love for street photography, but corporate clients usually call it "noise" and ask you to fix it. We want a deep depth of field—that is the zone of sharp focus from front to back—so we are setting our aperture to f/11. That ensures every part of our product is tack sharp. Shutter speed sits at 1/160th of a second, perfectly synced with our studio strobes, which are fired at half power to keep recycling times fast.

For lighting, clean means flat and even. We use a large softbox directly above and slightly in front of the product, dialed to a 2:1 lighting ratio. That means our shadow side is only slightly darker than our highlight side. No dramatic shadows. No moodiness. Just pure, legible form. We frame the bottle dead center, straight-on, using a 50mm focal length to eliminate perspective distortion. The bottle looks honest, symmetrical, and ready for a white-background cutout.

Now, let us shift gears to look number two: "Premium." This is where clients want luxury, warmth, and exclusivity. Think Scotch whiskey bottles or boutique skincare oils. Premium is not bright; premium is mysterious. Premium hides details in the shadows because mystery equals value.

On the simulator, we keep ISO at 100 to maintain that smooth, expensive-looking gradient on glass or metal labels. But we change our aperture to f/4. That shallow depth of field creates a creamy background blur—technically called bokeh—that instantly isolates our product from the environment. We drop our shutter speed to 1/125th to let in a touch of ambient room glow, balancing our main light with the environment.

Lighting for premium requires direction and restraint. We take that same softbox and move it entirely to the side, at a 90-degree angle to our subject. Suddenly, half the bottle falls into rich, controlled shadow. We add a silver reflector on the dark side to bounce just a whisper of light back in, preserving the edge of the bottle so it does not completely disappear into a black void. The color temperature shifts slightly warmer, around 5200 Kelvin, adding a tactile, inviting warmth. We drop our camera angle down, looking slightly up at the product, making it feel monumental and heroic on the page.

That brings us to look number three: "Bold." When a client asks for bold, they want energy, youth, and disruption. Think energy drinks, extreme sports gear, or street fashion. Bold rejects symmetry. Bold breaks the rules.

In the simulator, we keep ISO at 100, but we bring our aperture back to f/8 so our product is sharp, yet our background can be textured and raw—maybe a concrete block wall or distressed wood. We push our shutter speed to 1/200th to kill any stray ambient light, ensuring our flash is the absolute master of the scene.

For lighting, bold means high contrast and hard edges. We remove the softbox entirely and use a bare-bulb strobe with a standard reflector dish. This creates specular highlights—those bright, punchy reflections of the light source itself—that mimic harsh sunlight or club lighting. We angle our light low, raking it across the surface of the product to exaggerate every texture, scratch, and embossed logo. 

Compositionally, we throw out the rule of thirds. We tilt the camera at a dynamic Dutch angle—off-level by about fifteen degrees—and place our product in the far corner of the frame. We leave massive amounts of negative space in the upper left quadrant. Why? Because we are shooting for the edit.

Let us talk about that phrase: shooting for the edit. Commercial photography is rarely delivered as a final, uncropped JPEG straight from the card. Graphic designers need room to drop in promotional copy, sale banners, or website headers. If you frame your product so tightly that it touches the edges of your viewfinder, you have handcuffed the designer. 

Whenever you shoot a commercial brief, you must visualize the final layout. Leave twenty percent extra headroom above your product. Shoot horizontal when you know the client needs a banner, but always rotate the camera and shoot a vertical safety frame for their mobile landing page. Check your histogram—that little graph showing pixel brightness from pure black on the left to pure white on the right—and make sure your brightest highlights are not clipping against the right wall. Blown-out highlights on a shiny product label cannot be recovered in Photoshop.

By methodically breaking down these three looks—clean, premium, and bold—you realize that commercial photography isn't about guessing what the client likes. It is about systematically applying the triangle of exposure, light modification, and framing to match their vision. When you hand over files that respect their brief and give their designers room to work, you stop being just a person with a camera. You become an indispensable partner.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Shoot the same physical product three distinct ways—clean, premium, and bold—by actively changing your lighting angle, modifier, and camera settings, rather than relying on post-processing filters. Use the camera simulator to test your exposures first: lock ISO at 100, use f/11 for clean (even softbox overhead), f/4 with side lighting for premium (shallow depth of field), and f/8 with hard bare-bulb lighting for bold. Deliver three distinct horizontal frames with 20% negative space headroom for designer text placement.

## 7. RECAP
- Translate client adjectives into technical decisions: clean means flat light and deep focus; premium means shadow falloff and shallow depth of field; bold means high contrast and dynamic angles.
- Keep ISO at 100 for commercial work to eliminate digital noise and maximize clarity.
- Always shoot for the edit by leaving extra headroom and negative space for graphic design elements and copy.
- Check your histogram before wrapping a shot to ensure highlight details on reflective surfaces are not permanently clipped.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
- **Question:** What camera aperture and lighting modifier combination best achieves a "Clean" commercial look?
  - **Answer:** An aperture of f/11 for deep depth of field, paired with a large overhead softbox for flat, even, shadow-free lighting.
- **Question:** Why do we maintain ISO 100 for commercial product photography?
  - **Answer:** To eliminate digital noise (grain) and ensure maximum file clarity and smooth tonal gradients.
- **Question:** What does "shooting for the edit" mean in commercial practice?
  - **Answer:** Framing your shot wider with extra headroom and negative space so graphic designers can easily add promotional text and crop for different aspect ratios.
