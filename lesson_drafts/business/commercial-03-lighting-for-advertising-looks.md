## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to MuskegMan Photography’s commercial studio. Today we are tackling lighting for advertising looks. If you have ever wondered why a bottle of soda or a matte-black watch looks so intensely three-dimensional in a magazine, the secret isn't an expensive camera body. It is control. 

Specifically, it is light control. Soft boxes, smooth gradients, sharp rim lights, and intentional reflections are the building blocks of premium commercial images. And the best part? We are scaling this entire setup down to a simple desk in your studio or home office. No massive warehouse required.

Let's start with a quick mindset shift. In portrait or street photography, you might love shooting wide open at f/1.4 or f/1.8 to blur out your backgrounds. Commercial photography plays by a different rulebook. Today, we are locking our camera into manual mode at f/11. 

Aperture, by the way, is the adjustable opening inside your lens that lets light pass through to the sensor. Why f/11? Because at f/11, your entire product—from the front label to the back corner—stays tack-sharp. 

Let's set our concrete numbers right now. Mount your camera on a sturdy tripod. Set your shutter speed to 1/160th of a second. This easily syncs with standard studio strobes or continuous LED panels while cutting out any ambient room light. Set your ISO to 100 for maximum dynamic range and zero digital noise. And finally, turn your aperture dial to f/11. Every single shot we take today uses these exact manual settings. Manual everything stops being optional right here, because consistency is what clients pay for.

Now, let's talk about light modifier number one: the soft box. A soft box is a fabric enclosure around a light source that diffuses the raw bulb into a smooth, flattering spread. 

Imagine you are shooting a matte-black ceramic coffee mug. If you use a bare LED bulb, you get a harsh, blinding glare and pitch-black shadows that hide the texture of the ceramic. 

Instead, place a 24-by-24-inch soft box at a 45-degree angle to the side of your mug. This is your key light—the main light source illuminating your subject. Because the front diffusion panel is large relative to the mug, the light wraps around the curves. You instantly get soft shadow transitions instead of harsh black lines. You can see right here on the camera simulator how the surface texture of the ceramic suddenly pops with rich detail.

Next up, let's add dimension with a rim light. A rim light is a secondary light placed behind and slightly to the side of your subject, pointing back toward the camera lens. 

Its job is to carve out a bright, glowing outline around the edge of your product so it doesn't blend into a dark background. 

Let's bring in a small LED strip light behind our ceramic mug, flagged with black foam core so it doesn't blind the camera lens. Set the power of this rim light to half the output of your key light. Look at the live monitor. See that thin, crisp white line running down the right shoulder of the mug? That is the rim light doing its job. It separates the product from the void and immediately elevates the shot from a snapshot to an ad.

Now comes the trickiest part of commercial work: reflections. Whether you are shooting glass, polished metal, or glazed ceramic, reflective surfaces act like a mirror reflecting your entire room. If you aren't careful, you will see your tripod, your ceiling fan, and your own face staring back from the product. 

How do we fix this? We use negative fill and diffusion flags. Negative fill is simply a large piece of black foam core placed just outside the frame to absorb stray light and deepen shadows. 

If your glossy product has an ugly white reflection on its left side, move a black foam core board closer to that side just out of view. The black card absorbs the light, turning that ugly hot spot into a rich, deep, professional gradient. A gradient is a smooth, gradual transition from light to dark across a surface. Gradients create shape, and shape creates volume. 

Let's put all of this together on our desk setup. We have our camera locked on our tripod at ISO 100, 1/160th second, and f/11. Our key light is firing through a 24-by-24 soft box at 45 degrees. Our rim light is cutting a clean edge from behind. And our black foam core flags are shaping the reflections on our glossy product. 

Take a test capture. Look at the LCD. Every single edge is defined. Every highlight is intentional. There are no accidental glares or muddy shadows. This is the exact workflow you will use whether you are shooting a luxury watch for a catalog or a craft beer can for a local brewery. 

In our next and final lesson of this course, we are going to talk about the business side of commercial photography: licensing, usage rights, and pricing your work so you actually turn a profit. But for now, let's get your desk studio set up and put these lighting principles into practice. See you in the next module.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Set up a small desk studio with a single desk lamp or speedlight, a sheet of diffusion material (or a white pillowcase), and a glossy household object like a mug or water bottle. Lock your camera to manual mode: ISO 100, shutter speed 1/160s, and aperture f/11 on a tripod. Position your diffused key light at 45 degrees to shape the subject, and use a piece of black cardboard just outside the frame to control unwanted reflections until every edge is defined and every highlight is intentional.

## 7. RECAP
- **f/11 Sharpness**: Lock your aperture to f/11 to keep your entire commercial product in sharp focus from front to back.
- **Key Light Diffusion**: Use a soft box at a 45-degree angle to create smooth, flattering shadow transitions on your subject.
- **Rim Lighting**: Add a rear-offset rim light to carve a clean, glowing edge that separates your product from the background.
- **Reflection Control**: Use black foam core cards as negative fill to eliminate distracting room reflections and create professional gradients.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
- **Question**: Why do we use f/11 instead of a wide aperture like f/1.8 for commercial product photography?
- **Answer**: We use f/11 to ensure the entire product remains tack-sharp from front to back, whereas f/1.8 would create a shallow depth of field that blurs parts of the product.
- **Question**: What is the primary purpose of a rim light in a product setup?
- **Answer**: The rim light creates a bright, glowing outline around the edge of the subject, separating it cleanly from a dark background.
- **Question**: How do you eliminate unwanted glossy reflections on a product surface?
- **Answer**: You use negative fill by placing black foam core cards just outside the frame to absorb stray light and shape smooth gradients.
