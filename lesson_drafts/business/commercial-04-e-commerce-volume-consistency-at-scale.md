## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to our final commercial photography session here at MuskegMan Photography. Today, we are tackling the beating heart of online retail: e-commerce volume and consistency at scale. 

Online stores don’t want fifty products that look like fifty different artistic visions. They need fifty products that look like they live in the exact same physical universe. If a customer scrolls through a catalog and the lighting jumps from bright and airy to dark and moody, or the product shifts from the center left to the top right, trust drops instantly. Consistency is your actual deliverable. Not a single hero shot, but an entire catalog that looks like it rolled off an assembly line. 

Let's start by locking down your hardware. In e-commerce volume work, your camera is essentially a scanner. You want it bolted to a heavy studio tripod that cannot drift. Turn off autofocus entirely. Switch your lens to manual focus, tape down the focus ring with gaffer tape so it can never accidentally bump out of place, and tether your camera directly to your laptop via USB. Tethering means you see every frame instantly on a big screen, letting you spot stray dust motes or alignment errors before the client ever does.

Next, lock down your exposure. Manual mode is non-negotiable here. Set your ISO to a clean 100 for maximum detail and zero digital noise. Set your shutter speed to 1/160th of a second to completely kill any ambient room light and freeze your motion. Finally, set your aperture to f/8. F-stop is the size of the lens opening; f/8 gives you enough depth of field—the zone of sharp focus from front to back—to keep every part of a mid-sized product tack sharp, while keeping your lens in its absolute optical sweet spot. No auto-exposure, no auto-white balance. Lock your white balance to a custom Kelvin setting, like 5500K if you are using studio strobes, so the color temperature stays dead-even across every single frame.

Now, let's talk about the physical set. You need an assembly line jig. A jig is simply a physical guide taped or clamped to your shooting surface that tells you exactly where the product goes every single time. Take a piece of white seamless paper or acrylic sweep. Use a ruler and gaffer tape to mark the exact corners where your product base sits. If product A is a tall red box and product B is a short blue jar, their front edges hit that exact taped crosshair. This guarantees that when you swap items, the physical framing, scale, and perspective in the viewfinder do not change by even a millimeter. 

For lighting, use two large softboxes—modifiers that diffuse raw flash into soft, wrapping light—placed at forty-five degrees on either side of your product. Set your studio strobe power levels to fixed manual outputs, say, half power, and never touch them. Because your distance to the subject is locked by your tripod, and your flash power is locked by your pack, your light intensity is mathematically identical for product one and product five-hundred.

When you bring those files into editing software like Capture One or Adobe Lightroom, the workflow is lightning fast. You do not edit fifty photos one by one. You carefully edit the first hero image: set your white balance using a neutral grey card in the first frame, dial in your contrast, and crop to your exact catalog dimensions, say a clean 2000 by 2000 pixel square. Then, you select all remaining forty-nine images and hit "Sync" or "Auto-Import Batch." Every adjustment copies over instantly. 

Finally, let's talk about the business side of high-volume e-commerce. Never charge an hourly rate for volume work. If you work fast and efficiently, an hourly rate punishes you for being good. Instead, charge per SKU—Stock Keeping Unit—or tier your pricing based on volume blocks. For example, charge fifty dollars per SKU for sets of one to twenty items, dropping to thirty dollars per SKU for batches over one hundred items. 

Your licensing agreement should specify non-exclusive commercial web usage for the client's online storefront and social media for a period of one to three years. If they want to put your images on a massive billboard down in Anchorage, that requires a separate licensing fee add-on. Be crystal clear in your contract about what constitutes a "standard" product prep versus complex styling. If the client hands you wrinkled shirts that require steaming, or greasy metal parts that require cleaning, bill an additional prep fee of seventy-five dollars an hour. Protect your time, deliver identical visual results, and watch your commercial clients re-book you month after month.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
**Volume Drill:** Set up a stationary tripod and tethered camera in manual mode (ISO 100, 1/160s, f/8). Tape a placement jig onto your shooting table. Gather five different products of varying sizes and colors (e.g., a mug, a book, a small box, a jar, and a toy). Shoot all five products using the exact same framing, lighting, and manual exposure. Import the files into your editing software, color-correct the first frame, and batch-sync the settings across all five. Compare the final exported set side-by-side: every product must sit at the exact same scale, alignment, and color truth.

## 7. RECAP
- Lock your hardware completely: bolt down your tripod, tape your focus ring, and tether your camera to catch errors instantly.
- Use strict manual settings—ISO 100, 1/160 sec, f/8, and fixed Kelvin white balance—to guarantee absolute exposure consistency across hundreds of frames.
- Build a physical assembly-line jig with gaffer tape to keep product placement, scale, and framing identical from the first shot to the last.
- Batch-edit your catalog in post-production by perfecting one hero image and syncing settings across the entire folder.
- Price volume work per SKU rather than hourly, and protect your business with clear commercial licensing terms and prep-fee clauses.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
1. **Question:** Why is a fixed aperture of f/8 generally recommended for e-commerce volume product photography?
   **Answer:** F/8 provides enough depth of field to keep mid-sized products sharp from front to back while keeping the lens in its optical sweet spot for maximum sharpness and minimal distortion.
2. **Question:** Should you charge e-commerce clients an hourly rate for high-volume catalog shoots?
   **Answer:** No. Hourly rates penalize you for efficiency. You should price per SKU or in volume tiers, which rewards your speed and consistency.
3. **Question:** What is the primary purpose of using a physical jig (taped guides) on your shooting surface?
   **Answer:** To ensure exact product placement repeatability so that scale, perspective, and framing remain identical across every single image in the batch.
