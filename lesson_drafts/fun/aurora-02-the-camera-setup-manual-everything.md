# Northern Lights Lesson 2 - The camera setup: manual everything

## 1. LEARNING OUTCOME
By the end of this lesson, the student can configure a camera or phone with all-manual settings for aurora photography—including manual focus to infinity, wide aperture, ISO 1600, a 3500K white balance, and disabled stabilization—to capture sharp, properly exposed night skies.

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to MuskegMan Photography’s Northern Lights course. I’m your instructor, and today we are getting hands-on with the ultimate secret to aurora success: manual everything. 

In our first lesson, we talked about chasing the lights and understanding space weather. But once you’re standing out in the cold Alaskan wind with the green ribbons dancing overhead, your camera’s brain—its auto-focus and auto-exposure—is going to panic. It relies on contrast and daylight to think, and in the pitch-black Arctic night, it will just hunt back and forth, refusing to take a picture. That is completely normal, and it is entirely fixable. Today, we take the training wheels off. We are going to switch your camera into full manual mode and set up every single dial so you are in complete command. 

Let's start with the hardest part for most beginners: focus. Autofocus is completely useless on an aurora because there isn’t enough light for the camera’s phase-detection pixels to lock onto. So, we switch your lens focus ring from AF to MF, meaning manual focus. How do you find infinity—the exact focal point where distant stars are sharp—in the dark? You don't just twist the ring all the way to the infinity symbol, because modern autofocus lenses often spin past infinity. Instead, find the brightest star or a distant town light in your frame. Turn on your camera’s Live View screen and use the digital zoom button—usually a little magnifying glass icon—to punch in ten times closer on that bright star point. Now, slowly twist your focus ring until that star shrinks into the tiniest, sharpest pinprick of light you can possibly make. Once it's sharp, tape down your focus ring with a tiny piece of gaffer tape so it doesn't bump out of place when you pack up or handle the camera in the freezing cold. You are now permanently locked to infinity.

Next up is your exposure triangle, and we have a very specific working recipe for the northern lights. Aurora photography is a delicate balance between gathering enough light from a fast-moving ribbon and keeping your shutter speed short enough so the stars don't turn into ugly little streaks. 

Let's start with aperture—the size of the opening inside your lens that lets light in. You want your aperture as wide as possible. Set your lens to its lowest f-number, whether that’s f/1.4, f/1.8, f/2.8, or even f/4 if that's all your zoom lens allows. A wider opening lets in maximum starlight and aurora glow in the shortest amount of time. 

Now, let's talk about ISO—your camera sensor's sensitivity to light. For northern lights, your working sweet spot is between ISO 800 and ISO 3200. Let's make ISO 1600 your default starting point. It gives you plenty of brightness without flooding your image with too much digital noise, which is that ugly speckled grain. 

Finally, shutter speed—how long your camera sensor stays exposed to the dark sky. This is where you have to judge how fast the aurora is moving. If the lights are just a slow, lazy green arc across the sky, a shutter speed of 4 to 6 seconds works wonderfully. But if the aurora is exploding into fast, violent curtains that dance and ripple across the stars, you need to speed things up. Drop your shutter to 2.5 or 3 seconds to freeze those fast folds before they blur into a smudge of green paint. 

Now, what about color? Your camera's Auto White Balance tries to neutralize colors to look like daylight, which means it will often make your gorgeous green aurora look sickly white or weirdly blue. We want to take control of that, too. Switch your white balance from auto to a custom Kelvin setting and dial it right around 3500K. This Kelvin temperature preserves the rich, natural emerald greens of the aurora and keeps your night sky looking deep and atmospheric rather than artificially warm.

Before we hit the shutter button, there are two hidden traps you need to turn off. First, your camera's built-in flash. Leave it off. At aurora distances—which are miles up in the thermosphere—a tiny camera flash does absolutely nothing except light up the snowflakes two inches in front of your lens. Second, turn off Image Stabilization—sometimes called VR or IS—on your lens or camera body. Since your camera is locked down tight on a sturdy tripod, Image Stabilization tries to compensate for motion that isn't there, hunting for a handshake and actually softening your sharp star points. Turn it off and let your tripod do the heavy lifting. And always, always shoot in RAW format, capturing all the unprocessed data your sensor can grab for your final edits in Lightroom.

Now, I know many of you might be shooting on a smartphone tonight. Can you catch the northern lights on a phone? Absolutely, but the rules change just a little bit. On your phone, you don't have physical aperture rings, but you do have computational power. Open your native camera app, switch over to Night Mode, and let the exposure timer ramp up. If your phone has a pro mode or a third-party app like Lightroom Mobile, manually set your ISO between 400 and 1600, and use a mini tripod or prop your phone against a sturdy, unmoving surface. When you tap the shutter, hold your breath and stay completely still. The phone's internal software will take a multi-second computational stack, blending multiple exposures together to pull clean color out of the dark sky without turning your stars into smears.

Remember, auroras are hunted, not scheduled. You might stand out in the snow for an hour waiting for the sky to light up, but when it does, having your camera pre-dialed to these exact manual settings means you won't fumble in the dark. You'll just click, capture, and smile. In our next lesson, we’ll look at framing and composition in the dark, learning how to anchor those dancing lights with real foreground elements. I'll see you out there.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Open your interactive camera simulator and complete the full manual exposure walk for night skies. First, simulate focusing to infinity on a distant star point using digital magnification. Next, set your aperture to its widest possible setting (like f/2.8), lock your ISO at 1600, and experiment with your shutter speed by stepping it down from 15 seconds to 2 seconds. Watch how the stars begin to smear into tiny lines as the seconds stretch past 5 seconds, and find your crisp shutter ceiling. Finally, dial your white balance to the 3500K Kelvin setting and observe how the color temperature shifts the scene. (Phone shooters: switch your phone simulator to Night Mode, brace it against a stable surface, set your ISO between 400 and 1600, and test the multi-second capture stack).

## 7. RECAP
- **Focus to Infinity:** Turn off autofocus, use Live View digital zoom on a bright star, and manually crisp up your focus ring (then tape it down!).
- **The Exposure Trio:** Wide open aperture (f/1.4 to f/4), ISO 1600 as your anchor, and a 2.5 to 6-second shutter speed depending on aurora movement.
- **Color & Quirks:** Set white balance to ~3500K to keep greens honest, turn off flash (it does nothing to the sky!), and turn off Image Stabilization on your tripod.
- **Smartphone Adaptations:** Use Night Mode, manual ISO 400-1600 if available, and a steady tripod or braced surface to let computational stacking work its magic.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
1. **Question:** Why should you turn off Image Stabilization (IS or VR) when shooting northern lights?
   **Answer:** Because your camera is locked securely on a heavy tripod, Image Stabilization will mistakenly search for camera shake, causing the mechanism to move and soften your sharp star points.
2. **Question:** What is the ideal starting ISO and white balance Kelvin setting for capturing natural green aurora colors?
   **Answer:** ISO 1600 and a white balance set around 3500K.
3. **Question:** Why does autofocus fail when trying to shoot the night sky?
   **Answer:** Autofocus relies on contrast and daylight to lock onto subjects; in the pitch-dark night sky, it has nothing to contrast against and will endlessly hunt back and forth.
