# Northern Lights Lesson 3 - Exposing the dance

## 1. LEARNING OUTCOME
By the end of this lesson, the student can set up a complete manual exposure for the aurora borealis, starting from a baseline "working card" and actively adapting shutter speed, aperture, and ISO to capture both sharp structural curtains and faint glowing arches without clipping highlights or muddying the shadows.

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back. Today we put Manual mode to work on the most magical, unpredictable subject in the night sky — and I promise, by the end of this lesson you'll stop fearing the dark and start capturing the true motion of the lights.

Let's address the reality right out of the gate. Auroras are hunted, not scheduled. You cannot predict the exact moment a faint green arc will suddenly explode into pink-fringed dancing curtains overhead. Because the sky changes faster than any fixed recipe in a guidebook, you need a flexible starting point. We call that starting point your working card.

Let’s jump right into the camera simulator and set up your baseline working card for a moderate aurora display. First, turn your shooting mode dial firmly to M for Manual. Switch your lens to manual focus and ensure your focus is locked tight on infinity, just like we covered in our focus lesson. Turn your image stabilization, or IS, completely OFF because your camera is bolted to a sturdy tripod, and we don't want the lens looking for motion that isn't there. Set your white balance to around 3500 Kelvin to keep those greens looking natural against the deep night sky, and make sure your flash is off.

Now, let's dial in our core exposure triangle. Start by opening your aperture all the way. If you are shooting with a fast prime lens, that means f/1.4, f/1.8, or f/2.8. Let's set it to f/2.8 right now. Next, set your ISO to 1600. ISO is your sensor's sensitivity to light—think of it as turning up the gain on an audio track. Finally, set your shutter speed to 4 seconds. 

Let's take a test shot in the simulator. Look at that. At ISO 1600, f/2.8, and 4 seconds, you've gathered enough light to see the landscape foreground while freezing the basic structure of the aurora overhead without turning those dancing curtains into an unshaped green smear. 

But remember, the aurora loves to surprise us. What happens when the activity intensifies? Suddenly, those lazy green ribbons turn into high-speed dancing curtains that ripple across the sky like silk in a windstorm. If you leave your shutter at 4 seconds now, those fast movements will blur into a muddy green haze. We need to freeze time.

Let's adjust. Drop your ISO down to 800 to keep your digital noise cleaner since there is now plenty of light. Then, shorten your shutter speed down to 2.5 seconds. Take another shot in the simulator. Look at that! The faster shutter speed catches the crisp, sharp folds and distinct edges of the curtain. You’ve successfully adapted to a brighter, faster display.

Now let's look at the exact opposite scenario. You're standing out in the sub-zero wind, and the sky goes quiet. The aurora fades into a faint, ghostly green arc stretching lazily across the stars. If you shoot at ISO 800 and 2.5 seconds now, your image is going to be pitch black. The sensor is starving for light.

Let's save the shot. Raise your ISO up to 3200 to give your sensor a much stronger boost. Then, stretch your shutter speed out to 6 seconds to give the faint light more time to collect on the silicon chip. Take the shot. The faint glow appears clearly, revealing subtle details in the arc that your naked eye could barely pick out in the dark. 

How do we know if these settings are actually right? We read the histogram. That little graph on your screen is your best friend in the freezing cold. The golden rule for northern lights is simple: pull your exposure data off the left wall of the graph without clipping the bright green peaks on the right. If the hump of your data is squished completely against the left edge, your shadows are crushed, and your image is underexposed. If the data slams into the right edge, your bright green aurora highlights are blown out to pure white, destroying all the delicate texture inside the light. Use your ISO and shutter speed adjustments to slide that data nicely into the middle.

This brings us to the pro loop, which is the ultimate secret of great aurora photography. There is no magic setting that works for every minute of the night. The pros don't just set their camera once and walk away to drink hot cocoa. They follow a continuous loop: shoot a frame, review the image on the LCD screen, check the histogram, adjust one variable—like tweaking the shutter from 4 seconds to 3 seconds—and repeat. 

You are in total control. Embrace the cold, trust your baseline card, and let's get you ready for your practice drill.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Open your camera simulator and set up your baseline working card: ISO 1600, your lens's widest aperture (like f/2.8), and a 4-second shutter speed. Practice adapting to changing conditions: simulate a bright, fast-moving curtain by dropping your ISO toward 800 and shortening your shutter toward 2.5 seconds. Then simulate a faint, lazy glow by raising your ISO toward 3200 and stretching your shutter toward 6 seconds. Review every frame on your simulator screen, check your histogram to ensure data is pulled off the left wall without clipping the right, and practice the pro loop: shoot, review, adjust, repeat.

## 7. RECAP
- **Start with the Working Card:** Begin your session at ISO 1600, widest aperture (f/2.8 or lower), and 4 seconds to establish a reliable baseline.
- **Adapt to Speed:** Drop ISO toward 800 and shorten shutter toward 2.5 seconds when bright, fast-moving curtains threaten to blur.
- **Stretch for Faint Glows:** Raise ISO toward 3200 and extend shutter toward 6 seconds when the aurora dims into quiet, static arches.
- **Read the Histogram:** Pull your data off the left wall of the graph to avoid muddy shadows, but watch out for clipping the bright green peaks on the right.
- **Master the Pro Loop:** Continuously shoot, review, adjust, and repeat—the aurora changes faster than any fixed setting.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
1. **Question:** What is the recommended baseline "working card" setting for starting an aurora photography session?
   - **Answer:** ISO 1600, your lens's widest aperture (such as f/2.8), and a shutter speed of 2.5 to 6 seconds (with 4 seconds as a great starting point).
2. **Question:** How should you adjust your camera settings when the aurora changes from a slow, faint arc to fast, bright dancing curtains?
   - **Answer:** Shorten your shutter speed (toward 2.5 seconds) to freeze the motion, and drop your ISO (toward 800) since there is now plenty of ambient light.
3. **Question:** What does the histogram rule tell us to watch out for when shooting northern lights in the cold?
   - **Answer:** Pull your exposure data off the left wall to ensure your shadows aren't crushed, while making sure you do not clip the brightest green peaks on the right side of the graph.
