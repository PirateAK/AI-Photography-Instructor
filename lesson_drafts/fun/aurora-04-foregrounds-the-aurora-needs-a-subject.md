# Northern Lights Lesson 4 - Foregrounds: the aurora needs a subject

## 1. LEARNING OUTCOME
By the end of this lesson, the student can scout, compose, and execute a contextual northern lights photograph by anchoring the sky's motion with a compelling, daylight-pre-vetted foreground element.

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back. Today we tackle the secret ingredient that turns a snapshot of the sky into an unforgettable work of art: your foreground. 

Here is a hard truth about chasing the aurora. If your frame contains only green light against a black sky, you have taken a weather photo. A satellite image can do that. But you are a photographer. And a photograph is about *place*. It is about where you were standing when the magic happened. The aurora is the performance, but your foreground is the stage. A lonely log cabin, a jagged ridge of black spruce, or the glass-like reflection of a frozen lake turns a floating green ribbon into an emotional story. 

Let's start with the golden rule of foreground storytelling: never scout in the dark. That is the number one trap beginners fall into. You drive out, park on an icy shoulder at midnight, stumble into a snowbank, and try to compose a shot by the sickly glow of your phone screen. That is how gear gets broken and compositions get sloppy. 

Instead, practice our Advanced planning habit. Go out during daylight hours. Walk the perimeter of the lake. Find the abandoned homestead, the dramatic bend in the river, or the cluster of sentinel black spruce trees. Look through your viewfinder *before* the sun goes down to see what is clean, what is distracting, and what anchors the eye. Mark your spot mentally, or use a GPS waypoint. When the lights finally erupt at two in the morning, you should not be searching for a composition. You should already be locked in and waiting.

Now, let's talk about the technical marriage between your foreground and the sky. This is where manual mode saves the day. Let's dial in our proven aurora recipe right now. Turn your camera to Manual mode, and set your lens to manual focus. Find a bright star or a distant horizon light, zoom in to ten times on your live-view screen, and twist that focus ring until the star is a tiny, pin-sharp dot. Tape that focus ring down with gaffer tape so it never shifts in the cold. 

Next, set your aperture wide open. If you are shooting with a prime lens, dial it to f/1.4 or f/2. If you have a kit zoom, park it at f/2.8 or f/4 to let in as much starlight and green light as possible. 

What about shutter speed? We want the stars to remain pinpoints, not streaks, and we want to capture the fast, undulating folds of a bright aurora wave. Let us set our shutter speed to four seconds. 

Now, how do we expose the dark foreground without blowing out the glowing sky? We balance it with our sensor sensitivity. Set your ISO to 1600. If the aurora is faint and sluggish, bump it to ISO 3200. If the lights are blazing overhead like neon green fire, drop it down to ISO 800. 

Make sure your image stabilization, or IS, is turned completely off since your camera is locked down on a heavy, weighted tripod. And remember to turn off your camera's built-in flash. You never want to blast a flash into a snowy foreground—it ruins your night vision, disturbs the local wildlife, and makes your shot look like a snapshot in a parking lot.

Let's look at how this plays out in the field with three classic foreground choices for the exact same sky. 

Choice number one: The Cabin Silhouette. A rustic, weathered cabin gives the eye an immediate sense of human scale and shelter against the vast Arctic cold. Position your tripod low to the ground. Frame the cabin on the left or right third of your image using the rule of thirds, letting the cabin roof point up into the path of the dancing corona overhead. When the green lights ripple right above the chimney, the photo instantly tells a story of warmth and wilderness.

Choice number two: The Treeline. Dense black spruce trees create a jagged, textured black silhouette across the bottom third of your frame. By keeping your foreground dark and underexposed, you create a powerful contrast against the glowing upper atmosphere. Tilt your camera up just enough to give two-thirds of the frame to the sky, letting the trees anchor the bottom edge so the viewer feels grounded in the taiga.

Choice number three: The Still-Lake Reflection. If you find calm, unfrozen water or a smooth skim of early ice, drop your tripod as low to the water's surface as safely possible. The lake acts as a natural mirror, doubling the drama of the aurora. In this shot, the reflection *is* your foreground story. Set your focus carefully on the lakeshore rocks just a few feet in front of you, ensuring both your near rocks and distant sky are acceptably sharp.

When the aurora finally performs, stay calm. Take a test shot at ISO 1600, four seconds, f/2.8. Glance at your image histogram on the back of the camera. Is the graph bunched up too far to the left? Bump your ISO to 3200 or lengthen your shutter speed to six seconds. Is the green core of the aurora completely clipped and solid white? Drop your shutter speed to two and a half seconds. 

Auroras are hunted, not scheduled. They require patience, cold toes, and a well-scouted foreground. But when you combine a killer daytime location with clean manual settings, you will walk away with an image that goes far beyond a weather report. It becomes a true piece of landscape art.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
**Composition Drill:** Scout three distinct foregrounds for one sky in daylight—a cabin silhouette, a dense treeline, and a still-lake reflection—and physically mark or waypoint your tripod spots. When night falls and the aurora arrives, move to your pre-marked spots, lock in your camera settings (ISO 1600, 4s, f/2.8, manual focus to infinity), and execute all three compositions without fumbling in the dark. Let the sky do the performing and your foreground do the storytelling.

## 7. RECAP
- An aurora-only frame is just a weather photo; your foreground provides the scale, emotion, and sense of place.
- Always scout your foreground elements in daylight using the Advanced planning habit so you never have to fumble in the dark.
- Anchor your night exposure with reliable manual settings: ISO 1600–3200, an aperture of f/1.4–4, a 2.5–6 second shutter speed, manual focus to infinity, and Image Stabilization turned OFF.
- Utilize powerful storytelling anchors like cabin silhouettes, jagged treelines, or lake reflections to elevate your compositions.
- Auroras are hunted, not scheduled—patience and preparation are your best gear.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
1. *Question:* Why should you scout your foreground elements during daylight hours instead of at night?  
   *Answer:* Scouting in daylight lets you find clean compositions, avoid hidden hazards in the snow, and eliminates the need to fumble or guess your framing in the dark when the aurora unexpectedly appears.
2. *Question:* What are the recommended baseline camera settings for capturing a balanced foreground and aurora scene?  
   *Answer:* ISO 1600 to 3200, a shutter speed of 2.5 to 6 seconds, an aperture of f/1.4 to f/4 (wide open), manual focus locked to infinity, Image Stabilization OFF, and a sturdy tripod.
3. *Question:* How do you prevent your camera lens focus from shifting while shooting in freezing temperatures?  
   *Answer:* Manually focus on a bright star or distant light using live-view magnification, and then secure the focus ring firmly in place using gaffer tape.
