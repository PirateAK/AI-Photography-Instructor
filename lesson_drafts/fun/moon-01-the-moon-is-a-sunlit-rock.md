## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to MuskegMan Photography. Today we start our moon journey by breaking a massive misconception: the moon is not a night scene. It is a rock lit by full, glaring daylight. 

Think about it. The sun is shining directly on the moon's surface, just like it shines on the mountains or forests around your house at noon. When you point your camera at the night sky on auto, your camera panics. It sees a sea of blackness, tries to make the dark sky bright, and turns your glowing, textured moon into a featureless white blob. 

Let's fix that right now. We are going to take your camera off auto and put you in control. 

The secret to photographing the moon has been around for generations. It is called the Looney 11 rule. It’s a classic starting card that never lets you down: set your aperture to f/11, your ISO to 100, and your shutter speed to 1/100 of a second. 

Let's define those terms quickly so we're all on the same page. Aperture is the hole in your lens letting light in; f/11 is a narrow opening that keeps things sharp. ISO is your sensor's sensitivity to light; 100 is our clean, crisp baseline with zero digital noise. Shutter speed is how long your sensor is exposed; 1/100th of a second is fast enough to freeze any movement. 

Now, photography is all about trade-offs. What if f/11 isn't the sweet spot for your particular lens? You can use equivalent exposures. That means if you open your aperture to f/8, letting in twice as much light, you simply speed up your shutter to 1/250 of a second to take away half the light. Or bump your ISO to 200 and adjust accordingly. They all read the same amount of light on the sensor, giving you the exact same brightness. 

How do you know you've nailed it? You read your histogram. That's the little graph on your camera screen showing light distribution from pure black on the left to pure white on the right. When you look at the histogram of the moon's disc, you want the graph to sit comfortably in the middle. If your data touches or slams into the right wall of the graph, your highlights are clipped. That means the moon texture is gone, blown out to pure white. Back off your shutter speed or lower your ISO until that curve sits safely away from the right edge.

To get that crisp crater detail, your physical setup matters just as much as your numbers. Lock your camera down on a sturdy tripod. A tripod is a three-legged stand that holds your camera completely still, preventing blurry shots from hand shake. Switch your lens to manual focus. Autofocus gets confused by the high contrast of the night sky, so turn the focus ring yourself until the crater edges pop with razor-sharp clarity. Use your camera's spot metering mode, which measures light in just a tiny circle right over the moon, ignoring the dark sky around it. 

The keepers you get will cost you nothing, and every blurry failure is just teaching you how your gear reacts. Take many frames, experiment freely, and watch how quickly the magic happens. Let's head outside and put this into practice.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
**The Reframe Drill**: 
1. Mount your camera on a tripod and point it at the moon.
2. First, shoot on Aperture Priority (A/Av) mode with auto-ISO enabled. Take a shot and look at your image and histogram. Notice how the moon is a blown-out white circle and the histogram spikes hard against the right wall—that's the auto-mode mistake made visible.
3. Switch your camera to Manual (M) mode and dial in the Looney 11 baseline: f/11, ISO 100, and 1/100 second (or your lens's equivalent like f/8 at 1/250s). 
4. Switch your lens to manual focus, zoom in on your live view screen, and carefully twist the focus ring until the crater ridges are sharp.
5. Take a series of 10 frames. Review your histogram to ensure the data is safely away from the right wall, preserving the crater textures. Delete your failures freely and celebrate your sharp keepers!

## 7. RECAP
- The moon is lit by direct daytime sunlight, not darkness—expose it like a bright outdoor landscape.
- The Looney 11 rule gives you a foolproof starting point: f/11, ISO 100, and 1/100 second.
- Equivalent exposures let you trade aperture, shutter speed, and ISO without changing your image brightness.
- Read your histogram to protect your highlights; if the graph touches the right wall, your moon texture is gone.
- Always use a tripod, manual focus, and spot metering for tack-sharp lunar details.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
1. **Question**: Why do auto exposure modes usually fail when shooting the moon?
   *Answer*: Auto modes read the vast expanse of dark night sky around the moon and overcompensate, making the moon itself severely overexposed and washed out.
2. **Question**: What are the three core settings of the Looney 11 rule?
   *Answer*: f/11 aperture, ISO 100, and a shutter speed of 1/100 of a second (reciprocal of ISO).
3. **Question**: What does it mean on your camera's histogram if the graph touches the far right wall?
   *Answer*: It means your highlights are clipped (overexposed), resulting in the loss of all surface texture and detail on the moon.
