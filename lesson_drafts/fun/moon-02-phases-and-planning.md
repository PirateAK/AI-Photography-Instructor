## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to the MuskegMan Photography studio. Today we are tackling lesson two of Shoot the Moon: Phases and Planning. 

If you tried shooting the moon last night without checking a schedule, you probably noticed a funny thing. It wasn't in the same spot it was the night before. In fact, it might have been missing entirely when you went looking for it. Don't worry, that happens to all of us when we first start out. The moon isn't playing hide and seek; it’s on a very predictable schedule. 

Here is your first big takeaway for today: the moon rises about fifty minutes to an hour later each day. That means a full moon behaves completely differently than a crescent moon. 

Let's look at the full moon first. A full moon rises right around sunset. Because the sun is directly behind you when you face a full moon, the light hits the lunar surface straight-on. We call this flat, front-lit light. It’s bright, it’s easy to expose for—remember our Looney 11 rule from lesson one: ISO 100, f/11, and a shutter speed of 1 over 100th of a second—but it lacks texture. The craters look washed out because there are no shadows. 

Now, let's look at the crescent moon. A crescent moon rises much closer to the sun, often in the daytime or right at twilight. And the light hitting it is completely different. It's raking light. That means the light strikes the moon at a very low angle, casting deep, dramatic shadows across the craters along the terminator. 

The terminator, by the way, is simply the dividing line where day meets night on the lunar surface. It is your best friend for capturing incredible detail and texture. 

But shooting a crescent moon comes with a fun challenge. While the lit sliver of the moon is blindingly bright, the dark side of the moon is in deep shadow. Yet, you can often still see the dark side faintly glowing. That is called earthshine—sunlight bouncing off the Earth and illuminating the dark lunar landscape. 

To capture earthshine, you have to break the standard Looney 11 rule. You need to boost your camera settings. We bump our ISO up to 800 or even 1600, open up our aperture to f/4 or f/2.8, and let in more light. But here is the catch: if you do that, your bright crescent edge will blow out into a solid white blob. 

So how do the pros handle this? We plan like the advanced class. We use moon-tracking applications on our phones, like PhotoPills or The Photographer's Ephemeris. These apps show you the exact azimuth—that is just the compass direction—where the moon will rise, its exact phase, and how it aligns with your foreground landscape. 

When you know the phase and rise time in advance, you can plan your compositions. You aren't guessing. You arrive on location with your tripod already set up, your manual focus locked in on live view, and your spot meter ready. 

Let's head over to the camera simulator now for our practice drill. We are going to look at the crescent moon exposure challenge. 

Look at your screen. We have two exposure cards loaded up in the simulator. Card one is set for the bright crescent edge: ISO 100, f/8, 1 over 250th of a second. Notice how crisp the tip of the crescent is, but the rest of the moon is pitch black. 

Now, let's switch to card two: ISO 1600, f/4, 1 over 15th of a second. Watch what happens to the terminator and that dark side of the moon. Suddenly, the earthshine appears, and you can see the ghostly outline of the full sphere. 

In the real world, advanced photographers will sometimes blend two separate exposures—one for the bright crescent and one for the earthshine—to create a single, perfectly balanced image. But right now in the simulator, I want you to walk both exposure cards. Watch how the terminator detail appears and disappears as you adjust your shutter speed and ISO. 

Take a moment to run the simulator drill on your device. Play with those numbers. Remember, every mistake is just data helping you learn. I'll meet you right back here for a quick recap.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Phase drill: Open the camera simulator and load the crescent moon challenge. Walk through both exposure cards. Card 1: set your shutter to 1/250s, ISO 100, and f/8 to expose the razor-sharp lit crescent edge. Card 2: adjust your settings to 1/15s, ISO 1600, and f/4 to reveal the hidden earthshine on the dark side of the moon. Watch the terminator detail shift and practice recognizing how aperture and shutter speed trade off brightness for shadow detail.

## 7. RECAP
- The moon rises roughly fifty minutes later each day, making apps essential for accurate planning.
- Full moons offer flat, front-lit light that is easy to expose using the Looney 11 rule, while crescent moons feature dramatic raking light along the terminator.
- The terminator is the dividing line between day and night on the moon, where shadows create the best texture and detail.
- Earthshine illuminates the dark side of a crescent moon, requiring higher ISO values (800-1600) and wider apertures to capture.
- Moon-tracking apps let you predict azimuth, phase, and rise times so you can arrive on location prepared.
