## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to MuskegMan Photography’s Shoot the Moon. Today we are looking beyond the full moon. If you thought our bright lunar neighbor only had one look, you are in for a wonderful surprise. The moon has moods, and once you learn to read them, your camera becomes a true telescope for wonder. 

Let's start with the crescent sliver. Crescent moons are breathtaking, hanging low in a twilight sky like a golden fingernail. But they come with a technical puzzle. Only a tiny slice of the moon catches direct sunlight, while the rest of the sphere sits in shadow. 

When you look at a crescent through your viewfinder, don't just expose for the bright sliver, or the rest of the moon vanishes into blackness. Instead, look for a phenomenon called earthshine. Earthshine is sunlight bouncing off the blue oceans and white clouds of Earth, gently illuminating the dark side of the moon with a ghostly, ash-gray glow. 

To capture earthshine, you need to step outside our standard rules. While a full moon loves a fast shutter speed like one-two-hundred-and-fiftieth of a second, a crescent moon demands patience. Put your camera on your sturdy tripod. Switch your lens to manual focus, and use your live-view screen to zoom in five times on the bright edge of the crescent to nail your focus. 

For settings, let's dial in a wider aperture of f/four or f/eight, bump your ISO up to eight hundred, and let your shutter speed drop to one-second or even two-seconds. Because the Earth is rotating, anything longer than two seconds at a long focal length will give you motion blur, so keep an eye on your timing. Take a test shot, check your histogram, and enjoy that subtle, haunting texture of earthshine appearing on the dark side of the disk.

Next up: the lunar eclipse. There is nothing quite like watching a full moon turn the color of a copper penny as it slips into Earth's shadow. But here is the catch: when the moon goes into total eclipse, it loses ninety percent of its direct light. 

If you keep your full moon settings, your screen will be pitch black. During an eclipse, you need to open up your aperture as wide as your lens goes—say, f/two point eight or f/four. You will also need to push your ISO up to sixteen hundred or thirty-two hundred, and drop your shutter speed down to one-fiftieth or one-quarter of a second. You are trading a bit of digital grain for a glowing, blood-red moon that looks like a jewel hanging in velvet space. 

Whenever you are unsure of your settings under changing skies, fall back on our old friend, the Looney Eleven rule. Remember that baseline: set your aperture to f/eleven, your ISO to one hundred, and your shutter speed to one over your ISO—so one-hundredth of a second. It is your ultimate safety net when the clouds part and you need a shot right now.

Now, how do we tie all of this together so you never freeze up in the field? We build exposure cards. An exposure card is a simple, one-page cheat sheet you create for each lunar phase. 

Your first card is for the Full Moon: ISO one hundred, f/eleven, one-two-hundred-fiftieth of a second. Your second card is for the Crescent Moon: ISO eight hundred, f/five point six, one-half-second. Your third card is for the Eclipse: ISO thirty-two hundred, f/four, one-fiftieth of a second. Write these down on an index card or save them as a note on your phone. Tuck it right into your camera bag. 

Photography isn't about luck; it's about preparation meeting beauty. The next time the moon rises, whether it's a glowing full orb, a delicate sliver, or a copper eclipse, you won't be guessing your dials. You will pull out your card, set your camera with confidence, and capture an image that leaves your friends asking how you did it. Thank you for joining this course, and keep looking up.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
**The Card Drill:** Grab three index cards or open a notes app on your phone. Create your three-phase exposure plan: one card for the Full Moon (f/11, ISO 100, 1/250s), one for the Crescent Moon with earthshine (f/5.6, ISO 800, 1/2s), and one for the Lunar Eclipse (f/4, ISO 3200, 1/50s).

## 7. RECAP
- Crescent moons require longer exposures (1/2 second) and higher ISO (800) to capture faint earthshine on the dark side of the disk.
- Lunar eclipses block most sunlight, requiring wider apertures (f/4) and higher ISO (1600-3200) to keep the copper moon bright.
- The Looney 11 rule (f/11, ISO 100, 1/ISO shutter) remains your reliable baseline safety net.
- Building physical or digital exposure cards removes guesswork and turns variable moon phases into planned shots.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
1. *Question:* Why can't you use a fast shutter speed like 1/250s when photographing earthshine on a crescent moon?
   *Answer:* Because the dark side of the crescent receives only reflected light from Earth (earthshine), which is extremely dim and requires a slower shutter speed (like 1/2 second) to register on the sensor.
2. *Question:* What camera settings adjustment is most critical when transitioning from a bright full moon to a total lunar eclipse?
   *Answer:* You must increase your exposure significantly by opening your aperture, raising your ISO (e.g., 1600-3200), or slowing your shutter speed because the moon loses about 90% of its direct light during an eclipse.
3. *Question:* What is the primary purpose of creating moon exposure cards?
   *Answer:* To eliminate guesswork in the field by having pre-calculated manual settings ready for different lunar phases (full, crescent, eclipse).
