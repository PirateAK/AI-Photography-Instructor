# Following the Rainbow Lesson 1 - Chasing the Geometry

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to MuskegMan Photography. Today we kick off our special course, Following the Rainbow, with Lesson One: Chasing the Geometry. I promise you right now, by the end of this video, you'll stop treating rainbows like strokes of pure luck and start hunting them down like a pro. 

Let's bust a common myth right out of the gate. Rainbows don't just happen whenever. They obey strict, mathematical geometry. If you're standing in the field waiting for a colorful arc, looking in the wrong direction means you will miss it entirely, even if it's blazing right behind you. 

Here is the golden rule of rainbow geometry: the sun must always be directly behind your back, and the rain must be falling in front of you. Not to your left, not to your right—directly behind you. If the sun is in your eyes, turn around. You are looking at the wrong part of the sky. 

Let's talk about the numbers behind the magic. A primary rainbow is always refracted at a very specific angle: forty-two degrees. That means when sunlight hits a water droplet suspended in the air, it bounces off the back of that droplet and exits at a forty-two degree angle toward your eyes. Because of this fixed angle, a rainbow is actually a full circle. We only see an arc because the ground gets in the way. 

Now, how do you find the center of that circle in the wild? It is dead simple, and it's your secret weapon for composition. The center of every rainbow arc is the exact shadow of your own head. Seriously. When you see a rainbow forming, look for your shadow on the ground or on the mist. The tip of your shadow's head marks the absolute center point of that colorful ring. Once you know where the center is, you can anticipate how much of the arc is visible and frame your shot accordingly. 

Let's look at sun height, because that changes everything. The height of the sun dictates how tall the rainbow will appear in your viewfinder. When the sun is high in the sky—say, higher than forty-two degrees above the horizon—the rainbow dips below the horizon and you won't see it at all. That is why you only see rainbows early in the morning or late in the afternoon, when the sun is low. A lower sun gives you a taller, more dramatic bow stretching high into the clouds. A slightly higher sun pushes a flatter bow closer to the horizon. 

Now, let's switch over to our camera simulator and talk about gear settings. When a breaking storm clears out and a rainbow pops, the lighting is often wildly uneven. You might have dark, moody storm clouds on one side and bright, golden sunlight blasting through on the other. Do not trust auto-exposure here. It will get confused by the bright sky and underexpose your gorgeous foreground. 

Let's switch your camera dial to Manual mode. Set your ISO to a clean, noise-free ISO 100. Choose an aperture of f/8 to keep both your foreground landscape and your mid-ground elements sharp. Adjust your shutter speed to match the ambient light—let's start at one-two-hundred-fiftieth of a second (1/250s) and check our histogram. Make sure your highlights aren't blown out on the brightest clouds, and your shadows retain just enough detail in the trees or rocks below. 

If you use a circular polarizer filter on your lens—which is a piece of glass you twist to cut glare—use it with caution. A polarizer can dramatically darken a blue sky and make your rainbow pop right off the screen. But rotate it just a fraction too far, and it can completely wipe out the rainbow because a rainbow is made of polarized light! Always rotate your polarizer while watching the bow carefully through the viewfinder. If the colors start fading, back off the rotation immediately. 

Finally, let's talk about composition. Beginners often make the mistake of centering the rainbow and nothing else. A floating rainbow in an empty sky can feel a bit flat. Instead, treat the rainbow as a supporting element in a broader landscape story. Frame your shot with a strong foreground anchor—like an old wooden fence, a winding dirt road, or jagged mountain peaks. Let the rainbow sweep across the frame, leading the viewer's eye right into your main subject. 

Rainbow chasing takes patience, and some days the weather simply refuses to cooperate. If you open your camera simulator today and don't see a rainbow, don't sweat it. The simulator is set up to let you practice locking in that tricky storm-light landscape exposure so you are fully prepared the moment real nature puts on a show.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Head outside right after a rain shower when the sun is breaking through the clouds. 
1. Turn your back to the sun immediately. 
2. Locate the shadow of your own head on the ground—that is your center point. 
3. Scan the mist or rain showers opposite the sun to find your arc. 
4. Switch your camera to Manual mode (ISO 100, f/8, 1/250s as a starting point) and compose a landscape shot using a strong foreground element, like a tree or rock, with the rainbow sweeping through the frame. 
*(Note: If there is no rain or rainbow near you today, load up the camera simulator, practice dialing in your manual exposure for variable storm light, and ready your scene for the next clear day.)*

## 7. RECAP
- Rainbows require specific geometry: the sun must be behind you and rain in front.
- The primary rainbow forms at a fixed 42-degree angle from the anti-solar point.
- The center of every rainbow arc is the exact shadow of your own head.
- Low sun angles create taller, more dramatic bows; high sun pushes bows below the horizon.
- Be careful with circular polarizers—too much rotation can accidentally erase the rainbow.
- Treat the rainbow as a supporting element in a broader landscape composition rather than the sole subject.
