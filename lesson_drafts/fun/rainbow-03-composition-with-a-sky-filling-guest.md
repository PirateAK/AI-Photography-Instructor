# Following the Rainbow Lesson 3 - Composition with a sky-filling guest

## 1. LEARNING OUTCOME
By the end of this lesson, the student can frame a rainbow using a grounding subject to create a narrative landscape, and make a deliberate choice on whether to include or exclude a secondary bow.

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to MuskegMan Photography. Today we are tackling one of the trickiest traps in weather photography: the floating bow. We have all been there. You spot a brilliant rainbow arching across the Alaskan sky, you pull over, raise your camera, and snap a picture of just the colors. And when you look at it later on your screen? It looks flat, distant, and honestly, a bit boring. 

Here is why. A rainbow is an optical illusion. It has no physical substance, so it cannot anchor a frame all by itself. Today, we fix that. By the end of this lesson, you will know how to turn a passing weather event into a real photograph by inviting a sky-filling guest into your composition. Let us dive in.

Our first rule of thumb is simple: give your rainbow a friend. A rainbow is huge, spanning miles across the sky, so it needs a scale bar. Think of a lonely black spruce tree, a winding gravel road, or a cabin tucked away in the taiga. When you place a solid, real-world object under that glowing arc, you transform a simple weather snapshot into a narrative photograph. Your viewer stops seeing just colors in the sky, and starts wondering who is standing under that light.

Let us look at two ways to frame this. First, the wide approach. When the sun is low behind your back—remember our forty-two-degree geometry from lesson one—the entire semicircle might be visible. To capture the full sweep, you need to go wide. Slap a twenty-four millimeter lens on your camera, drop your ISO to one hundred, set your aperture to f-eight for sharp details across the landscape, and step back. You want to include enough foreground—maybe a rushing creek or a stand of fireweed—to lead the viewer’s eye straight up into the apex of the bow. If you crop too tight here, you chop off the very thing that makes the scene majestic.

Now, let us flip the script and go tight. Instead of chasing the whole arc, zoom in on where the rainbow touches the ground. This is where the magic happens. Grab a seventy to two-hundred-millimeter telephoto lens and scan the horizon where the colors seem to physically melt into the trees or hills. By isolating this touchdown point, you create a sense of mystery. Is the rainbow really landing in that exact patch of muskeg? Your viewer's imagination fills in the rest. Use an aperture of f-four to slightly blur the background forest, making that vibrant touchdown point pop right off the sensor.

Finally, we need to talk about the guest that shows up uninvited: the secondary bow. Sometimes, Mother Nature blesses us with a fainter, second rainbow arching outside the main one, with its colors completely reversed. When this happens, you have a creative choice to make. Do you include both bows, or do you isolate the primary star? 

If the background sky is messy or cluttered, including the faint secondary bow can make your image look muddy and confusing. In that case, use your circular polarizer—remember, rotate it just enough to darken the blue sky and boost the contrast of the primary bow, letting the secondary fade away. But if the sky is dark and stormy, embrace the double bow. Open your composition just enough to capture the full pair, celebrating the rare optical physics unfolding right above your lens. Let us head to the field and put these framing choices into practice.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Head outside during the next sun-and-shower mix with your camera and a zoom lens. 
1. **The Wide Anchor:** Find a foreground subject (a fence, a tree, a vehicle) and shoot a wide-angle frame (around 24mm) that captures the entire arc of the rainbow soaring above it. 
2. **The Tight Touchdown:** Zoom in to 100mm or more and isolate the exact spot where the rainbow meets the ground. 
3. **The Double Check:** Look for a secondary bow. Take one shot including the faint double bow, and a second shot where you adjust your polarizer to make the secondary bow disappear, leaving only the punchy primary arc. Compare the two later to decide which story you prefer.

## 7. RECAP
- Rainbows alone are floating optical illusions; they always need a foreground anchor subject to tell a compelling story.
- Use a wide lens (24mm, f/8, ISO 100) to capture the grandeur of the full semicircle alongside a strong landscape element.
- Use a telephoto lens to isolate the touchdown point where the colors meet the earth for a mysterious, intimate framing.
- Make a deliberate choice regarding secondary bows: use your polarizer to eliminate a messy double bow, or embrace both if the background is clean and dramatic.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
- **Question 1:** Why is a photograph of just a rainbow in an empty sky often unsatisfying?
  - *Answer:* Because a rainbow has no physical substance and acts as an optical illusion, meaning it lacks an anchor or scale without a foreground subject.
- **Question 2:** What lens focal length and aperture settings are recommended for capturing a wide, full-semircle rainbow landscape?
  - *Answer:* A wide lens around 24mm, set to f/8 for edge-to-edge sharpness, paired with a low ISO like 100.
- **Question 3:** How do you handle a faint secondary rainbow if the background sky is cluttered and messy?
  - *Answer:* You rotate your circular polarizer to darken the blue sky and boost primary contrast, which helps fade out the distracting secondary bow.
