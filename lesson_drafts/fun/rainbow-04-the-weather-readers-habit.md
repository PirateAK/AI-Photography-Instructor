## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back. Today we bring our entire rainbow-chasing toolkit together into one powerful, repeatable routine. I promise, by the end of this lesson, you will stop waiting for random luck and start anticipating the light like a seasoned weather-reader.

Rainbows cannot be scheduled, but they can definitely be anticipated. You do not need a weather miracle; you just need to know what to look for and where to stand. Let us start with the core geometry we have built across this course. To see a primary rainbow, two things must happen simultaneously. The sun must be low behind your back, sitting lower than forty-two degrees in the sky. And active rain, mist, or spray must be falling out in front of you. 

When you see those two conditions align, do not pack your gear away. Look up immediately. 

Let us look at how you read your local weather apps to make this happen. Stop looking just for clear blue skies or continuous gloom. You want the classic rain-then-clear pattern. Look for a fast-moving frontal system where heavy rain cells are followed immediately by clearing skies and direct sunlight. That sweet spot right at the edge of the storm front is your golden window. It usually lasts only ten to fifteen minutes, which means your camera needs to be ready before you even step outside.

This brings us to our camera setup. When you spot that sun-behind, rain-ahead scenario, you need to transition into your Landscape style instantly. On your camera simulator or your real body, dial in your settings for maximum depth and punchy color. Set your aperture to f/8 or f/11 to keep your entire landscape sharp from foreground rocks to distant mountains. Keep your ISO at 100 for maximum dynamic range, and adjust your shutter speed to match the ambient light—often landing around one-two-hundred-and-fiftieth of a second.

Now, let us talk about exposure compensation. Rainbows are bright, luminous phenomena, but they sit against darker storm clouds. If you let your camera's light meter average out the scene, it will often overexpose the bright sky and wash out the delicate pastel colors of the arc. Dial in minus one exposure compensation, written as minus one EV. This drops your exposure slightly, deepens the background storm clouds, and makes the neon reds and greens of the bow pop right off the sensor.

Here is the biggest gear trap to avoid: your circular polarizer. Polarizers are fantastic tools for cutting glare on water and darkening blue skies. But remember how a rainbow works. A rainbow is polarized light. If you rotate your polarizer to the wrong angle, you will literally erase the rainbow from your frame. When you spot the arc, keep your polarizer completely off, or rotate it carefully while watching the bow through the viewfinder to ensure you are darkening the sky *behind* the rainbow without filtering out the bow itself.

So, what is the secret of the masters? The habit is the class. Every single time rain falls while the sun is shining on your back, you stop what you are doing and check the sky. It is a reflex. You do not wait for a special photography trip to the mountains or the coast. Rainbows happen over suburban backyards, city parking lots, and highway overpasses every single week. The rainbow always finds the prepared photographer.

Let us build your physical safeguard right now. We are going to tape a simple checklist directly to your camera bag. Write these four steps down on a weatherproof label or index card: Sun behind you, rain ahead, polarizer off, and minus one EV. Add your Landscape picture style, and make sure your favorite wide-angle lens—something like a twenty-four-millimeter prime or a sixteen-to-thirty-five-millimeter zoom—is mounted and ready to go.

By building this simple weather-reading habit, you transform yourself from a casual observer into an intentional light hunter. The next time the storm breaks and the sun peeks out behind your shoulder, you will not be scrambling for settings. You will already be framing the arc. Let us jump into the practice drill and lock this habit in for good.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Your practice drill this week is the **Rainbow Checklist and Seven-Day Commitment**. 

1. **The Bag Label:** Write your rainbow checklist on a piece of tape or an index card and tape it securely to the outside of your camera bag: *Sun behind you, rain ahead, polarizer off, −1 EV, subject under the arc.*
2. **The Commitment:** For the next seven days, every single time rain falls while the sun is shining on your back, look up immediately. Step outside, load your Landscape style, dial in your settings, and shoot whatever is under that arc. Don't worry about perfection; build the reflex.

## 7. RECAP
- **The Golden Geometry:** Rainbows require the sun low behind your back and active rain falling in front of you at a 42-degree angle.
- **Read the Weather:** Look for fast-moving frontal systems with the classic rain-then-clear pattern to find your shooting window.
- **Camera Prep:** Set your camera to the Landscape style, dial in −1 EV to preserve highlight colors in the arc, and use your polarizer with caution so you don't filter out the bow.
- **The Habit:** The secret to capturing rare light is building a daily observation habit—every time sun and rain overlap, you look up and shoot.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
1. **Question:** What is the specific sun angle required behind the photographer to see a primary rainbow?
   **Answer:** The sun must be low behind your back, specifically sitting lower than 42 degrees in the sky.
2. **Question:** Why should you apply −1 exposure compensation (-1 EV) when shooting a bright rainbow?
   **Answer:** It prevents the camera's meter from overexposing the bright sky, deepening the background storm clouds and making the colors of the rainbow pop.
3. **Question:** How does a circular polarizer interact with a rainbow, and what should you do with it?
   **Answer:** Because rainbows consist of polarized light, a polarizer can accidentally erase the bow if rotated to the wrong angle; you should either remove it or carefully rotate it while watching through the viewfinder.
