## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back. Today we master autofocus—and I promise, by the end of this lesson you'll stop guessing whether your shot is sharp and start trusting your camera to nail the focus every single time. 

If you've ever looked at a sequence of action shots and noticed that half of them are blurry right on the subject's nose, don't worry. That is completely normal when you leave your camera on factory default settings. Autofocus isn't magic; it's a computer making rapid calculations based on contrast, distance, and motion. And just like any computer, it needs you to give it the right instructions. Today, we're going to learn how to speak your camera's focusing language.

Let's start with the foundation: Focus Modes. Look at the side of your lens barrel or check your top LCD screen, and you'll see letters like AF-S, AF-C, and sometimes AF-A. What do these mean? They tell the camera *how* to behave once you press the shutter button halfway down.

AF-S stands for Autofocus Single. Think of the "S" as stationary. When you choose AF-S, you press the shutter button halfway, your camera locks onto a subject—let's say a portrait of a friend standing still—and it locks focus. Once it achieves focus, it locks tight. If your friend takes a step forward after the focus locks, they will be out of focus unless you let go and press the shutter halfway down again. Use AF-S for landscapes, still life, architecture, and posed portraits. Settings-wise, pair AF-S with a stationary subject, an aperture like f/2.8 for portraits or f/8 for landscapes, and let the camera do its precise single-point calculation.

Now, what happens if your subject starts running toward you? That's where AF-C comes in. AF-C stands for Autofocus Continuous. Think of the "C" as continuous tracking. When you select AF-C, your camera doesn't just lock focus once and stop. It constantly recalculates distance as long as you hold the shutter button halfway down. If your subject moves closer or further away, the lens elements physically shift inside to track them in real time. Use AF-C for sports, pets, playing children, flying birds, and street photography. It is your go-to mode for anything with a pulse that won't stay still.

What about AF-A? That stands for Autofocus Automatic. In this mode, the camera tries to guess whether your subject is moving or still, and automatically switches between AF-S and AF-C. While it sounds convenient, I generally recommend steering clear of it. Why? Because sometimes the camera guesses wrong at the worst possible moment, switching to single focus just as your runner starts sprinting. Take manual control of your tools: choose AF-S for still subjects, and AF-C for moving ones. You'll get vastly more consistent keepers.

So you've chosen your Focus Mode. Now you need to decide *where* the camera should look. That brings us to AF Area Modes. This is where Nikon's system really shines, offering you incredible precision. 

Let's look at the first group: Single-Point AF and Dynamic Area AF. When you look through your optical viewfinder, you'll see a grid of little square focus points. With Single-Point AF, you manually select just one tiny square using your multi-selector pad or joystick. That square is your laser-sharp focus tool. If you want a portrait where the sharpest point is precisely on the left eyeball, you place that single point right on the eyeball. The camera focuses only on what's under that specific square. It gives you absolute, surgical control. 

But what if you're photographing a soccer player dribbling down the field, and it's tough to keep that tiny single square glued to their jersey? That's when you switch to Dynamic Area AF. Dynamic Area lets you pick a primary focus point, say a 9-point or 21-point cluster. If your subject momentarily slips off that exact center point, the surrounding focus points kick in as backup helpers, borrowing contrast information from their neighbors to maintain the lock. It's like having training wheels that give you a wider margin of error while still letting you dictate the general composition.

Now let's step up to the advanced modes: 3D-Tracking and Auto-Area AF. 3D-Tracking is one of the most powerful features in your Nikon toolkit. You select a point, place it on your subject—let's say a red bicycle riding across your frame—and press the shutter halfway down in AF-C mode. As the bicycle moves from left to right, you can literally watch the active focus point dance across your viewfinder grid, tracking the color and pattern of the bike. It frees you up to focus purely on your composition and timing, while the camera handles the tracking mechanics.

On the other hand, Auto-Area AF hands the reins completely over to the camera. The camera scans the entire frame, uses face and eye detection algorithms if available, and decides what to focus on. While it's great for quick, casual family snapshots where you don't have time to select a point, it can easily get confused if a branch crosses in front of your subject or if multiple people are in the frame. For intentional photography, stick to Single-Point, Dynamic Area, or 3D-Tracking so *you* remain the artist in charge.

Let's put this all together into a reliable field recipe. How do we shoot action without the frustration? Here is the Nikon action recipe: set your camera to AF-C for continuous tracking, pair it with 9-point or 21-point Dynamic Area AF, and set your shutter speed to at least 1/500s to freeze the motion. When you look through the viewfinder, keep that primary focus point dot placed firmly on your subject's face or body, and follow their movement smoothly while pressing the shutter. 

Mastering autofocus isn't about letting the camera do all the work—it's about knowing how to configure the camera's brain so it executes your exact creative vision. Practice switching between AF-S and AF-C today, and watch your sharp-shot percentage climb.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Find a moving subject—a pet, a running child, or cars passing on a street. Set your camera to AF-C and 9-point Dynamic Area AF. Dial in a shutter speed of 1/500s (adjusting ISO and aperture for a balanced exposure). Practice tracking your subject through the viewfinder for three seconds, keeping the focus point dot glued to the subject before pressing the shutter. Review your images to ensure the point of sharpest focus lands precisely where you intended.

## 7. RECAP
- **AF-S (Single):** Locks focus once; ideal for stationary subjects like landscapes and portraits.
- **AF-C (Continuous):** Continuously recalculates distance; essential for moving subjects like sports and wildlife.
- **Single-Point & Dynamic Area:** Give you precise manual control over where the camera looks, with backup points for motion.
- **3D-Tracking:** Automatically follows a subject's color and pattern across the viewfinder grid.
- **The Action Recipe:** Combine AF-C, Dynamic Area AF, and a shutter speed of 1/500s or faster to nail moving action.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
1. *Question:* Which autofocus mode should you choose when photographing a sleeping cat?
   *Answer:* AF-S (Autofocus Single), because the cat is stationary and you want a locked, precise focus point.
2. *Question:* What is the primary difference between Single-Point AF and Dynamic Area AF?
   *Answer:* Single-Point uses only the exact focus square you select, whereas Dynamic Area uses surrounding points as backup helpers if the subject moves slightly off the main point.
3. *Question:* Why is AF-C paired with a faster shutter speed like 1/500s recommended for action photography?
   *Answer:* AF-C keeps the subject in focus as they move toward or away from you, while 1/500s (or faster) freezes the physical motion blur of their movement.
