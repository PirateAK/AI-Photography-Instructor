## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to our online workshop series. Today we are tackling lighting on location, and I promise you, by the end of this session, you will stop guessing where to put your flash and start painting with it. If you have ever walked into a dim room, popped a flash on your camera, and ended up with a harsh, flat subject against a pitch-black background, take a deep breath. That is completely normal, and it is entirely fixable. Today, we are breaking down a foolproof, step-by-step workflow that turns tricky indoor spaces into your favorite studio.

Let's start with a simple truth about how light works in any room. Every single location has two layers of light happening at the exact same time. First, you have the ambient light—that is the practical room light coming from windows, overhead fixtures, and lamps. Second, you have your introduced light—that is the flash or strobe you bring into the space. Most beginners try to fight the room light with their flash. We are going to do the exact opposite. We are going to make them work together as partners.

To do that, we use a concept I call ambient-first thinking. Instead of turning on your flash first, you turn it off completely. You walk into the room, you look at the scene, and you ask yourself: how do I want the background to look? Because remember, your shutter speed controls the room light, while your aperture and flash power control your subject. Let's say we are standing in a living room with soft window light coming from the left, and a warm tungsten floor lamp in the corner. We want to capture that cozy room ambiance without letting it ruin our subject's skin tones. 

Let's look at our camera simulator on screen right now. We are shooting on a full-frame sensor at ISO 400. Why ISO 400? Because it gives us enough sensitivity to capture the room's natural brightness without introducing ugly digital noise, which is just those tiny colored speckles that make an image look grainy. Now, look at our exposure meter. Without any flash, the room feels a little dim. If we want to bring up the background, we don't touch our flash. We adjust our shutter speed. Let's drop our shutter speed from one-two-hundred-fiftieth of a second down to one-sixtieth of a second. Look at that on the simulator. The shadows open up, the wall texture appears, and the window light starts to feel warm and inviting. 

Now, here is the magic rule you need to tattoo on your brain: your shutter speed controls the ambient room light, and your aperture controls your flash power. Let me repeat that because it is the master key to location lighting. Shutter speed lets you make the room brighter or darker. Aperture—along with your flash power setting—lets you make your subject brighter or darker. Because our focal plane shutter can only synchronize with a flash up to a certain speed—usually around one-two-hundred-fiftieth of a second—we use our shutter speed anywhere from one-sixtieth to one-two-hundred-fiftieth to dial in the background. Anything slower than one-sixtieth and you risk motion blur if your subject moves. Anything faster than sync speed and you get a black bar across your frame. So we keep our shutter locked right in that sweet spot.

Let's move on to phase two: adding our single light source. We have our background looking gorgeous at ISO 400, f/4, and one-sixtieth of a second. But when we look at our subject sitting on the couch, their face is a bit underexposed, and the room's mixed lighting is giving their skin a muddy green tint. This is where our single flash comes in. We are going to place a speedlight on a light stand just outside the frame to the left, fitted with a small softbox to diffuse the light—which just means spreading it out so it creates soft, flattering shadows instead of harsh, punchy ones. 

We turn the flash on. We set it to manual power at one-quarter strength. We take a test shot. Now look at our simulator screen. The background looks exactly the same as it did a moment ago because our shutter speed hasn't changed. But our subject? They pop. They are crisp, their skin tones are clean and natural, and they stand out from the couch behind them. But wait—let's examine the shot closely. The subject's face is a tiny bit too bright compared to the rest of the room. They look like they were pasted onto the background. How do we fix that? Do we change the shutter speed? No! Remember, shutter speed controls the room. If we change the shutter speed now, our background gets darker or brighter. We want to leave the room alone. 

Instead, we adjust the variable that controls the subject: our aperture or our flash power. Let's go into our flash menu and drop the power from one-quarter down to one-eighth power. Let's take another test shot. Perfect. The flash output is now dialed in to match the ambient light of the room. The transition from the warm room light to our clean flash light is seamless. It looks like natural window light, but better. It has direction, shape, and punch. 

Let's do another quick scenario to lock this in. Imagine you walk into a much darker room—say, a dimly lit restaurant or a moody office with heavy curtains. If you set your shutter speed to one-sixtieth and your aperture to f/4, and the room still looks pitch black on your exposure meter, what should you adjust? If you guessed ISO, you are spot on. We bump our ISO up from 400 to 1600. That quadruples our sensor's sensitivity to light, instantly bringing the background details out of the shadows. Now our ambient exposure is locked in at ISO 1600, f/4, and one-sixtieth of a second. Once that background looks the way you want it to look, you freeze those settings. You do not touch them again. From that second forward, every adjustment you make is strictly about your flash. 

Let's talk about color temperature for a moment, because mixing ambient light and flash is where many photographers get tripped up by weird skin colors. Ambient light has a color temperature measured in Kelvin—which is simply the scientific scale we use to describe how warm or cool a light source looks. Indoor tungsten bulbs and lamps give off a very warm, orange light, around 3000 Kelvin. Standard daylight from a window or an ungelled flash is much cooler and bluer, around 5600 Kelvin. When you mix 3000K room light with 5600K flash light without a plan, your camera gets confused. It tries to balance the whole scene, leaving your subject looking blue or your background looking overwhelmingly orange. 

The fix is simple: decide which light source is your primary mood setter. If you want the room to feel warm and moody, you can put a matching orange warming gel—a thin, heat-resistant colored film—over the front of your flash head. That brings your flash down to match the 3000K temperature of the room bulbs. Now, both the room and your subject share the exact same color temperature. Your camera's white balance setting—say, set to Tungsten or Auto—renders the entire scene cleanly without weird color clashes. 

Let's review our live simulator demo one more time to watch this workflow in action. Watch the screen as we reset the camera. Step one: turn off the flash. Step two: adjust ISO to 400 for a clean image. Step three: dial in aperture to f/5.6 for good depth of field so both the subject's eyes and shoulders are sharp. Step four: adjust shutter speed to one-one-hundred-twenty-fifth of a second until the room background looks naturally exposed. We have now built our foundation. Step five: turn on our off-camera flash at one-half power, positioned at a forty-five-degree angle to our subject. Step six: take the shot. The background is gorgeous, the subject is beautifully lit, and the two light sources live in total harmony. 

As you move through your practice session today, I want you to remember that lighting on location isn't about mastering some giant, complicated studio setup with six different lights. It is about control. You control the room with your shutter. You control the subject with your flash and aperture. Take your time, experiment with these values, and I will see you in the next lesson where we take these skills outside into natural daylight.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
**Ambient-First Practice Drill:**
1. Open your camera simulator or grab your camera and walk into any indoor room with a window or lamp.
2. Turn your flash or strobe completely off. Set your camera to Manual (M) mode.
3. Choose a starting ISO (e.g., ISO 400 or 800) and an aperture of f/4.
4. Adjust **only** your shutter speed (keep it between 1/60s and 1/250s) until the background room exposure looks bright and natural to your eye. Lock these settings in—do not change them.
5. Now, turn on your single off-camera flash (or simulator flash source) at 1/4 power. Take a photo of a subject in the room.
6. Evaluate: Is the subject too bright? Adjust **only** your flash power down to 1/8 (leave shutter and aperture alone). Take another shot until your subject and room blend into a clean, balanced portrait.

## 7. RECAP
- **Ambient-First Workflow:** Always establish your background exposure using the room's available light before introducing your flash.
- **The Golden Rule:** Shutter speed controls the ambient room brightness; aperture and flash power control your subject's brightness.
- **Sync Speed Limits:** Keep your shutter speed between 1/60s and 1/250s when shooting with flash to avoid motion blur and shutter curtain clipping.
- **Color Temperature Harmony:** Match your flash color to your room's ambient light using warming gels if necessary to prevent unnatural skin tones.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
1. **Question:** If your background room exposure is too dark while using flash, which setting should you adjust first—shutter speed or flash power?
   **Answer:** Shutter speed (or ISO if shutter is already at 1/60s). Shutter speed controls the ambient room light, while flash power only affects the subject.
2. **Question:** Why do we avoid using a shutter speed of 1/500s with a standard focal-plane shutter flash setup?
   **Answer:** Because most cameras exceed their maximum flash sync speed around 1/250s, resulting in a dark, shadowed black bar across part of your image.
3. **Question:** What is Kelvin used to measure in location lighting?
   **Answer:** Color temperature—describing how warm (orange/tungsten) or cool (blue/daylight) a light source appears in your scene.
