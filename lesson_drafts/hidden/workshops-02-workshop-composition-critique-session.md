# Online Workshops Lesson 2 - Workshop: Composition Critique Session

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to our online workshop series. Today we are diving into the live-critique session, and I promise you, this is the absolute fastest learning loop in photography. If you have ever felt stuck with an image that looks flat, cluttered, or just plain boring, you are in the right place. We are going to look at real images sent in by photographers just like you, break down what is working, fix what is not, and then talk about how to re-shoot them. Never fear a critique here; every mistake is just normal data and entirely fixable. Let us dive right in.

When we talk about a great photograph, we are really talking about three core pillars: framing, timing, and intent. Framing is simply what you choose to include and exclude inside your four edges. Timing is the exact fractional second you press the shutter button. Intent is the underlying story or emotion you want the viewer to feel. If any one of those three pillars is out of alignment, the photo falls flat. 

Let us pull up our first image on the screen using the MuskegMan app. This is a landscape shot taken on a Sony mirrorless camera at ISO 100, f/8, and one-hundred-and-fiftieth of a second. Right away, let us run it through the app's composition scoring tool. The app scores it at a fifty-eight out of one hundred. Why? Let us look closer. 

Look at the bottom edge of the frame. There is a severed log cutting right across the lower left corner. That is what we call an edge collision—a visual distraction pulling the viewer's eye right out of the picture. Also, look at the horizon line. It sits dead center, dividing the frame equally in half, which creates visual confusion about whether the subject is the sky or the foreground. 

How do we fix this? We don't need a new camera or an expensive lens. We just need a physical adjustment. When the photographer re-shoots this, they need to take three steps forward to step over that foreground log, or crouch lower to eliminate it entirely. And by placing the horizon line one-third up from the bottom—following the rule of thirds, which is just placing your main subjects along a tic-tac-toe grid for natural balance—the image instantly gains breathing room and depth. 

Let us look at a second image. This one is a street portrait shot at ISO 400, f/2.8, and one-thousandth of a second. The app scores it at sixty-two. The shutter speed is plenty fast enough to freeze motion, and the aperture gives us a lovely, blurry background, known as bokeh—that aesthetic quality of blur in the out-of-focus parts of an image. But let us examine the timing and intent. 

The subject is mid-blink, and their head is turned slightly away from the primary light source, casting a heavy shadow across both eyes. The intent seems to be a candid portrait capturing local culture, but the split-second timing missed the spark of connection. The photographer clicked too fast, before the person settled into a natural pose. 

So what is the prescription? In the re-shoot, we change our timing. We wait for the natural pause in conversation. We wait until the subject turns three degrees toward the window light to illuminate their eyes. And we re-frame slightly tighter, moving from a loose waist-up shot to a chest-up portrait that eliminates a distracting red sign glowing in the upper right corner. 

This is the beauty of the critique loop. You shoot, you analyze, you find the gap, and you re-shoot. Every professional photographer on earth goes through this exact mental process, often in a matter of seconds. But slowing down to do it in a structured workshop setting builds muscle memory. Soon, you will catch these edge distractions before you even press the shutter button. 

Let us talk about how you are going to apply this today. In a moment, we will look at your practice brief. You are going to pull an existing image from your own archive, load it into the app, and run a complete composition analysis. Look at the score, read the specific issues flagged by the app, and note the composition tips provided. Then, you are going to grab your camera, go back to that exact same subject or a very similar one, and execute a re-shoot with one deliberate, conscious change based on your analysis. 

Before we wrap up, let us do a quick recap of what we covered. First, framing is about controlling your edges and eliminating distractions like severed logs or glowing signs. Second, timing is about waiting for the right micro-second of expression and light. Third, intent is your clear visual story. And fourth, critiques are your friend—they are the shortcut to better images without needing to buy a single piece of new gear. 

Thank you for joining today's workshop session. Pull up your app, find that practice image, and I will see you in the next lesson where we master lighting ratios. Keep shooting, keep learning, and I'll see you out there.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Bring your own image to the critique: open it in the app first to analyze your score, listed issues, and composition notes. Then, grab your camera and re-shoot the scene making one deliberate change based on the app's feedback (such as shifting your angle, removing a distracting element, or waiting for better timing).

## 7. RECAP
- Framing is what you include and exclude; watch out for edge collisions and distracting objects.
- Timing is the exact fractional second you press the shutter, impacting expression and action.
- Intent is your clear visual story and the mood you want the viewer to experience.
- The critique loop (shoot, analyze, adjust, re-shoot) is the fastest way to improve your photography skills.
- Mistakes are normal data and entirely fixable with simple physical adjustments, not new gear.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
1. **Question:** What does a score from the app's composition analysis tool primarily help identify?  
   **Answer:** Specific visual flaws like edge distractions, awkward horizon placements, or poor framing choices.
2. **Question:** What is an edge collision in photography composition?  
   **Answer:** An unwanted object (like a log or a sign) touching or cutting across the edge of the frame, pulling the viewer's eye away from the main subject.
3. **Question:** What is the primary benefit of the live-critique learning loop?  
   **Answer:** It accelerates learning by diagnosing exact mistakes and immediately applying corrections through a deliberate re-shoot.
