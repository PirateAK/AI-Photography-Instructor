# Intermediate Lesson 1 — Manual Exposure in Practice

Class: Intermediate
Prerequisite: Beginner lessons on shooting modes, exposure, focus, and depth of field

## 1. LEARNING OUTCOME

By the end of this lesson, learners can choose shutter speed, aperture, and ISO deliberately instead of relying entirely on the camera's suggested exposure — and hold that control consistent across two different shooting situations.

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT

**Welcome**

In the Beginner class, you learned what each dial does. Now we make those dials obey in real situations. Manual mode isn't about ceremony — it's about consistency: when you choose the settings, the camera can't second-guess the look you decided on.

**The meter is a guide, not a command**

Inside the camera (or on the display) is a light meter. It reads the scene and suggests settings that produce a middle brightness. That suggestion is often useful — and it is only a suggestion. The meter doesn't know you wanted a dark, moody portrait or a bright, airy one. It knows middle gray. In Manual mode, you read the meter, then choose settings that deliver YOUR intent, even if that intent isn't "middle."

**Building an exposure for the look you want**

Here is the decision order most working photographers use. First, the look: how much background blur, how motion appears. That usually means choosing aperture or shutter speed first — whichever drives the look. Second, the remaining exposure dial to suit the light. Third, ISO to balance the whole equation — high enough to expose, low enough to keep noise acceptable. There is no single correct combination; there is your combination, and the photo tells you whether it worked.

**Demonstration: a portrait with blurred background**

For this portrait, the look I want is a softly blurred background. That's an aperture decision, so I choose aperture first — a wide aperture on this lens. Shutter next: the subject is still, so I only need it fast enough for steady hands. The meter now tells me I'm underexposed, so ISO comes up until the meter reads balanced. Notice the order: look first, light second, ISO last.

**Demonstration: a moving subject kept sharp**

Same method, different priority. Here the look is a frozen moment, so shutter speed is chosen first — fast enough for this subject's speed. The aperture follows, as wide as the lens allows to buy back light. ISO balances again. Same three dials, different decision order — because the intent changed.

**When Manual fights back: limits in dim light**

Manual mode has limits, and it's honest to show them. In dim light you may open the aperture no wider than the lens allows and slow the shutter no slower than camera shake allows — and ISO takes the remainder, sometimes climbing high enough to show noise. That isn't failure; that's the exposure triangle balancing itself. Knowing the limits tells you when to add light or change approach.

**Learner practice**

Now you: photograph one subject in Manual mode in two different situations — for example, a portrait with a blurred background, and a moving subject you want sharp. Write down which dial you chose first in each, and why.

**Recap**

The meter is a guide, not a command. Choose the dial that drives the look first, balance with the others, and let ISO finish the job. Manual is useful when you want consistent control — and now you've used it twice, on purpose.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE

Photograph one subject in Manual mode in two situations:

1. A portrait with a blurred background — choose aperture first.
2. A moving subject you want sharp — choose shutter speed first.

For each, write down: which dial you chose first, the final three settings, and whether the result matches the look you intended. If the photo is too dark or too bright, adjust ISO and try again — that adjustment is the lesson working, not a mistake.

If you're using a phone: use its Pro mode if it has one and follow the same decision order; if it doesn't, practice the decision order out loud with the simulator instead.

## 7. RECAP

The meter suggests a middle exposure; your intent may not be middle. Choose the dial that drives the look first — aperture for blur, shutter for motion — balance the light with the next dial, and let ISO finish the job. Manual mode buys consistency, and its limits in dim light are the triangle balancing itself.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK

- Verify the meter display behavior on the exact camera filmed; meter readouts and scales vary by model.
- State the actual aperture, shutter speed, and ISO for every demonstration photo; never imply the values are universal.
- Exposure compensation does not apply in Manual mode — if mentioned, clarify that in Manual the dials themselves are the adjustment.
- Do not present the decision order (look → light → ISO) as the only valid order; it is a dependable habit, not a rule.
- Do not claim Manual mode is required or superior; it is one tool, chosen when consistency matters.
- If grain appears in the dim-light demo, describe it accurately as visible noise at that ISO on that camera — noise performance varies by model.
