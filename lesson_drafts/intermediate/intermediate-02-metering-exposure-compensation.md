# Intermediate Lesson 2 — Exposure Metering and Exposure Compensation

Class: Intermediate
Prerequisite: Beginner lessons on exposure and shooting modes

## 1. LEARNING OUTCOME

By the end of this lesson, learners can interpret the camera's exposure meter and correct an exposure that looks too bright or too dark — using exposure compensation in automatic and semi-automatic modes.

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT

**Welcome**

The camera's meter is a fast, competent guesser. In automatic and semi-automatic modes it works constantly to expose your photo at a middle brightness. Most of the time that guess is excellent. This lesson is about the times it isn't — and the polite way to correct it.

**How the meter thinks: everything wants to be middle**

A reflected-light meter reads the brightness coming into the camera and recommends settings that render the scene at a middle tone. Not white, not black — middle. It has no way to know whether a very bright scene is a snowfield at noon or a badly overexposed living room. It aims for middle and lets you decide if that's right.

**When the guess goes wrong: bright scenes and dark scenes**

Photograph a snow scene on Auto and the camera, trying to pull all that brightness toward middle, will often underexpose it — the snow turns gray. Photograph a dark subject on a black background and the meter does the opposite: it lifts the exposure until the darkness turns muddy gray. In both cases the camera did exactly what it was designed to do, and the result is still wrong. The meter measures; it cannot know what it's measuring.

**Exposure compensation: overriding politely**

Exposure compensation — the little +/- scale — tells the camera to shift its recommendation: brighter or darker than its middle-brightness guess, by stops. Positive compensation brightens; negative darkens. It works in automatic and semi-automatic modes, where the camera still drives the dials. In full Manual, there's nothing to compensate — you simply change the dials themselves.

**Demonstration: light subject on dark, dark subject on light**

First scene: something light — white fabric — against a dark background. Auto exposure will tend to brighten the whole frame and wash the subject. Add negative compensation and the whites return to white. Second scene: something dark against a bright background. The camera brightens the dark subject into haze; positive compensation deepens it back. In both cases, keep everything constant except the compensation, and compare.

**Where the control lives**

On many cameras this is a dedicated +/- dial or button near the shutter; on others it lives in a menu or quick screen. Its range varies too — some cameras offer two or three stops in each direction, others five. Find it on your camera before you need it; the moment you need it is the moment the scene is already changing.

**Learner practice**

Photograph a light subject against a dark background, and a dark subject against a light background. In each, start with plain auto exposure, then apply compensation in both directions and compare. The histogram is your referee — we'll see it in the simulator drill.

**Recap**

The meter aims for middle; bright and dark scenes push that aim wrong. Exposure compensation shifts the camera's recommendation without taking over the dials. Learn where the control lives on your camera, and let the image — or the histogram — tell you when to use it.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE

Photograph two scenes:

1. A light subject against a dark background.
2. A dark subject against a light background.

For each: take one frame on plain auto exposure, then apply compensation in both directions (a frame brighter, a frame darker) and compare. Note the compensation values you preferred and why. If your camera offers a histogram, use it to confirm which version put the tones where you wanted.

If you're using a phone: many phone cameras let you drag an exposure slider after tapping to focus — that is exposure compensation in casual clothes. Practice pushing it both directions on a bright-window scene.

## 7. RECAP

The meter aims every scene toward middle brightness, which is wrong for genuinely bright and genuinely dark subjects. Exposure compensation shifts the camera's recommendation in semi-automatic modes — brighter or darker, in stops. Find the control before you need it, and let the image or histogram referee.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK

- Exposure compensation applies in automatic and semi-automatic modes; in full Manual it does not apply — clarify this on camera if both modes are shown.
- Compensation ranges vary by camera (±2, ±3, ±5 stops are all common); state the range of the camera filmed.
- Metering modes (evaluative/matrix, center-weighted, spot) change HOW the meter reads the scene; if mentioned, present them as options that vary by brand and model, not as a universal system.
- Use genuinely bright and genuinely dark scenes for the demonstrations; a mild scene will not show the effect honestly.
- Do not claim the meter is ever "wrong" as a mechanism — it is doing what reflected metering does; the mismatch is between middle-gray intent and photographic intent.
- Verify every compensation value stated against the camera's actual display.
