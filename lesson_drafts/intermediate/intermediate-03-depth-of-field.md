## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT

**Welcome**

In the Beginner class, aperture was introduced as the blur dial. That was true and incomplete. Depth of field — the zone of acceptably sharp focus around your focus point — responds to four factors, and aperture is only one of them. This lesson turns "the background got blurry" into "I know why, and I can predict it."

**The four factors**

One: aperture — wider openings thin the zone of sharpness. Two: focal length — longer lenses thin it further at the same framing. Three: subject distance — the closer you focus, the thinner the zone becomes; this is why macro photography is so unforgiving. Four: background distance — the farther the background sits behind your subject, the more blurred it appears. Notice only one of these is a dial. The other three are decisions about glass and geometry.

**Aperture: the volume knob**

Aperture is the factor you control most directly, so we treat it as the volume knob for blur. Wide open, the zone of focus is at its thinnest for your setup. Stopped down, it widens. But because the other three factors are in play, the same f-number produces very different depth on different setups — an f/2.8 on a phone's tiny wide lens can keep almost everything sharp, while f/2.8 on a long lens up close can melt a background away.

**Focal length: the second factor**

At the same subject distance and framing, longer focal lengths produce thinner depth of field. This is one reason portrait photographers reach for longer lenses: it isn't only compression — the depth itself thins.

**Distance: where you stand, where the background sits**

Focus closer and the sharp zone shrinks around your subject. And the background's distance behind the subject governs how visible the blur is — a background ten meters behind a subject at two meters renders softer than one just a step behind. This is why moving your feet, or moving your subject, changes blur without touching a single dial.

**Demonstration: predict first, photograph second**

Here's a subject with a distant background. Before each photograph, I'll say the four factors out loud and predict: thin zone or deep? First frame: wide aperture, close, long lens — prediction, razor-thin. Second: same framing achieved by stepping back and zooming less, aperture stopped down — prediction, much deeper. The photographs confirm or correct the predictions. Prediction, then photograph — that is the skill.

**Learner practice**

Photograph the same subject with a soft background, then with more of the scene in focus. Then write down which of the four factors you changed to create each result. There is more than one route to each look.

**Recap**

Depth of field responds to four factors — aperture, focal length, subject distance, and background distance. Aperture is the only one on a dial. Predict the zone before you shoot, then let the photograph grade your prediction.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE

Photograph the same subject twice:

1. A soft-background version — use whichever factors get you there: wide aperture, longer lens, closer position, or a farther background.
2. A deep-focus version — stop down, widen the lens, step back, or bring the background closer.

Write down which of the four factors you changed for each version. Repeat with a different subject and try to reach the same two looks using a DIFFERENT factor than before — that is the prediction skill forming.

If you're using a phone: most phones simulate background blur in portrait mode. Compare its result with a real camera's optical blur, and note where the simulation gets the edges wrong — it teaches what optical depth of field actually is.

## 7. RECAP

Depth of field responds to four factors: aperture, focal length, subject distance, and background distance — and only the first is a dial. Wide, long, close, and far-background thins the zone; the opposites deepen it. Predict before you photograph, and let the photo grade you.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK

- State the actual aperture, focal length, and approximate distances for every demonstration frame; do not present values as universal.
- "Acceptably sharp" depends on viewing size and distance — clarify that depth of field boundaries are gradual, not hard edges.
- At very small apertures, diffraction can soften the whole image; if mentioned, present it as an advanced caveat, not a reason to avoid stopping down.
- Do not claim the same f-number produces the same depth on cameras with different sensor sizes without qualification — sensor size changes the framing/field-of-view relationship.
- Phone portrait-mode blur is software simulation; do not present it as optical depth of field.
- Verify every prediction-then-result pair actually matches before publishing the demonstration.
