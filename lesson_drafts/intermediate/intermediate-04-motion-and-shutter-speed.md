# Intermediate Lesson 4 — Motion and Shutter Speed

Class: Intermediate
Prerequisite: Beginner lessons on the exposure triangle and focus

## 1. LEARNING OUTCOME

By the end of this lesson, learners can choose a shutter speed to freeze movement, show motion intentionally, or reduce camera shake — and explain why the right value depends on subject speed, direction, distance, and intent.

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT

**Welcome**

In the Beginner class, shutter speed was the time door: faster freezes, slower blurs. Now we treat motion as a creative choice — sometimes you want it stopped, sometimes you want it visible, and sometimes the blur you're fighting isn't the subject's movement at all.

**Three questions: speed, direction, distance**

Before choosing a shutter speed, ask three things about your subject. How fast is it moving? A running dog needs a faster shutter than a strolling one. Which direction? A subject crossing the frame moves across many pixels per instant and needs a faster shutter than one moving toward you. How far away? A distant subject crosses the frame more slowly in pixel terms, buying you stops. Speed, direction, distance — then choose.

**Freezing: fast shutters**

To freeze a walker, think 1/250 and up. Runners and cyclists, 1/500 to 1/1000. Birds in flight and splashing water, 1/1000 and beyond. These are starting points, not laws — the proof is always in the photograph, enlarged.

**Showing motion: slow shutters and panning**

Motion can be the story. A slow shutter turns a waterfall to silk and car headlights into ribbons. Panning is the middle art: track the subject with the camera while using a slower shutter — the subject stays roughly sharp while the background streaks. It takes practice, and failures teach the timing faster than successes.

**Camera shake: the OTHER blur**

Blur isn't always the subject's fault. At slower shutter speeds, YOUR movement — heartbeat, hands, breath — smears the whole frame. A rough hand-holding floor is around 1/60 of a second, and it shifts with focal length: the longer the lens, the more it magnifies your shake. Image stabilization buys several stops below that floor, and so do bracing, exhaling, and solid stances. When a whole frame is blurry, not just the subject — that's shake, and it's about you, not them.

**Demonstration: one subject, four shutter speeds**

One moving subject, one position, four photographs: 1/1000, 1/250, 1/60, 1/15. At 1/1000, frozen. At 1/250, mostly sharp with soft edges. At 1/60, clearly blurred — and at 1/15, motion becomes an abstract streak. Same subject, same camera position; the only change is time. And one shake demonstration: the same still subject handheld at 1/15 — the whole frame soft — then on a solid surface, sharp. Two kinds of blur, two different fixes.

**Learner practice**

Photograph a moving subject twice: once frozen, once showing motion on purpose. Then take one handheld photo at a shutter speed below your shake floor and study what YOUR unsteadiness looks like.

**Recap**

Subject speed, direction, and distance set the freezing shutter speed. Slow shutters show motion on purpose — and panning steers it. Whole-frame blur is camera shake: slower shutter, longer lens, less support. Choose the blur you want; fix the blur you don't.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE

Photograph one moving subject — a person walking, a pet, passing traffic — three ways:

1. Frozen: a shutter speed that stops the motion cleanly.
2. Shown: a slower shutter that keeps the subject recognizable while motion is visible — try panning if the subject repeats a path.
3. Shake study: a handheld frame below your comfortable hand-holding speed — look at how YOUR movement blurs the whole frame.

Write down the shutter speed and what changed in each. If stabilization is available, repeat the shake study with it on, and note the difference.

If you're using a phone: use its Pro mode if it offers shutter control; if not, practice the panning and shake observations with the normal camera — motion lessons still apply even when the dial is hidden.

## 7. RECAP

Freezing depends on subject speed, direction, and distance — faster, crossing, closer all demand more shutter. Slow shutters make motion the story: water, lights, and panning keep a moving subject recognizable inside streaking surroundings. Whole-frame blur is camera shake: your movement, tamed by faster shutters, stabilization, or support. Choose the blur you want; fix the blur you don't.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK

- State the actual shutter speed for every photograph shown; do not present the numbers in the narration as universal thresholds.
- The hand-holding floor varies with focal length, stabilization, and the photographer — present rough values as starting points, not rules.
- Image stabilization systems differ (in-body, in-lens, electronic); describe only the system actually demonstrated.
- Panning results vary widely with practice; show honest attempts, including imperfect ones if useful.
- Do not claim phone digital zoom or multi-lens switching behaves like changing a camera's shutter-speed reach — verify any phone example against its actual behavior.
- If a tripod or support is used in any "handheld" demo frame, label it accurately.
