## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT

**Welcome**

The Beginner class gave you three tools — balance, leading lines, the rule of thirds. This lesson moves up a level: from placing the subject to deciding how every element in the frame competes for the viewer's eye. Rules become decisions.

**Visual weight: what pulls the eye**

Every element in a photo carries visual weight — how strongly it pulls attention. Brightness pulls (the eye goes to the brightest area), contrast pulls, sharpness pulls, color pulls, faces pull hardest of all. A small bright patch can outweigh a large dull one. When you compose, you're really arranging weights: decide what should weigh the most, then let nothing else out-pull it accidentally. The classic failure isn't bad placement — it's a stray bright blob or a second face stealing the lead.

**Layers: near, middle, far**

Strong photographs often read in depth: something near the camera, the subject in the middle distance, and a background that completes the scene. Layers give a two-dimensional photograph a feeling of space. Look for a foreground element that leads in, keep the middle distance for the subject, and let the background add context rather than clutter. Sometimes a layer is a frame — branches, a doorway — and sometimes just a line at the bottom of the frame that says "here is where you stand."

**Negative space: the power of emptiness**

Negative space is the area around your subject — the emptiness. It's not wasted frame; it's emphasis. A small subject in a large empty space feels isolated, small, or peaceful. Crowding the frame with the same subject feels intense, tight, urgent. Neither is correct — they are different statements. The discipline of negative space is keeping the emptiness CLEAN: an empty area with a distracting bright spot in it is no longer empty.

**Viewpoint: the dial that isn't on the camera**

Viewpoint — where you physically stand and how high you hold the camera — changes everything: what overlaps what, what the background becomes, how tall or small the subject reads. Most photographers shoot from standing eye height by default. Crouch, climb, step sideways, get behind — each move rewrites the relationships in the frame. If the composition isn't working, move before you adjust anything else.

**Demonstration: one scene, four decisions**

One subject, four frames. First, the default: standing height, subject centered — honest, and uninspired. Second: three steps left, so a dark doorway becomes negative space and the subject sits against clean emptiness. Third: crouched low, so a near bench edge becomes a foreground layer and the subject gains depth. Fourth: tight, from farther back with a longer lens, compressing the layers. Same scene — four decisions, four different photographs.

**Learner practice**

Three images of one subject: one built in layers, one using negative space deliberately, one using balance in an unusual arrangement. Then look at all three and name what pulls the eye first in each.

**Recap**

Visual weight, layers, negative space, viewpoint — four decisions that turn arrangement into intention. Move before you adjust. Decide what the eye should find, then let nothing out-pull it by accident.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE

Photograph one subject three ways:

1. Layers: include a near element, the subject in the middle distance, and a background that adds context.
2. Negative space: place the subject small inside clean emptiness — and defend the emptiness.
3. Unusual balance: arrange the weights in a way you wouldn't normally try, and make it hold together.

Review all three and name what pulls the eye first in each. If something out-pulls your subject, you've found the lesson: re-weigh or exclude it.

If you're using a phone: composition is entirely viewpoint and timing — everything in this lesson applies unchanged.

## 7. RECAP

Brightness, contrast, sharpness, and faces carry visual weight — arrange it deliberately. Layers give a flat frame depth: near, subject, far. Negative space is emphasis, as long as it stays clean. And viewpoint is the free dial: move before you adjust.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK

- Eye-path claims (where the viewer "looks first") are well-supported tendencies, not laboratory facts; phrase them as what photographers observe, not as measured certainties.
- The demonstration set must show the SAME scene across all four frames; verify nothing else changed between frames.
- Annotated labels (near/middle/far, weight arrows) must match what the image actually shows.
- Do not present layers or negative space as mandatory for strong photos — the lesson's own premise is that they are decisions.
