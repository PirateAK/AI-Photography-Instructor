# Intermediate Lesson 7 — Working with Natural Light

Class: Intermediate
Prerequisite: Beginner lesson on lighting basics

## 1. LEARNING OUTCOME

By the end of this lesson, learners can adapt position, subject placement, and exposure to different natural-light conditions — shade, backlight, and harsh sunlight — and choose the light that suits the mood they want.

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT

**Welcome**

The Beginner class taught you to recognize light direction and softness. Now we work it: using position, placement, and exposure to make natural light do what the photo needs. No extra equipment in this lesson — just observation and movement.

**Open shade: the beginner's best studio**

Open shade is the soft, even light on the shadow side of a building, under a porch roof, or beneath a tree's outer canopy — bright enough to expose, shaded enough to soften. It flatters faces, tames contrast, and renders color cleanly. When a portrait looks harsh in your review, the first fix is rarely a setting: it's finding shade. Watch for the boundary — subject in shade, background in light — which gives depth and separation for free.

**Backlight: the dramatic one, and its meter trap**

Put the sun behind your subject and two things happen: the edges of hair and shoulders glow, and the meter tries to darken your subject to a silhouette. Both can be the picture. For a glowing portrait, expose for the subject — add positive compensation or meter from their face — and let the background go bright. For a silhouette, expose for the background and let the subject fall to shape. The lesson is that the same backlight serves opposite intents, and the meter's suggestion picks a side without asking.

**Harsh sun: the problem, and its four fixes**

Direct midday sun makes crisp shadows, squinting faces, and contrast that overwhelms color. Four fixes, in order of ease. Move the subject into shade. Move yourself, so the sun comes from behind or beside you. Wait — clouds are diffusers, and the hour near sunset is softer and warmer. Or use the harshness on purpose: hard light is graphic and honest, and some subjects wear it well. What you can't fix with dials alone is squinting — that's a position problem.

**Bounce: free light from nearby surfaces**

Light reflects. A pale wall, sidewalk, white shirt, or tabletop throws a soft fill back into shaded areas — and repositioning the subject a step or two can bring that bounce into play at no cost. This is the taste of reflector technique: light can be redirected, not only received. The next lesson formalizes it with actual reflectors and flash.

**Demonstration: one portrait, three lights**

One subject, three frames. In open shade: even, soft, natural. In backlight: rim-glow on the hair, exposure added for the face, background blooming bright. In direct sun: hard-edged shadows and squint — then the fix, rotating the subject until the sun skims from the side, and the shadow side picks up bounce from a pale wall. Watch the exposure shift in each frame — brighter scene, darker exposure — because that's the camera responding to light we changed, not settings we fussed.

**Learner practice**

Photograph one subject under two different natural lights — for example, open shade and backlight. For each, note the light's direction and softness, what you changed to make it work, and which version suits the mood you wanted.

**Recap**

Open shade flatters; backlight dramatizes (and fools the meter); harsh sun squints — fix it with shade, position, patience, or purpose. Bounce is free fill light. The photographer's first exposure tool is a pair of feet.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE

Photograph one subject under two different natural lights — open shade and backlight, or shade and sun:

1. For each, note the light's direction and whether it reads soft or hard.
2. State what you changed to make the light work — subject position, your position, or exposure.
3. Choose which version suits the mood you wanted, and say why in one sentence.

If you're using a phone: everything here applies — light is camera-independent. Expect the phone's auto exposure to fight you in backlight; drag its exposure slider toward the subject, exactly like compensation.

## 7. RECAP

Open shade is the flattering default. Backlight is the dramatist — rim glow or silhouette, decided by where you point the exposure. Harsh sun has four fixes: shade, position, patience, purpose. And bounce from nearby surfaces is free fill. When the light changes, the exposure changes — move first, adjust second.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK

- State the actual exposure adjustments made for each lighting setup; backlight compensation values will differ by scene.
- Do not promise "glowing" backlight results — rim visibility depends on sun angle, hair, background, and exposure.
- Squinting in direct sun is presented as common but not universal; some subjects and eyewear change it.
- Bounce depends on surface color and proximity; a dark or distant wall provides little fill.
- The "four fixes" are presented as options in rough order of ease, not a mandatory sequence.
- Verify that shade is "open" shade — under a roof or deep canopy the light drops too far and the lesson's exposure claims shift.
