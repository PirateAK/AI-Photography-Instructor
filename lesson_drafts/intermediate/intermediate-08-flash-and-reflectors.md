# Intermediate Lesson 8 — Using Flash and Reflectors

Class: Intermediate
Prerequisite: Beginner lessons on lighting and exposure

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT

**Welcome**

So far, light was something you found. This lesson adds the two ways photographers make it: a reflector redirects light that already exists; a flash adds light of its own. Both are simple tools — the skill is in where you put them.

**Reflectors: bouncing what already exists**

A reflector is any surface that throws light back toward the subject — a purpose-built collapsible disc, a sheet of white cardboard, a pale wall. Point it at the shadow side of a subject and the shadows soften and lift. The color of the surface rides along: white is neutral, silver is punchier, gold warms. Reflectors add no light to the scene — they re-route some of what's already falling, so they're strongest when the original light is strong.

**Flash: carrying your own sun**

A flash — the camera's pop-up unit or a small external one — adds light that didn't exist. It frees you from the sun's schedule: balance a dim room, fill harsh shadows, or outshine daylight on a close subject. Modern cameras meter flash during the exposure itself, so the basic workflow is simple: raise the flash, let the camera control its output, and adjust with flash-power compensation if the result is too bright or too flat.

**The three levers: position, distance, size**

Every added light answers to the same three levers. Position: in front flattens, from the side shapes, from above mimics the sun. Distance: closer makes the light bigger relative to the subject — softer and brighter; farther makes it smaller — harder and dimmer. And the inverse-square reality means distance changes brightness fast: twice as far is roughly a quarter of the light. Size: a bare flash is a small, hard source; a diffuser or a bounce off a ceiling makes it effectively larger and softer.

**Demonstration: no-added-light, reflector, flash**

One portrait, three frames. First, as found: strong side light, deep shadow side — honest, and heavy. Second: a reflector opposite the window, lifting the shadow side — the same light, now balanced. Third: flash bounced off a white ceiling — the room lights up softly, the face models naturally, and the hard edges are gone. Same subject, same camera position; only the added light changed.

**Flash power and exposure: what the camera does**

Watch the settings as flash enters: the camera may hold or shorten the shutter — flash duration is so brief that shutter speed mostly affects the background, not the flash exposure. Ambient light sets the room; flash sets the subject. If the flash output is too hot, turn its power down or move it back; if too weak, the reverse. Iterate one variable at a time, exactly like the exposure triangle.

**Learner practice**

Make a portrait using a reflector or flash, then change the light's position and compare. One variable at a time — the comparison is the curriculum.

**Recap**

A reflector redirects existing light; flash adds its own. Position shapes, distance brightens-or-softens fast, and size governs hardness. The room belongs to ambient exposure; the subject belongs to the flash. Move one lever at a time and let the photograph referee.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE

Make a short portrait series with one light-altering tool:

1. Baseline: photograph the subject as found, with the shadow side heavy.
2. Add a reflector (or a white surface) opposite the main light. Shoot. Move the reflector closer, then farther. Shoot each.
3. If flash is available: raise it, shoot, then adjust its power or distance one step and shoot again.

For each change, write down which lever moved — position, distance, or size — and what the light did. Stop when the light serves the photo, not when every setting is "correct."

If you're using a phone: the flash is small and close to the lens — try it and note honestly what it does (flat, harsh, short-range). Then try the reflector experiment: a friend holding white paper can transform a backlit photo.

## 7. RECAP

A reflector re-routes light that already exists; flash brings its own. Every added light answers to three levers — position shapes, distance changes brightness fast, size sets hardness — and the inverse-square law makes distance the strongest. Ambient exposure owns the room; flash owns the subject. Change one lever at a time.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK

- Flash metering behavior varies by camera and mode (TTL systems differ by brand); describe only the system demonstrated.
- Inverse-square falloff is stated as approximate ("roughly a quarter") — present it as the working rule photographers use, with the physical law behind it noted once.
- Pop-up flash results differ substantially from external flashes; do not generalize one to the other.
- Reflector color changes the added light's temperature (white neutral, silver punchy, gold warm) — state the color used in each demo frame.
- Sync-speed limits and high-speed sync exist but are out of scope for this lesson; avoid stating shutter-speed claims that imply sync behavior.
- Verify every stated flash power or compensation change against the equipment's actual display.
