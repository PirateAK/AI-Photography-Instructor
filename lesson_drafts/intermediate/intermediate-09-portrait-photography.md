# Intermediate Lesson 9 — Portrait Photography

Class: Intermediate
Prerequisite: Beginner lessons on focus, composition, lighting, and exposure

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT

**Welcome**

Everything in the Intermediate class converges here. A strong portrait uses light, focus, composition, and exposure — but settings are the smallest part of the craft. The larger parts are planning and talking.

**The plan: subject, location, light**

Before anyone picks up a camera, answer three questions. Who is the subject, and what should the photo say about them? Where will the light come from — open shade is still the reliable default? And what background serves the story: clean and empty, or contextual and busy? Two minutes of planning saves twenty minutes of fixing.

**Directing: comfort before cleverness**

Most portrait problems are comfort problems. People frozen in front of a lens look frozen in the photograph. Give the subject something to do — hold the mug, walk toward you, look over there. Talk constantly, show them a good frame on the back of the camera, and never say "relax" (it never works). The technical goal is focus on the eyes; the human goal is a face that looks like the person on a good day.

**Background and framing: the eyes decide**

Scan the frame edges before you shoot: stray poles and bright blobs go at the edges on purpose or not at all. Focus on the near eye — the one closest to the camera — and keep both eyes at the same plane when possible. Framing from chest-up or with intentional space around the subject both work; what matters is that the framing choice was made, not defaulted.

**Demonstration: a short session**

Here's the whole method in three minutes. Location: open shade beside the building — soft, even, no squinting. Subject placed two steps forward, so the background drops away. I check the frame edges, place the focus on the near eye, shoot, and talk: "Turn toward the window a touch — there. Hold that." A few frames, a quick review together on the camera back, one adjustment — the reflector trick from the last lesson lifting the shadow side — and the session has its keeper. Total fuss: minimal. Total intention: high.

**The recipe habit**

Because portraits repeat, save your decisions. In this app, the Portrait style carries a real recipe — wide aperture priority, the settings and priorities the coach uses to judge portraits. Load it, adjust for the day's light, and your sessions start from experience instead of zero.

**Learner practice**

Make a short portrait series of a willing subject — a friend, family member, or cooperative stranger. Change the background or the light between frames, keep the conversation going, and end by asking the subject which frame looks most like them. Their answer teaches you something no histogram can.

**Recap**

Plan the subject, location, and light. Talk the subject comfortable. Focus the near eye, scan the edges, and use your saved recipe as the starting point. The camera is the smallest part of a portrait.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE

Make a short portrait series of a willing subject:

1. Plan first: shade or window light, a background that helps, two framing ideas.
2. Direct gently — give the subject actions, not poses.
3. Focus the near eye; scan the edges; shoot a handful of frames.
4. Review together and let the subject pick their favorite.
5. Write down what you changed between frames and which change mattered most.

If you're using a phone: portraits are the phone's home turf — the same plan applies, and portrait mode's simulated blur should be checked around hair and glasses edges.

## 7. RECAP

Plan subject, location, and light. Talk the subject comfortable — give actions, not poses. Focus the near eye, scan the frame edges, and start from a saved recipe. The camera is the smallest part of a portrait.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK

- Do not promise specific results from any posing or direction technique; people vary.
- The app's Portrait style recipe is presented as a starting point, not a mandatory setup.
- Focus-on-the-near-eye is the standard convention for close portraits; for environmental portraits, placement may differ — say so if demonstrated.
- Subject consent and comfort: never film or publish a session where the subject was uncomfortable; the practice requires a willing subject.
- Verify every stated setting against the demonstration footage.
