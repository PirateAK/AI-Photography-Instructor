# Mastering the Exposure Triangle Lesson 1 - What is exposure?

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome to MuskegMan Photography, and welcome to mastering the exposure triangle. I'm so glad you're here. Today we are answering the foundational question: What is exposure? 

Let's start with a simple promise. Photography isn't about memorizing complicated math or buying the most expensive camera body in the shop. It's about light. 

Here is your big-picture definition for today. Exposure is simply the total amount of light your camera sensor records while the shutter is open. That's it. When you press that shutter button, a little window opens up, light rushes inside, and your sensor drinks it in. 

Imagine your sensor is a little bucket, and light is rainfall. If you leave the bucket out in a drizzle for two seconds, it's mostly empty. If you leave it out in a hurricane for an hour, it overflows. Photography is just learning how to catch the exact right amount of rain every single time. 

Now, what happens when you get it wrong? We run into two walls: the shadow wall on the left, and the highlight wall on the right. 

Let's look at underexposure first. That's when you let too little light in. Maybe you're shooting indoors with your ISO set to 100 and your shutter speed way up at one-thousandth of a second. Your image looks dark. If you try to brighten those dark areas later on your computer, you'll notice the shadows look muddy, grainy, and eventually turn into pure, flat black. The detail is just gone. 

On the flip side, we have overexposure. That's when you let way too much light in. Say you're out on a bright Alaska snowfield and you leave your shutter open too long. Your image looks blindingly bright. The highlights wash out to pure, featureless white. Clouds lose their fluffy texture, snow looks like blank paper, and no amount of editing software can bring those details back. We call this clipping, and it's the photographer's worst enemy. 

The whole game of exposure is landing your light right between those two walls. You want rich shadows with a bit of detail, glowing highlights with clear textures, and midtones that feel natural to the eye. 

How do we control this? Through three dials on your camera body: aperture, shutter speed, and ISO. Aperture is the size of the opening in your lens. Shutter speed is how long that opening stays uncovered. And ISO is how sensitive your digital sensor is to the light that arrives. 

Throughout this course, we're going to use a priority-first teaching arc. That means you will always choose one creative priority first—like freezing a leaping salmon, blurring the background behind a portrait, or shooting in dim evening light—and then we'll let the other two legs of the triangle adapt to support it. You never have to guess. 

Let's jump into the camera simulator on your screen right now. I want you to tap your exposure compensation button and dial it up to plus one EV, which means you are intentionally adding one stop of extra brightness. Watch what happens to the graph on your screen, called a histogram. That graph is our visual map of light. When you push to plus one EV, you'll see the graph slide toward the right-hand wall. 

Now tap it down to minus one EV. Watch that same graph slide all the way over to the left-hand wall, crowding the shadows. 

Whenever your graph crashes into either the left or the right wall, that's called clipping. It means data has been crushed into solid black or blown out into pure white, and that detail is gone forever. 

Take a breath. You are already building the intuition of a pro photographer. In our next lessons, we'll break down each of those three dials one by one. For now, open up your simulator and try the practice drill below. You've got this!

## 7. RECAP
- Exposure is simply the total amount of light your camera sensor records while the shutter is open.
- Underexposure creates muddy, grainy shadows that can clip into pure black with lost detail.
- Overexposure blows out highlights into featureless white, clipping away textures permanently.
- The exposure triangle consists of three controls—aperture, shutter speed, and ISO—that work together to land light safely between the two walls.
- The histogram is your visual map of light, showing you instantly if your image is clipping at either edge.
