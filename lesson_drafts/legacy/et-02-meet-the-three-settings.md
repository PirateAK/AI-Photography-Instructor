## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back. Today we put M mode to work—and I promise, by the end of this lesson you'll stop fearing that dial. Last time, we talked about exposure as a simple bucket of light. Today, we look inside the camera and meet the three controls that fill that bucket: shutter speed, aperture, and ISO. 

Think of these three settings as three different doors letting light into your camera. Each one brightens or darkens your photo, but here is the secret most beginners miss: each one also drags along a completely different side effect. Those side effects are actually what photography is all about. 

Let's start with our first door: shutter speed. Shutter speed is simply how long light is collected—it's a time door. On your camera simulator, look for a setting like 1/500th of a second or 1/30th of a second. When you dial your shutter speed to 1/500th, that tiny curtain inside your camera opens and slams shut in the blink of an eye. It lets in a small amount of light, making your image darker. But if you slow that down to 1/30th of a second, the door stays open longer, letting in a flood of light and making your image brighter. 

What's the catch? The side effect? Motion blur. If anything moves while that time door is open—a running kid, a splashing wave, or even the slight tremor of your hands—it smears across the sensor. So, we use fast shutter speeds like 1/1000s to freeze action, and slow shutter speeds like 1/4s to blur waterfalls. Shutter speed rules motion.

Now, let's look at our second door: aperture. Aperture is how wide the light funnel opens inside your lens—it's a size door, measured in f-stops. You'll see numbers like f/2.8 or f/11. Now, this trips up every beginner: a small number like f/2.8 means a huge opening, letting in a ton of light and brightening your photo. A big number like f/11 means a tiny pinhole opening, letting in very little light and darkening your photo. 

What's the catch here? Depth of field—how much of your scene is in sharp focus. An open door at f/2.8 gives you that gorgeous, dreamy, blurry background behind your portrait subject. A closed-down door at f/11 keeps everything sharp from the flowers at your feet to the mountains on the horizon. Aperture rules focus and background blur.

That brings us to our third setting: ISO. ISO isn't a physical door letting light in; instead, ISO sets how sensitive your digital sensor is to whatever light actually hits it. You'll see values like ISO 100 for bright sunny days, and ISO 6400 for dark indoor rooms. When you bump your ISO from 100 up to 6400, you are electronically boosting the signal. Your photo gets much brighter, fast. 

So what’s the price of admission? Digital grain, sometimes called noise. High ISO photos look speckled, rough, and lose fine color detail, like an old newspaper photograph. ISO is your safety net when there simply isn't enough light in the room, but you want to keep it as low as possible.

Here is why this matters. All three settings change brightness. If your photo is too dark, you can slow your shutter, open your aperture, or raise your ISO. Any of the three will brighten the shot. But because each one creates a totally different side effect—motion blur, background blur, or grain—your creative goal always decides which dial you touch first. That is the priority-first rule, and mastering it changes everything.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Open your camera simulator and load the default indoor scene. Your challenge is to brighten the exact same dark photo three different ways. First, keep your aperture and ISO locked, and brighten the photo solely by slowing down your shutter speed. Notice the motion blur. Second, reset, keep your shutter and ISO locked, and brighten the photo solely by opening your aperture. Notice the background blur change. Third, reset, keep your shutter and aperture locked, and brighten the photo solely by raising your ISO. Notice the grain appear. As you do this, name the side effect out loud every single time. That habit of naming the side effect is the key to mastering exposure.

## 7. RECAP
- Shutter speed controls the duration of light collection and dictates motion blur.
- Aperture controls the physical size of the lens opening and dictates depth of field.
- ISO controls sensor sensitivity to light and introduces digital grain when raised too high.
- Multiple combinations of these three settings can yield the exact same overall brightness.
- Your creative priority—freezing action, blurring backgrounds, or shooting in low light—always dictates which setting you adjust first.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
- **Question:** If you change your aperture from f/4 to f/16, are you letting more light in or less light in?
  **Answer:** Less light. f/16 is a much smaller physical opening than f/4, which darkens the image.
- **Question:** What is the primary creative side effect of lowering your shutter speed from 1/1000s to 1/2s?
  **Answer:** Motion blur. Keeping the shutter open longer allows any movement in the frame or camera shake to blur across the sensor.
- **Question:** Why wouldn't you just leave your ISO set to 6400 all the time so your photos are always bright?
  **Answer:** Because high ISO values introduce digital grain and noise, reducing overall image clarity and color fidelity.
