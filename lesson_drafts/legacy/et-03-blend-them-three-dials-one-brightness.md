## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back. Today we put Manual mode to work—and I promise, by the end of this lesson, you'll stop fearing those three dials and start seeing them as a team. 

In our last lessons, we looked at aperture, shutter speed, and ISO on their own. We learned that aperture controls your background blur, shutter speed freezes or blurs motion, and ISO brightens your image digitally when light runs low. But here is the secret every pro uses: you don't change all three at random. You trade them. 

Think of exposure like a bucket of water. Your final brightness is the water level. It doesn't matter if you pour it in with a big pitcher or a small cup; the total water in the bucket stays the same. Every time you adjust one dial by one full stop—remember, a stop is just a doubling or halving of light—you change the water level. But you can instantly balance it out by adjusting another dial in the opposite direction. 

Let's look at a concrete example. Imagine you are out shooting a landscape. You set your aperture to f/16 to get everything sharp from the flowers in front of your boots to the mountains in the back. But because f/16 is a tiny pinhole letting in very little light, your camera's light meter tells you your shutter speed has dropped to one-thirtieth of a second ($1/30$). 

Now, here is our priority-first rule in action. You always pick your creative priority first. For this landscape, your priority is deep depth of field—everything in focus. So f/16 stays. But $1/30$ is well into handheld camera shake territory. If you press the shutter, your photo is going to be blurry. 

So how do we fix the blur without ruining the brightness and without losing our deep focus? We trade! 

We look at our shutter speed. We need it faster to stop our hand tremors. Let's jump our shutter speed from $1/30$ up to $1/125$. That is two full steps faster. Let's count them: from $1/30$ to $1/60$ is one stop, and from $1/60$ to $1/125$ is the second stop. By speeding up the shutter, we just cut our incoming light down to one-quarter of what it was. Our bucket is emptying out. Our photo is going to be two stops dark. 

So, where do we take that light back? Since our aperture is locked at f/16 for our deep focus, we have one dial left: ISO. 

Right now, let's say we are sitting at ISO 100. We need to boost our light back up by two stops to compensate for our faster shutter. One stop of ISO brightness takes us from 100 to 200. The second stop takes us from 200 to 400. 

Let's check our math on the simulator screen here. We started at f/16, $1/30$, and ISO 100. We traded to f/16, $1/125$, and ISO 400. The overall brightness of the scene on your screen hasn't changed by even a fraction of a stop. But look at what we achieved: our image is now sharp, free of hand-shake blur, and our deep depth of field is completely intact. 

This is the beauty of the exposure triangle. It is not a math test. It is a series of creative exchanges. You give up a little bit of light on one dial—like using a faster shutter or a narrower aperture—and you take it right back on another. 

When you pick up your camera today, don't spin all three dials blindly. Ask yourself: what is my priority? Is it freezing that running dog? Is it blurring that distracting background? Or is it keeping my ISO clean in a dim room? Lock that first dial down. Then, let the other two adapt to keep your brightness right where you want it. You've got this. Let's jump into the practice drill and make this second nature.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
- **Drill Name:** The Shake-Saver Swap
- **Instructions:** Open your camera simulator or your physical camera in Manual (M) mode. Set your lens aperture to f/16 and your shutter speed to $1/30$ of a second at ISO 100. Notice how the image is bright, but your shutter is dangerously slow for handheld shooting. Now, open your shutter speed two full stops up to $1/125$ of a second to eliminate hand shake. Your image will go dark. Finally, bring your brightness back by raising your ISO two full stops up to ISO 400 (remembering the steps: 100 to 200, 200 to 400). Verify on your exposure meter that the indicator sits dead-center at 0. You've just successfully blended the triangle!

## 7. RECAP
- Exposure is a zero-sum game: changing one dial requires an offsetting adjustment on another to keep brightness constant.
- Always establish your creative priority first (depth, motion, or low light) and lock that dial in.
- Every time you speed up your shutter by two stops, you can recover that light by boosting your ISO by two stops.
- Trading stops lets you eliminate camera shake or control depth of field without ruining your exposure.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
- **Question:** If you change your shutter speed from $1/60$ to $1/250$ (two stops faster), how many stops of light have you lost?
  - **Answer:** You have lost two stops of light because the shutter is open for a quarter of the time.
- **Question:** If you lose two stops of light by speeding up your shutter, how do you compensate using ISO to maintain the same brightness?
  - **Answer:** You increase your ISO by two stops (for example, moving from ISO 100 to 200, then 200 to 400).
- **Question:** When using the priority-first rule, what should you do before adjusting your secondary and tertiary dials?
  - **Answer:** You must lock in your primary creative choice—whether that is your aperture for depth of field or your shutter speed for motion freeze.
