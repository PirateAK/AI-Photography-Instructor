# Mastering the Exposure Triangle Lesson 4 - Pick your priority first

## 1. LEARNING OUTCOME
By the end of this lesson, the student can evaluate a photographic subject, select the single non-negotiable exposure priority—shutter speed, aperture, or ISO—and lock it in before managing the remaining two settings.

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back. Today we put M mode to work—and I promise, by the end of this lesson you'll stop fearing that dial. Exposure is a triangle of three unknowns, and if you try to adjust shutter speed, aperture, and ISO all at once, your head is going to spin. So here is the secret workflow: decide which one setting is non-negotiable before you touch anything else. One dial gets picked first, and the other two become simple bookkeeping. Your subject picks the priority. Let's look at how that works in the real world. 

First up: motion. When your subject is moving, shutter speed is your absolute priority. Shutter speed is simply how long your camera's curtain stays open to let light in. If you are shooting a sprinting dog, a soaring eagle, or a kid running across the backyard at a soccer game, a slow shutter speed like one-thirtieth of a second will give you a blurry mess. Motion smear everywhere. So you ignore aperture and ISO for a second, look at your top dial, and snap your shutter speed to one-five-hundredth of a second first. You've locked your creative priority. Now the other two legs of the triangle adapt. You open up your aperture—say, to f/4—to let in more light, and you bump your ISO to 800 so the camera sensor is sensitive enough to make the image bright. But the shutter speed stays locked at one-five-hundredth because freezing that action was your non-negotiable rule.

Second scenario: depth. What if your subject is standing still, but you want that gorgeous, blurry background that professional portraits have? That is called shallow depth of field, and aperture is your priority. Aperture is the physical opening inside your lens that lets light pass through. A wide aperture, like f/2.8, lets tons of light in and blurs everything behind your subject. So you pick f/2.8 first. You lock it in. Now your shutter speed and ISO are just bookkeeping. You check your light meter, set your shutter speed to one-hundred-and-twentieth of a second so it's steady in your hands, and adjust your ISO to 400 to balance the exposure. You didn't guess. You let the subject dictate the aperture, and the rest fell into place.

Third scenario: dim light. Imagine you are walking through a dimly lit indoor museum or an evening street market. You don't have a tripod. Your hands shake naturally, and if your shutter speed drops below one-sixtieth of a second, your whole photo will blur from camera shake. That shake floor is your constraint. You set your shutter to one-sixtieth to keep it sharp, and you open your lens as wide as it goes to f/4. But your exposure meter is still telling you the scene is way too dark. That is when ISO—the digital amplification of your camera's sensor—steps in to fill the gap. You roll your ISO dial up to 3200. Is there a little digital grain? Sure. But your shot is sharp, your exposure is bright, and you got the memory. 

Here is your takeaway for today: stop tweaking all three dials at random. Look at your scene. Ask yourself, "What is the most important thing here? The motion, the blur behind the subject, or the lack of light?" Pick that priority first. Lock it. Then let the other two dials do the math. You've got this. Let's jump into the simulator and try it out.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Open your camera simulator to the running dog scene set at 1/30s where motion smear is everywhere. Because shutter speed is your priority here, snap it immediately to 1/250s or faster. Observe how dark the image becomes, then fix the brightness by opening your aperture, and finally let ISO absorb whatever light is left to achieve a sharp, properly exposed action shot.

## 7. RECAP
- Exposure has three variables, so trying to adjust them all at once leads to frustration.
- Let your subject dictate your priority: moving subjects need shutter speed first.
- Subjects that need background separation need aperture first.
- Low-light handheld situations require you to manage your shake floor first and let ISO fill the gap.
- Once you pick your priority, the remaining two settings become simple bookkeeping.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
1. **Question:** If you are photographing a fast-moving bicycle race, which leg of the triangle should you set first?
   **Answer:** Shutter speed, to ensure you have a fast enough speed (like 1/500s or 1/1000s) to freeze the motion.
2. **Question:** What is aperture responsible for besides controlling the amount of light?
   **Answer:** Controlling depth of field, which determines how much of the background is sharp or blurred.
3. **Question:** In a dim low-light situation where you are handholding your camera, what is the primary danger of dropping your shutter speed too low?
   **Answer:** Camera shake blur, caused by natural hand movements while the sensor is exposed to light.
