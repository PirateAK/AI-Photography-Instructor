## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to MuskegMan Photography. Today we are tackling the second leg of our exposure triangle: shutter speed. Every single photo of a moving subject is a slice of time. The faster your subject moves, the thinner the slice you need to stop the action and capture a sharp moment. If your slice is too thick, motion smear happens. Let's fix that right now.

Think of your shutter speed as a set of mechanical curtains inside your camera body. When you press the shutter button, those curtains fling open and snap shut, letting light hit your sensor for a measured fraction of a second. We measure shutter speed in seconds and fractions of a second, from 1/4000th all the way down to 30 seconds or more. 

Let's look at some real numbers for freezing action. Suppose you are out photographing wildlife. A bird in flight moves incredibly fast. To freeze its wingtips without any blur, you need a very thin slice of time—something like 1/1000th of a second or even 1/2000th of a second. If you are shooting a human runner sprinting past you, 1/250th of a second usually holds them nicely. For someone just taking a casual stroll down the sidewalk, 1/60th of a second will do the trick. 

Notice what happens as we climb up this ladder of speeds. Every time you jump from 1/125 to 1/250, you are cutting the amount of light entering your camera in half. You are making your slice of time thinner, which means less light gets in. Keep that trade-off in mind, because we always have to pay back the light we lose.

Now, what happens when we drop below 1/60th of a second? This brings us straight to a critical landmark in photography: the shake floor. Below about 1/60th of a second, your own natural hand tremors join the motion. Even if you try your absolute best to stand completely still, your heartbeat and breathing will cause micro-shakes that turn a crisp photo into a blurry mess. 

There is a classic rule of thumb to help you avoid this hand-held shake floor. It is called the reciprocal rule. Your slowest safe shutter speed is roughly the reciprocal of your lens focal length. If you are shooting with a standard 50mm lens, your hand-held shake floor is about 1/50th of a second. If you zoom out to a heavier 200mm telephoto lens, your shake floor jumps up to 1/200th of a second because every tiny twitch is magnified. 

Thankfully, modern technology gives us a safety net. Image stabilization—often called vibration reduction or IS—buys you roughly four stops of protection below your shake floor. That means if your shake floor with a 50mm lens is normally 1/50th of a second, good image stabilization can let you drop all the way down to about 1/3rd of a second while still holding the camera in your hands and keeping your static background sharp. That is a massive superpower in low-light situations.

But what if you want blur on purpose? What if you do not want to freeze time, but rather show the passage of time? That means taking a thick slice. 

Imagine you are standing beside a rushing river in the Alaskan backcountry. If you shoot at 1/1000th of a second, every single water droplet looks frozen like shards of glass. But if you mount your camera on a sturdy tripod, drop your shutter speed down to 1 full second, and let the water flow past your sensor, something magical happens. The rushing water turns into smooth, misty, silky white ribbons. You took a thick slice of time, and the motion blended together into art. 

The same principle applies at night in the city. If you leave your shutter open for 5 or 10 seconds on a tripod, the cars driving down the highway turn into bright, glowing streaks of red and white light, while the buildings stay pin-sharp. 

Let's tie this back to our priority-first teaching arc. In our last lesson, we focused entirely on aperture for depth of field. Today, shutter speed takes the wheel. If your absolute priority for a shot is freezing a speeding bicycle or blurring a waterfall, you lock in your shutter speed first. Then, your aperture and ISO adapt to handle the light requirements. 

You are no longer guessing. You are choosing how time behaves inside your frame. Grab your camera, and let us put this into practice.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Walk your camera's shutter speed through a sequence from 1/30 up to 1/1000 while photographing a moving subject (like a pet walking, a ceiling fan spinning, or water pouring from a tap). Watch the motion smear shrink with every step—you are actively thinning the slice of time. Find the exact stop where your subject freezes completely, then notice how much darker your image became and write down how you would pay that light back using aperture or ISO.

## 7. RECAP
- Shutter speed controls how long light hits your sensor, acting as a slice of time that either freezes or blurs motion.
- Fast speeds like 1/1000 freeze birds and sports; slow speeds like 1/60 or slower capture casual movement or intentional blur.
- The hand-holding shake floor sits right around 1/60th of a second for standard lenses due to natural body tremors.
- Image stabilization acts as a safety net, buying you roughly four stops below your shake floor for handheld shots.
- Intentional slow shutter speeds (using a tripod) turn rushing waterfalls into silky ribbons and city traffic into light trails.
