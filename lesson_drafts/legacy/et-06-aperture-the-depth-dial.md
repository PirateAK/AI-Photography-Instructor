## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to MuskegMan Photography's AI Photography Instructor. Today we are tackling the aperture, which I like to call the depth dial. Aperture does two big jobs at once, and once you see how they connect, your creative control jumps overnight. 

Let's start with the light job. Think of your aperture as the iris of your eye. It is a mechanical circle of blades inside your lens that opens and closes to let light reach the camera sensor. If you open it wide, a flood of light pours in. If you close it down to a tiny pinhole, just a trickle gets through. 

Here is the famous beginner trap that trips everyone up on day one: the numbering runs backwards. F-stops count how far the aperture is CLOSED, not how big the physical hole is. So an f-number like f/2 means the aperture is barely closed at all—you have a giant opening, lots of light, and a fast exposure. Meanwhile, an f-number like f/16 means the blades are nearly shut, leaving a tiny pinhole that chokes off the light. A smaller f-number gives you a bigger hole and lets more light in. Say that to yourself a few times until it sticks: small number, big hole.

Now let's talk about the second job: the story job. This is depth of field—the zone of sharpness around your focus point. Change your aperture, and you move the thickness of that zone. If you shoot wide open at f/2, you get a very thin, razor-thin zone of sharpness. Your subject's eyes might be tack-sharp, but everything three inches behind them melts into a creamy, soft blur. That is gorgeous for portraits. 

If you stop down to f/16, that zone gets deep. Everything from the wildflowers at your feet to the mountain peak on the horizon stays sharp. That is the go-to setting for big landscapes. 

Here is the second trap beginners miss: perfect focus does not mean sharp. A subject sitting outside your depth of field lands soft and blurry, no matter how precisely you focused on them. Your focus point picks *where* the zone sits in your scene, but your aperture picks how *thick* that zone is. 

Let’s see this live in the camera simulator. Pull up your simulator screen now. We are shooting a portrait of a local Alaskan sled dog. Right now, our camera is set to ISO 400, shutter speed is 1/250th of a second, and our aperture is cranked all the way down to f/16. Look at the background—you can see every pine needle, every detail of the doghouse behind our husky. 

Now, let's step that aperture wider. We are going to roll our dial from f/16 down to f/5.6, then f/2.8, and finally all the way to f/2. Watch two things move together on your screen: first, the background blur grows into a gorgeous, smooth wash of color. Second, the whole image brightens up dramatically because that bigger hole is letting a flood of extra light onto the sensor. 

Remember our priority-first teaching arc from earlier in this course? You pick your creative priority first, and the other settings adapt. If your priority is a blurry background, you choose f/2. But look what happens to our exposure—it is way too bright now, washed out in the bright midday sun. 

So how do we fix it? We pay the light back with our shutter. We leave our aperture wide open at f/2 for that creamy background blur, and we turn our shutter speed dial faster, moving from 1/250th up to 1/2000th of a second. Watch the simulator: the brightness returns to normal, the exposure balances out, but that dreamy background blur stays right where we want it. 

That is the depth dial working independently from your exposure. You used aperture for your story, and you used shutter speed to balance your light. Take a breath, experiment with those numbers in the simulator today, and I will see you in the final lesson.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Open your camera simulator and load the practice scene with the sled dog subject. Set your ISO to 400 and your shutter speed to 1/250. Step your aperture from f/16 all the way down to your lens's widest stop (like f/2 or f/2.8) and watch two things move together: the background blur grows AND the image brightens up. Now, pay that extra light back by speeding up your shutter until the brightness returns to normal, while your background blur stays creamy and isolated.

## 7. RECAP
- Aperture controls two things at once: the amount of light entering the camera and your depth of field (the zone of sharpness).
- F-numbers run backwards: smaller numbers (like f/2) mean a bigger physical opening, letting in more light and creating a thinner zone of sharpness.
- Focus picks *where* the sharp zone sits in your scene, but aperture picks *how thick* that zone is.
- When you open your aperture for a blurry background, your image gets brighter—so you must pay the light back using a faster shutter speed.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
1. **Question:** Does an f/2 setting let in more or less light than an f/16 setting?
   *Answer:* More light, because f/2 represents a much larger physical opening (a smaller f-number means a bigger hole).
2. **Question:** If your subject's nose is in focus, but their ears are blurry, did you use a wide aperture like f/2 or a narrow aperture like f/16?
   *Answer:* A wide aperture like f/2, which creates a very thin zone of sharpness (shallow depth of field).
3. **Question:** If you change your aperture from f/16 to f/2.8 to get a blurry background, what happens to your exposure brightness and how do you fix it?
   *Answer:* The image becomes much brighter because more light is entering. You fix it by paying the light back with a faster shutter speed (e.g., speeding it up from 1/250s to 1/2000s).
