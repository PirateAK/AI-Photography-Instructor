# Introduction to Digital Photography Lesson 2 - Composition: giving the eye a path

## 1. LEARNING OUTCOME
By the end of this lesson, the student can deliberately arrange visual elements in the frame using the rule of thirds, leading lines, and subject separation to guide the viewer's eye.

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to MuskegMan Photography's AI Photography Instructor. Today, we are moving past simply pointing the camera at a cool thing and pressing the shutter button. We are going to talk about composition. Composition is simply arranging the elements inside your frame so the viewer looks exactly where you want them to look. 

Have you ever taken a photo of a stunning mountain, looked at it later, and felt like it looked a bit boring or flat? That happens because the viewer's eye wanders all over the frame without a clear place to land. Today, we fix that. We are going to give the eye a path. 

Let's start with our first tool: the rule of thirds. Think of your camera screen or viewfinder like a tic-tac-toe board. You have two vertical lines and two horizontal lines dividing your frame into nine equal rectangles. The rule of thirds says that if you place your main subject on one of those intersecting points—rather than dead center—your photo immediately feels more dynamic and natural. 

Let's look at the camera simulator on screen. Right now, I have a landscape shot of an Alaskan lake. If I place the horizon line right across the middle of the frame, the photo is cut in half. Is it the sky we care about, or the water? Let's fix that. I will move the horizon up to the top third line, giving two-thirds of the frame to the stunning water reflection in the foreground. Or, if the sky is doing something incredible with sunset colors, I will drop the horizon down to the bottom third line, giving two-thirds to the sky. 

Now, let's talk about placing our subject horizontally. Say I am photographing a moose standing by the tree line. If I put the moose right in the dead center, the photo feels static like a passport photo. Instead, I will slide the moose over to the left vertical third line, facing toward the right side of the frame. That leaves empty negative space in front of the moose, giving it room to look into the frame. Our eyes naturally follow the subject's gaze across that open space. 

Next up: leading lines. Leading lines are natural or man-made lines in your scene that pull the viewer's eye from the edge of the frame directly toward your subject. Think of a wooden boardwalk winding through a marsh, a gravel road cutting through the forest, or even a fallen log pointing toward a patch of wildflowers. 

Let's switch to the simulator to see a leading line in action. Here we have a shot of a cabin nestled in the trees. Right now, it's a bit messy. But watch what happens when I step to the left and use the wooden fence line in the foreground. The fence rails start at the bottom left corner of our frame and angle diagonally upward, pointing right at the front door of the cabin. When you look at this photo, your eye doesn't wander. It hits that fence and zips straight to the cabin like a car on a highway. That is the power of a leading line. Look for lines in your environment—fences, rivers, pathways, even shadows—and use your feet to position yourself so those lines draw the eye where you want it to go.

Our final composition tool for today is clean edges and subject separation. Have you ever taken a portrait of a friend, only to realize later that a tree branch looks like it's growing right out of their head? Or maybe the background is so busy and colorful that your subject completely disappears into the noise. That is a lack of subject separation. 

Cleaning your edges means taking a slow, deliberate second to scan the four outer borders of your viewfinder before you press the shutter button. Is there a stray bright wrapper in the bottom left corner? Is a tree branch poking into the top right? Step to the side, crouch down slightly, or zoom in just a bit to crop those distractions right out of the camera. 

Now, what about a cluttered background? Let's say you are shooting a portrait outdoors, and behind your subject is a messy tangle of bushes, parked cars, and bright signs. You can't move the cars, but you can control your lens. This is where your aperture comes to the rescue. Aperture is the size of the opening inside your lens that lets light in, measured in f-stops. A smaller number like f/2.8 means a wide-open aperture, which creates a very shallow depth of field. Depth of field is simply the zone of acceptable sharpness in your front-to-back space. 

Let's dial our camera simulator from f/8 to f/2.8. Look at what happens to that messy background. It melts away into a soft, creamy blur of color. Your subject remains tack-sharp at f/2.8, but the distracting clutter behind them simply vanishes. By opening up your aperture, you are using depth of field to isolate your subject and clean the background for you, without needing to clean up the physical world. 

Composition isn't a rigid rulebook; it's a toolbox. When you combine the rule of thirds for placement, leading lines for direction, and a wide aperture for clean separation, you stop hoping for a good photo and start creating one. Take your time, look at the edges of your frame, and give your viewer's eye a path to follow. I will see you in the next lesson.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Find a cluttered outdoor location (like a park with benches, trees, and paths). Take two photos of the same subject: first with the subject dead-center against the busy background at f/8. Then, re-compose using the rule of thirds, align a natural leading line pointing to your subject, and open your aperture to f/2.8 to blur the background. Compare the two shots and notice how depth-of-field isolation and composition instantly clean up the frame.

## 7. RECAP
- Composition is arranging frame elements so the viewer looks where you want.
- The rule of thirds uses an imaginary grid to place subjects on dynamic intersecting points.
- Leading lines use natural pathways like fences or roads to pull the eye straight to your subject.
- Scanning clean edges prevents distracting elements from creeping into the corners of your frame.
- A wide aperture (like f/2.8) creates a shallow depth of field that blurs cluttered backgrounds and isolates your subject.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
- Question: What grid is used in the rule of thirds?
  - Answer: A tic-tac-toe grid dividing the frame into nine equal rectangles with two vertical and two horizontal lines.
- Question: How do leading lines help a photograph?
  - Answer: They pull the viewer's eye from the edge of the frame directly toward the main subject along a natural or man-made path.
- Question: Which camera setting can you use to blur a cluttered background and separate your subject?
  - Answer: A wide aperture (small f-number like f/2.8) which creates a shallow depth of field.
