## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to MuskegMan Photography's AI Photography Instructor. Today, we are moving past the technical mechanics of your camera and diving into the real magic of photography: seeing light. I promise you, once you start paying attention to light, you will never look at an ordinary room or a sunny afternoon the same way again. 

Let's start with a foundational truth that every great photographer lives by. The subject of every photo isn't really the person, the mountain, or the flower. The true subject of every single photograph is the light falling on that object. Light is your paint. Your camera is just the canvas. And today, we are going to learn how to paint with direction, quality, and color.

Let's talk about quality first. Quality refers to how hard or how soft your light is. Hard light creates sharp, distinct, high-contrast shadows with hard edges. Think of the midday sun in July hitting a parking lot. It creates deep black shadows right next to brilliant, blinding highlights. Hard light is dramatic, punchy, and raw. If you're shooting a rugged portrait or want to emphasize texture on an old wooden cabin, hard light is your friend. 

Soft light, on the other hand, creates gentle, gradual transitions between light and shadow. You get soft light on an overcast day when the clouds act like a giant diffusing blanket in the sky, or indoors when light bounces off a white wall. Soft light is flattering, forgiving, and wraps around your subject. If you want to smooth out skin tones or create a peaceful, dreamy mood, you want soft light. The secret to spotting the difference is simple: look at the shadow edge. If the shadow has a sharp, razor-thin edge, that's hard light. If the shadow edge is blurry and gradual, that's soft light.

Now, let's look at light direction. Where the light comes from relative to your subject completely changes the emotional impact of the photo. Let's start with front lighting. That is when the light source is behind you, shining directly onto the front of your subject. Set your camera to ISO 200, an aperture of f/5.6, and a shutter speed of 1/250th of a second, and snap a photo of a friend facing the sun. Notice how front lighting fills in all the shadows and flattens out skin blemishes. It's safe and bright, but it can also look a bit flat and lifeless because it removes dimension.

Next, let's move that light source to the side. Side lighting comes from a 90-degree angle to the left or right of your subject. Keep your exposure settings locked at ISO 200, f/5.6, and 1/250th, but ask your subject to turn sideways. Suddenly, one side of their face is brightly lit while the other side falls into shadow. Side lighting reveals texture, creates dramatic depth, and adds a three-dimensional feel to your two-dimensional sensor. It is one of the most powerful tools in your kit.

Finally, we have backlighting. This is when your light source is directly behind your subject, facing your camera. Backlighting is bold. If you meter strictly for the bright sun behind your subject at ISO 100, f/8, and 1/500th of a second, your subject will turn into a dark, mysterious silhouette. But if you open up your shadows or use exposure compensation to brighten your subject, you get a gorgeous glowing rim of light—a halo effect around their hair and shoulders. Don't fear backlighting; embrace the glow.

Beyond quality and direction, we also need to talk about color. Light has a temperature measured in Kelvin. We call this color temperature or white balance. Warm light, like a campfire or an old incandescent bulb, has a low Kelvin number around 2700K and looks very orange or yellow. Cool light, like an overcast grey sky or heavy shade, has a high Kelvin number around 7000K and looks blue. 

Our eyes are amazing at automatically adjusting to these color shifts, but digital camera sensors need a little help. If you shoot indoors under standard household lamps without adjusting your settings, your photos will look muddy and intensely orange. Indoor bulbs are orange and dim, and our camera simulator right now is mimicking that exact struggle. Let's fix it together. 

Look at your camera screen or our live simulator on the app. Switch your white balance setting from Auto White Balance, or AWB, over to the Tungsten preset, often represented by a little glowing lightbulb icon. Watch what happens. Instantly, that heavy, sickly orange color cast vanishes. The whites turn clean, the skin tones look natural, and the image pops back to life. That is the power of taking manual control over your light's color.

Remember, photography is literally "drawing with light." By paying attention to whether your light is hard or soft, coming from the front, side, or back, and whether it's warm or cool, you take total creative ownership of your imagery. Take a deep breath, experiment with these three pillars of light on your next outing, and I will see you in the final lesson of our series.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Open your camera app or digital camera simulator indoors under standard household light bulbs where the image looks noticeably orange and dim. Navigate to your White Balance menu, select the Tungsten preset (lightbulb icon), and observe how the orange color cast instantly vanishes, restoring natural skin tones and neutral whites. Take three test shots before and after applying the preset to compare the difference.

## 7. RECAP
- The subject of every photograph is the light falling on your subject, not just the physical object itself.
- Hard light creates sharp, high-contrast shadows with crisp edges; soft light creates gradual, flattering shadow transitions.
- Light direction changes the mood: front lighting flattens and brightens, side lighting reveals texture and depth, and backlighting creates dramatic silhouettes and glowing halos.
- Color temperature is measured in Kelvin; warm light is orange, cool light is blue.
- Use the Tungsten white balance preset indoors to cancel out the orange cast of dim household light bulbs.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
- **Question:** How can you quickly tell the difference between hard light and soft light just by looking at a shadow?
  - **Answer:** Look at the transition edge of the shadow. Hard light produces a sharp, crisp shadow edge, while soft light produces a blurry, gradual transition edge.
- **Question:** What is the primary visual effect of side lighting on a subject?
  - **Answer:** Side lighting reveals texture, adds dramatic depth, and gives a three-dimensional feel by casting one side in shadow while highlighting the other.
- **Question:** Which white balance preset should you choose when shooting indoors under warm, dim incandescent household bulbs to eliminate an orange color cast?
  - **Answer:** The Tungsten white balance preset (represented by a lightbulb icon), which cools down the image to counteract the warm orange light.
