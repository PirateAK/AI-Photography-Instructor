## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to our final lesson in introduction to digital photography. Today we are tackling the physical side of shooting: holding and moving the camera. Have you ever looked at a photo on your computer and noticed it looked just a little bit soft, even though you nailed the focus? Don't worry, that happens to every single one of us. Usually, it is not a lens problem. It is camera shake. When you press the shutter button, tiny physical movements can blur your images, especially as light drops. By the end of this lesson, you will know how to lock your body in like a tripod and when to let your ISO rescue your shot instead of risking a blurry frame.

Let's start with your physical stance. Put down the camera for a second and stand up with me. Place your feet shoulder-width apart. This creates a solid foundation, just like the legs of a tripod. Now, bring the camera to your eye. Do not hold it out in front of you like a smartphone screen. Tuck your left hand underneath the lens barrel to support the weight, and pull your right elbow firmly against the side of your ribcage. That contact point against your body is your best friend. It dampens vibration and stops your arms from floating in midair. 

Next up is your breathing. It sounds funny, but breath control changes sharpness. When you are ready to take a picture, take a normal breath in, let half of it out, and pause for just a moment. That pause between inhales and exhales is when your chest is most still. That is the exact window to press the button. 

Speaking of pressing the button, let's talk about the shutter press. A lot of beginners stab at the shutter button like they are trying to swat a fly. That sudden jab bounces the whole camera downward right as the shutter opens. Instead, think of it like a gentle squeeze of a trigger. Rest your index finger on the button, take that half-breath pause, and smoothly depress the button straight down. 

Now, how slow can you let your shutter speed go before your hands ruin the shot? There is a classic rule of thumb called the reciprocal rule. Your shutter speed denominator should match or exceed your lens focal length. If you are shooting with a 50 millimeter lens, your slowest safe handheld shutter speed is one-fiftieth of a second, written as 1/50. If you drop down to 1/15 of a second with that same 50 millimeter lens, tiny tremors in your hands will smear the image across the sensor. 

So what do you do when the sun goes down and 1/15 of a second is all the light gives you? Do you let the image blur? Absolutely not. This is exactly why ISO exists. ISO, your sensor's digital sensitivity, is your safety net. If you are sitting at f/2.8 and 1/15 of a second, and your images are coming out blurry, bump your shutter speed back up to a safe 1/60, and raise your ISO from 400 up to 1600. Sure, you will introduce a little bit of digital noise, but digital noise can be cleaned up in post-processing. Motion blur cannot. A slightly grainy, sharp photo will always beat a smooth, blurry mess.

Let's do a quick recap. Stand wide, tuck your elbows into your ribs, time your shots with your breathing, squeeze the shutter gently, and respect the reciprocal rule. If the light gets too dim for your hands, trade a bit of ISO for a sharp, clean freeze. You are now ready to take total physical control of your camera. Let us jump into the practice drill and find your personal handheld limit.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Find your personal handheld floor. Put a 50mm lens (or set your zoom to 50mm) on your camera in Aperture Priority or Manual mode. Find a stationary indoor subject in dim light. Take a test shot at 1/15s. Zoom in on the image playback to check for smear. Step your shutter speed up by full stops (1/30s, then 1/60s) while raising your ISO to compensate, until the smear completely disappears. That shutter speed threshold where your images become consistently crisp is your personal handheld floor.

## 7. RECAP
- Stance and elbows: Stand shoulder-width apart and lock your elbows against your ribs to build a human tripod.
- Breathing: Take a half-breath, pause, and press during the natural stillness.
- Smooth shutter press: Squeeze the shutter button gently like a trigger instead of stabbing at it.
- Reciprocal rule: Match your handheld shutter speed denominator to your focal length (e.g., 50mm lens needs at least 1/50s).
- ISO rescue: Raise your ISO when light drops so you can keep your shutter speed above your handheld floor, trading minor noise for absolute sharpness.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
- Question 1: What physical body part should your elbows touch to stabilize a handheld camera?
  Answer: Your ribcage. Tucking your elbows against your ribs prevents your arms from floating and dampens micro-movements.
- Question 2: According to the reciprocal rule, what is the minimum safe handheld shutter speed for a 200mm telephoto lens?
  Answer: 1/200 of a second (or faster), matching the focal length denominator.
- Question 3: If your shutter speed drops below your handheld floor in low light, what setting should you increase instead of letting your images blur?
  Answer: ISO. Raising ISO increases sensor sensitivity so you can use a faster, safer shutter speed.
