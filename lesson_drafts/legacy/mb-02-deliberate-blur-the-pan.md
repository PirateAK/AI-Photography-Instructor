# Motion, Blur & Freeze Lesson 2 - Deliberate blur: the pan

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back. Today we are going to talk about one of the most exciting techniques in photography: panning. If you want to make a still photo feel like it's racing at a hundred miles an hour, freezing the subject completely won't always do the trick. Sometimes, a frozen car against a frozen road just looks like a toy parked on a carpet. Panning—moving your camera right along with your subject as it passes—keeps your subject readable and crisp while turning the background into a gorgeous, dynamic streak of speed. 

It is a wrist skill, and I promise you, it takes a little practice. But once you lock in the rhythm, it feels amazing.

Let's start with what panning actually is. Panning is tracking a moving subject with your camera while using a shutter speed slow enough to blur the background. When you pan successfully, your camera moves at the exact same speed as your subject. Because your subject stays in the exact same spot on your sensor for the entire exposure, it stays sharp. Meanwhile, the background is moving relative to the camera, so it blurs into beautiful horizontal streaks. 

What shutter speed do we need for this? You want to drop down into what I call pan territory. That is usually around 1/15th of a second. Think about that for a moment. In our last lesson, we talked about using 1/500th or 1/1000th of a second to freeze action. Today, we are intentionally dropping down to 1/15th of a second. That is slow enough that any hand shake will ruin the shot, which is precisely why our technique has to be rock solid.

Let’s jump into our camera simulator and look at how to set this up. First, switch your camera to Shutter Priority mode, often labeled 'S' or 'Tv', or go straight to Manual mode if you are feeling brave. Let's set our shutter speed to 1/15th of a second. Now, what about our ISO and aperture? Because 1/15th lets in a lot of light, your camera might overexpose the scene, especially if you are shooting outside on an Alaskan afternoon. Drop your ISO down to its base setting, like ISO 100. If it's still too bright, stop down your aperture to f/8 or f/11. That restricts the light coming through the lens and helps keep your exposure balanced. 

Now, let's talk about the physical mechanics of the shot. Panning isn't just about moving your hands; it's about your entire upper body. Stand with your feet shoulder-width apart. Face the point where you want to take the picture, and then twist your upper body back toward where the subject is coming from. 

When you track your subject, you want to use a smooth, fluid motion originating from your hips and core, not just your wrists. Bring the camera to your eye, acquire your subject in the frame, and start tracking them *before* you press the shutter. 

This is the secret sauce: the follow-through. Most beginners press the shutter and immediately stop moving. That sudden stop creates a jerky jolt right at the end of the exposure, ruining the shot. You need to track the subject into the frame, squeeze the shutter gently, and *keep panning* even after the photo has finished exposing. Think of it like a golf swing or a baseball swing. You don't stop the bat the second it hits the ball; you follow through. 

Let's address the elephant in the room: your keeper rate. When you first start practicing panning at 1/15th of a second, expect to get about one keeper in twenty shots. That is totally normal. Even professional sports and wildlife photographers expect a high failure rate when panning. Some shots will be too blurry because your speed didn't match the subject. Some will be shaky because your vertical grip slipped. Do not get discouraged. Every single blurry frame is just muscle memory building in your hands. 

If your subject's face or main body is blurry, it means your camera speed didn't match their speed. You were either panning too fast or too slow. If the background looks sharp instead of streaked, your shutter speed was too fast—try dropping from 1/30th down to 1/15th. 

Panning takes patience, but it is one of the most rewarding creative tools in your camera bag. When you finally nail that sharp subject against a wildly streaked background, you'll know it was worth every failed frame. Let's get out there and try it.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Head outside to a sidewalk, bike path, or driveway where cars, cyclists, or running dogs pass by. Set your camera to Shutter Priority mode and dial your shutter speed to 1/15s (drop your ISO to 100 to avoid overexposure). Lock your focus, track a passing subject smoothly through your frame, and remember to keep your camera moving even *after* you hear the shutter click. Take at least twenty shots, expect nineteen to be blurry experiments, and celebrate that one glorious sharp keeper.

## 7. RECAP
- Panning keeps your subject sharp while turning the background into dynamic motion streaks.
- 1/15s is the sweet spot for pan territory, requiring low ISO (like 100) and a narrower aperture (like f/8) to balance the exposure.
- Twist from your core and hips, not just your wrists, for a smooth tracking motion.
- Always practice the follow-through: keep moving the camera even after the shutter fires.
- Expect a 1-in-20 keeper rate when starting out—it's a physical skill that builds with repetition.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
- **Question:** What shutter speed range is recommended for a deliberate pan in this lesson?
  - **Answer:** Around 1/15th of a second ("pan territory"), which is slow enough to create background blur while testing your tracking stability.
- **Question:** What does a sharp background and a blurry subject usually indicate during a panning attempt?
  - **Answer:** It indicates your shutter speed was set too fast, failing to let the relative motion blur the background, or that your camera speed did not match the subject.
- **Question:** Why is the "follow-through" critical when executing a pan?
  - **Answer:** Stopping your camera movement the moment you press the shutter causes a mechanical jolt that ruins the exposure and blurs the subject.
