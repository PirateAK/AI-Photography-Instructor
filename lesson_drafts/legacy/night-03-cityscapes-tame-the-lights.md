## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to MuskegMan Photography. Today we are tackling cityscapes, and I promise you, by the end of this lesson you will stop fearing those bright, chaotic city lights. 

City lights are both your absolute best friend and your worst hazard. When you point a camera at a glowing downtown skyline at night, your camera’s built-in light meter gets completely confused. It looks at all that dark night sky, panics, and tries to make the shadows as bright as daytime. The result? Blown-out streetlights, glowing blobs where windows should be, and an image that looks crunchy and fake. 

Today, we change that. We are going to tame those lights so your skylines glow with rich detail instead of clipping into a blinding white mess. 

Let's start with our exposure bedrock. When you shoot a cityscape, you want total sharpness from the closest street corner all the way to the farthest skyscraper. That means we are stepping away from wide-open apertures and dialing in f/8. 

Aperture, simply put, is the size of the opening in your lens that lets light in. An f-number like f/8 gives you deep depth of field—meaning everything is in sharp focus—while keeping your lens at its optical sweet spot for maximum sharpness. 

Next up, your sensor sensitivity. Dial your ISO down to ISO 100. ISO is your sensor’s sensitivity to light. At ISO 100, your sensor is as clean and unbothered as possible. No digital grain, no noise, just pure, clean data. 

Now, because f/8 and ISO 100 let in very little light, we need our shutter to stay open longer to gather the scene. Shutter speed is simply the length of time your camera sensor is exposed to light. 

Here is your magic baseline starting point for a city skyline: f/8, ISO 100, and a shutter speed of 4 seconds. 

Let’s talk about that 4-second shutter speed for a moment. Why 4 seconds? Because a city at night is alive. Cars are moving, ferries are crossing the harbor, and office lights are glowing. A 4-second shutter speed is long enough to let light accumulate smoothly on your sensor, turning moving car headlights into beautiful, silky streaks of red and white light, while keeping the stationary buildings pin-sharp. 

But here is where the hazard comes in. As you lengthen your shutter, light keeps piling onto your sensor. If you leave the shutter open too long—say, 15 or 30 seconds—those bright LED signs and streetlamps will flood your image with pure white data that can never be recovered. 

How do you know when to stop? You watch your histogram. A histogram is simply a little graph on your screen that shows the tonal distribution of your image, from pure shadows on the left to pure highlights on the right. 

As you lengthen your shutter—moving from 1 second to 2, then to our target 4 seconds—watch the right edge of that histogram. That right wall represents pure white. As long as your graph's data slope climbs up and tapers off before it smashes hard against that right wall, you are safe. The second that data stacks up vertically against the right edge, your highlights are clipped. That means data is lost forever. Stop lengthening your shutter right before that wall happens. 

Now, let's talk about color. City lights are a messy cocktail of different light sources. You have old-school high-pressure sodium vapor streetlights casting a gross, sickly orange glow, mixed with modern, cool-blue LEDs coming from office windows and storefronts. 

If you leave your camera on Auto White Balance, it will constantly bounce back and forth trying to fix the orange, turning your skies an unnatural, muddy green or magenta. White balance is simply the camera setting that removes unrealistic color casts so objects that look white in real life appear white in your photo. 

Instead of trusting auto, switch your camera off AWB. Try setting your white balance manually to Kelvin mode, right around 3800K to 4000K. Kelvin is a numerical scale of color temperature where lower numbers are cooler and bluer, and higher numbers are warmer and yellower. Setting your camera to 3,800 Kelvin cools down that ugly orange sodium glare, neutralizing the streetlights and leaving the sky with a gorgeous, deep, cinematic midnight blue. 

Let’s jump into our camera simulator on screen right now to see this in action. Notice how we dial in Manual mode. We set our aperture to f/8. We drop our ISO to 100. We roll our shutter speed dial until we hit 4 seconds. Look at that preview. The buildings are crisp. The car trails are smooth brushstrokes of light. And look at the histogram—the peak for the bright lights is leaning right up against the edge, but it hasn’t clipped. That is a gorgeous, balanced urban exposure. 

You don't need expensive gear to get this right. You just need patience, a sturdy tripod, and the discipline to read your histogram instead of guessing. 

Take a breath, dial in these settings, and let's go build some incredible cityscapes together. I'll see you in the next lesson.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Mount your camera on a tripod facing an illuminated street or building at night. Switch to Manual mode, set your aperture to f/8 and your ISO to 100. Start with a 1-second shutter speed and gradually increase it—2 seconds, 4 seconds, 8 seconds—while watching your live histogram. Stop lengthening your shutter right before the right-side data stacks vertically against the wall. Take that shot, check your focus, and note how the light accumulation improves the scene without blowing out the highlights.

## 7. RECAP
- Set your aperture to $f/8$ for maximum optical sharpness and deep depth of field across the entire skyline.
- Lock your ISO at 100 to keep your sensor noise-free and maintain maximum dynamic range.
- Use a 4-second baseline shutter speed to let light accumulate and smooth out moving traffic into clean light trails.
- Watch the right edge of your live histogram to prevent highlights from clipping against the wall.
- Switch off Auto White Balance and use Kelvin mode around 3800K to neutralize ugly orange sodium vapor lights and restore a clean midnight blue sky.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
- **Question:** Why is f/8 recommended as a baseline aperture for night cityscapes instead of a wide-open aperture like f/1.8?
  **Answer:** f/8 provides a deep depth of field to keep distant skyscrapers and near street corners sharp, while hitting the optical sweet spot of most lenses.
- **Question:** What does a spike pressed hard against the right wall of the histogram indicate?
  **Answer:** It indicates that highlights are clipped (overexposed) and data has been permanently lost in the brightest areas of the image.
- **Question:** Why should you avoid Auto White Balance when shooting under city streetlights?
  **Answer:** City lights mix orange sodium vapor and cool LEDs; Auto White Balance will constantly miscalculate, resulting in muddy, inconsistent color casts. Kelvin mode (approx. 3800K) locks in a clean, natural balance.
