# Photography Masterclass Lesson 5 - Focusing systems in depth

## 1. LEARNING OUTCOME
By the end of this lesson, the student can configure advanced autofocus modes, select optimal AF-area settings for static and moving subjects, and execute back-button focus to take total control over when and where their camera locks sharpness.

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back, everyone. Today we are diving deep into your camera’s focusing systems. I know the feeling—you line up a gorgeous portrait, press the shutter halfway, and the lens just hunts back and forth, missing the exact moment you wanted to capture. It feels like the camera has a mind of its own. But by the end of this lesson, you are going to take full manual control of where and when your camera locks sharpness, so that never happens again.

Let us start with the foundation: AF modes. On your top dial or in your quick menu, you will see letters like AF-S, AF-C, and sometimes AF-A. What do these mean in plain English? AF-S stands for Autofocus Single. When you press your shutter button halfway down, the camera looks at your subject, locks focus once, and stays locked right there. It assumes your subject is standing still. If you are shooting a landscape at f/8, a bowl of fruit on your kitchen table, or a posed portrait of a friend, AF-S is your best friend. It gives you maximum precision because the camera checks focus one time and holds it.

Now, what happens if your subject starts moving? That is where AF-C comes in—Autofocus Continuous. In AF-C mode, the moment you press that shutter button halfway, your camera’s computer goes into overdrive. It predicts where your subject is moving and continuously updates the focus distance inside the lens until you press the shutter all the way down. If you are shooting kids running in the backyard, a dog chasing a ball, or a wedding guest walking down the aisle, you want to be in AF-C. Forget AF-A, which stands for Automatic. That is just the camera trying to guess whether your subject is moving or not, and honestly, it guesses wrong at the worst possible times. Take control and choose between S and C yourself.

Next up, we need to talk about AF-area choices. This is not *how* the camera focuses, but *where* you tell the camera to look. If you leave your camera on Auto-Area AF—where the little green boxes light up all over the screen—your camera will usually guess that the closest object is what you want. But what if your subject is standing behind a fence, or a tree branch is hanging in the foreground? The camera gets confused and focuses on the branch. 

Instead, switch your AF-area mode to Single-Point. This gives you one tiny, movable focus square in your viewfinder or on your rear screen. You use your joystick or multi-selector pad to physically move that square right over your subject's eye. When you see that little focus confirmation dot light up solid in the corner of your viewfinder, it means the exact zone under that square is sharp. You are no longer letting the camera gamble with your composition. For wider moving scenes, you can step up to Zone AF, which groups a cluster of points together, or Wide/Tracking, which lets you pick a subject and lets the camera track it across the frame.

Now, let us talk about a game-changing habit used by professional photographers everywhere: back-button focus. By default, your camera is set up so that pressing the shutter button halfway down both focuses *and* takes the picture. But think about that for a second. Every time you recompose your shot—shifting your camera slightly after locking focus—you change the distance to your subject, which can ruin your sharpness. And if you are waiting for a fleeting moment, pressing the shutter button down can cause micro-shake. 

Back-button focus fixes this by separating the two jobs. You go into your camera menu, find the custom controls, and assign the Autofocus-On (AF-ON) button—usually located on the back right of your camera body—to handle all focusing duties. Then, you take focusing completely off the shutter button. 

What does this feel like in practice? Your index finger now *only* presses the shutter to take pictures. Your thumb rests comfortably on the back AF-ON button. When you want to focus, you press your thumb down and hold it. If you are in AF-C, your thumb tracks the subject. When you let go of the back button, focus locks instantly, and your index finger is free to click the shutter as many times as you want without the camera trying to refocus. It feels a little clumsy for about ten minutes, and then you will never, ever go back to the standard setup.

Finally, let us look at a neat trick called trap focusing. This is an advanced technique for when you know *exactly* where a subject is going to pass, but the movement is too fast for human reaction time. Imagine you are trying to photograph a specific branch where a bird lands, or an athlete crossing a finish line. In this setup, you switch your camera to manual focus on the lens, but you set your camera body to only fire the shutter when focus is confirmed on that exact spot. You pre-focus on the finish line, hold the shutter down, and the camera waits, firing the exact microsecond something enters that sharp zone. 

Let us do a quick mental check. We have mastered AF-S for static scenes, AF-C for motion, Single-Point AF for pinpoint accuracy, and back-button focus to keep our thumbs in charge of sharpness. You are no longer fighting your gear. You are directing it. Let us head into the practice drill and put these systems into action.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Find a moving subject—such as a family member walking across the room, a pet, or cars passing on a nearby street. Switch your camera to AF-C (Continuous Autofocus) and use a fast shutter speed of at least 1/500s. Select Single-Point AF-area, place the focus point dot directly on your moving subject, and practice tracking them smoothly while keeping the focus point locked on their eye or center of mass. Take three burst shots and review your images to confirm the sharp zone sits precisely where your focus point was tracking.

## 7. RECAP
- Use **AF-S (Single)** for static subjects like landscapes and portraits where maximum precision is needed.
- Use **AF-C (Continuous)** combined with a fast shutter speed to successfully track moving subjects.
- Switch from Auto-Area to **Single-Point AF** to take control of exactly where your camera locks sharpness.
- Adopt **Back-Button Focus** by assigning the AF-ON button to separate focusing from the shutter release.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
- **Question 1:** When should you choose AF-S over AF-C?
  *Answer:* AF-S is best for static subjects (like landscapes, still life, or posed portraits) because it locks focus once for maximum precision, whereas AF-C continually adjusts focus for moving subjects.
- **Question 2:** What does the focus confirmation dot in the viewfinder tell you?
  *Answer:* It indicates that the exact zone under your active focus point has achieved sharp focus.
- **Question 3:** What is the primary benefit of back-button focus?
  *Answer:* It decouples focusing from the shutter release button, allowing you to lock focus independently, recompose without shifting focus distance, and track motion seamlessly using your thumb.
