# Photography Masterclass Lesson 6 of 6 - The photographer's workflow

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to the final lesson of our Photography Masterclass. Today, we’re talking about the photographer’s workflow. I want you to think about the last time you came home from a great shoot. You dumped two thousand raw files onto your hard drive, opened up your editing software, and then... stared at a blinking cursor for an hour, totally overwhelmed. Sound familiar? It happens to all of us. 

Here is the secret the pros know: photography is only fifty percent shooting. The other fifty percent is system management. Today, we are going to build that system together, taking you step-by-step from pre-visualization all the way to final delivery, without drowning in files. Let’s get into it.

Let's start at the very beginning: Phase One, pre-visualization. Before your finger ever touches the shutter button on your camera, you need to know what you are making. Pre-visualization simply means picturing the final image in your head before you even unpack your gear. Are you shooting a moody landscape at ISO 100 with a 3-second exposure, or a punchy portrait at f/2.8? 

When you pre-visualize, you shoot with purpose. You aren't spraying and praying three hundred frames of the same exact angle. You take ten intentional shots because you know exactly how they fit into your final story. On set, this means setting your camera up right the first time. Shoot in RAW so you capture every single detail of dynamic range—that’s the span of light from deepest shadow to brightest highlight your sensor can record—and check your histogram immediately after your first test frame. If the highlights are blinking, dial down your exposure compensation by minus one-third stop. Get it right in the camera, and your future self will thank you.

Now let’s jump ahead to Phase Two: Ingestion and culling. You’ve wrapped the shoot, you’re back at your desk, and your SD card is packed. Step one is ingestion. That just means transferring your image files from the camera card to your working hard drive using a dedicated cataloging software. Create a clear folder structure right away: year, date, and shoot name. No mystery folders named "New Folder 3." 

Once the files are safe on your drive, it is time to cull. Culling is the ruthless process of sorting your photos to separate the keepers from the trash. This is where most photographers stall out. They spend hours staring at twenty slightly different frames of the exact same landscape. Here is your golden rule for culling: trust your gut reaction. 

Use a simple rating system. Press one key to flag your absolute favorites, and another to mark the rejects. Move fast. If you spend more than three seconds debating whether a blurry, out-of-focus shot is "artsy," hit delete. Be brutal. A pro photographer with a hundred amazing images looks infinitely more talented than one with five thousand mediocre ones. We are looking for sharp focus, compelling composition, and strong emotion. Everything else gets left on the cutting room floor.

That brings us to Phase Three: Processing and editing. This is where you develop your selects and bring out their full potential. The biggest mistake beginners make here is sliding every single slider to the maximum. Heavy-handed HDR effects, neon greens, and crushed blacks scream "amateur hour." 

Instead, adopt a clean, repeatable editing rhythm. Start with your global adjustments—that means fixing your white balance, dropping your highlights to recover sky detail, and lifting your shadows just enough to reveal texture. Then, move to your local adjustments. Use a graduated filter or a brush tool to subtly darken distracting background elements or brighten your subject's face. 

Keep your editing style consistent. If you use a warm, muted color grade on one photo in a set, carry that same mood through the rest of the delivery. Your edits should serve the story of the image, not distract from it. When you feel the urge to push the saturation up another twenty points, walk away from the screen for five minutes, come back, and look at it with fresh eyes. Less is almost always more.

Finally, we arrive at Phase Four: Exporting and delivery. You’ve edited your final selects. Now you need to get them out into the world. Exporting is the process of converting your raw editing instructions into finished, compressed file formats like JPEGs that clients, family, or social media platforms can actually view.

You need to export with intent. If you are uploading to Instagram, export your JPEG to a 4:5 vertical crop with a long edge of 1350 pixels, saved at high quality. If you are delivering high-resolution files to a paying client for printing, export full resolution at maximum quality in the sRGB or AdobeRGB color space, depending on their print lab's requirements. 

Always rename your exported files professionally. "IMG_4829.jpg" tells nobody anything. "Alaska-Landscape-Sunset-01.jpg" tells the world you run a serious operation. Deliver your files through a clean, web-based client gallery link rather than clogging up email attachments. It looks professional, keeps your backups secure, and gives your viewer an incredible browsing experience from start to finish.

Let's do a quick mental check. We pre-visualized our shot on location to cut down on useless shutter clicks. We ingested and culled our files quickly using a strict one-to-three-second rule. We processed our images with a clean, consistent global-to-local editing rhythm. And we exported with custom sizing and professional naming conventions. 

When you follow this workflow, photography stops feeling like an endless chore of file management and starts feeling like a smooth, creative craft. You stay organized, you protect your energy, and you deliver work you can genuinely be proud of every single time.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Recreate your best-ever shot from memory using only the camera simulator—settings, framing intent, and light. Once built, apply our four-step workflow: pre-visualize the scene, evaluate your virtual "RAW" file, make clean global and local edits, and export a properly named JPEG. If you can rebuild and process it from scratch, you truly understand your own creative process.

## 7. RECAP
- Pre-visualize your shot before touching the shutter to eliminate useless duplicates and shoot with purpose.
- Ingest files into a strict, date-based folder structure immediately after a shoot.
- Cull ruthlessly using a fast gut-check method, keeping only your absolute best frames.
- Process with a clean rhythm: start with global adjustments like white balance and highlights, then use local tools.
- Export with intent, applying custom file naming, proper sizing for your delivery platform, and organized galleries.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
- **Question 1:** What is the primary purpose of pre-visualizing an image before shooting?
  - *Answer:* To picture the final result ahead of time, which leads to intentional framing, fewer wasted shutter clicks, and better on-set camera settings.
- **Question 2:** Why should you avoid heavy-handed slider adjustments during the processing phase?
  - *Answer:* Excessive adjustments like maxed-out saturation or crushed blacks create an unnatural, amateur look and distract from the true story of the image.
- **Question 3:** What is the benefit of renaming your exported files instead of keeping the camera's default naming (like IMG_XXXX)?
  - *Answer:* Custom naming conventions (like Project-Name-01.jpg) look professional, help with file organization, and make your work much easier to search and archive.
