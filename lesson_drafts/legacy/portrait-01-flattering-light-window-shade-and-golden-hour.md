# Portrait Photography Lesson 1 - Flattering light: window, shade and golden hour

## 1. LEARNING OUTCOME
By the end of this lesson, the student can identify and utilize soft, flattering light sources—such as north-facing windows, open shade, and golden hour—to shape dimension and minimize harsh shadows on a subject's face while operating in manual exposure.

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to MuskegMan Photography's portrait series. Today we are tackling light—specifically, finding and using light that flatters every single person you point your camera at. If you have ever taken a portrait outside at noon and wondered why your friend looks like they spent ten rounds in a boxing ring with deep raccoon eyes under their brows, this lesson is for you. 

Let us start with a foundational truth. Soft light flatters skin, while hard light sculpts it. Hard light comes from a small, direct source—think of the blazing midday sun or a bare desk lamp. It casts sharp, dark shadows under the nose, chin, and eye sockets. Soft light, on the other hand, comes from a large source or a diffused sky. It wraps gently around the curves of a face, smoothing out texture, blemishes, and harsh lines. As portrait photographers, we are almost always hunting for soft light. 

Our first and most accessible soft light source is right inside your home: the window. Think of a window as a giant softbox. When you place your subject near a window, you get directional light that falls off softly across their face. 

Let us look at how to position your subject. If you stand your subject directly in front of the window, facing you while you look out the window, you get flat lighting. That is fine, but it lacks dimension. Instead, try turning your subject at a forty-five-degree angle to the window. One side of their face is kissed by the window light, while the other side falls into a gentle, natural shadow. This is called Rembrandt lighting, named after the famous painter. It instantly adds depth and drama without making the subject look harsh. 

What if the window light is too contrasty on one side? You can use a simple reflector, or even a white poster board placed on the shadow side of your subject, to bounce some of that window light right back into the dark side of their face. This is called filling the shadows, and it brings back detail without ruining the soft wrap of the light.

Now let us step outside. If it is an overcast, cloudy day, pack your gear and get outside immediately. Beginners often think cloudy days are gloomy and boring. Portrait photographers know that an overcast sky is a giant, outdoor diffusion panel. The clouds scatter the sun's rays across the entire sky, turning the atmosphere into one enormous softbox. On an overcast day, you can shoot anywhere. You do not have to worry about harsh shadows under the eyes or squinting subjects. You get even, gorgeous, creamy skin tones in every direction. 

Our third favorite lighting scenario is the golden hour. That is the magical hour right after sunrise and right before sunset when the sun is low on the horizon. During this time, the sunlight has to travel through a thicker layer of atmosphere, which strips away harsh blues and leaves behind warm, golden, buttery tones. 

When you shoot during golden hour, try putting the sun behind your subject. This is called backlighting. Position your subject so the low sun acts as a rim light, outlining their hair and shoulders in a glowing halo. To keep their face from turning into a silhouette, simply step in close, meter for the shadows on their face, and let the background glow. If a stray beam of sunlight hits your front glass element, you might get a lovely lens flare, which adds an organic, dreamy mood to the portrait.

Let us jump into our camera simulator and put these lighting concepts together with manual settings that melt your backgrounds away. 

Imagine you have got your subject standing in that gorgeous open shade beside a building, or glowing in the golden hour light. Let us set your camera up for a classic portrait look. Dial your aperture wide open to f/2. Your aperture is the opening inside your lens that lets light in; a low number like f/2 means a big opening, which lets in lots of light and creates a very blurry, dreamy background—what we call shallow depth of field. 

Next, set your shutter speed to 1/200th of a second. Shutter speed is simply how long your camera sensor is exposed to light. 1/200s is fast enough to freeze any minor hand shake or subtle movement from your subject. 

Now, look at your screen. Watch that background instantly melt away into creamy bokeh. But here is the catch when shooting at f/2: your zone of sharp focus is razor thin—sometimes only a couple of inches deep. If you focus on the tip of their nose, their eyes might be soft. 

Take your finger or your mouse and drag that autofocus dot right onto the iris of the eye closest to you. That is the critical zone you must protect at all costs in a portrait. The eye is the anchor of the human face. If the eye is sharp, the viewer will forgive a slightly soft ear or shoulder every single time. 

Practice moving your subject around your house or yard today. Find a window, find some open shade under a tree, and wait for that golden hour glow. You are going to see an immediate leap in the quality of your portraits. Keep shooting, be kind to your subjects, and I will see you in the next lesson.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
- **Portrait Starting Point Drill**: Open your camera simulator or grab your digital camera. Step outside into open shade or stand near a large window indoors. Set your lens to its widest aperture (such as f/2 or f/2.8) and your shutter speed to 1/200s. Observe how the background completely melts away into soft blur. Now, manually drag your focus point or select your single autofocus zone, placing it directly over the subject's eye closest to the camera. Take the shot, zoom in on the eye to confirm it is tack-sharp, and practice this three times until placing focus on the eye becomes second nature.

## 7. RECAP
- Soft light wraps around the face and flatters skin, while hard light creates deep, harsh shadows.
- Position your subject at a 45-degree angle to a window for natural, flattering dimension.
- Overcast, cloudy days act as giant outdoor softboxes, making them ideal for portraits anywhere.
- Golden hour provides warm, low-angle light and gorgeous backlighting opportunities.
- Shooting wide open at f/2 with a 1/200s shutter speed creates a dreamy background blur, but requires you to place your focus point precisely on the subject's eye.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
- **Question 1**: Why does a cloudy, overcast day make portraits easier for beginners?
  *Answer*: Clouds act as a giant natural diffuser that scatters sunlight evenly across the sky, eliminating harsh directional shadows and high contrast.
- **Question 2**: What camera settings are recommended in the practice drill to achieve a melted, blurry background?
  *Answer*: A wide-open aperture (such as f/2) combined with a shutter speed of 1/200s.
- **Question 3**: Why is it critical to place your autofocus point directly on the subject's eye when shooting at wide apertures like f/2?
  *Answer*: Because a wide aperture creates a very shallow depth of field (a very thin zone of sharpness), and the eye is the essential anchor of human attention in a portrait.
