# Portrait Photography Lesson 2 - The eyes have it: focus and connection

## 1. LEARNING OUTCOME
By the end of this lesson, the student can set single-point autofocus on a subject's near eye, balance an aperture between f/1.8 and f/5.6 for individual or group portraits, and direct eye contact to create an engaging emotional connection.

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to the portrait studio. Today we are talking about the soul of every single portrait you will ever take: the eyes. I'm sure you've looked at photos before where something felt a little off, even if the lighting was gorgeous. Nine times out of ten, the focus missed the eyes. Today, we fix that for good. 

Let's start with our first big concept: Single-Point Autofocus. Autofocus, or AF, is your camera's built-in motor that locks onto your subject so they aren't blurry. Most cameras come out of the box set to "Auto-Area AF," which means the camera guesses what you want to be sharp. Usually, it guesses the tip of a nose or a shiny jacket button. We are taking back control. Switch your focus mode from auto to Single-Point AF. This lets you manually place a tiny focus box over one specific spot in your frame. 

In portrait photography, that spot is almost always the near eye—the eye physically closest to your camera lens. Why the near eye? Because as humans, the very first thing we look at when we see a face is the eye closest to us. If that near eye is tack-sharp, our brain forgives a lot of other minor softness in the image. If that near eye is blurry, our brain immediately rejects the photo as a mistake. 

Let's look at our camera simulator on screen right now. Watch as I take my joystick, move that single focus point right over the model's left eyelash, and half-press the shutter button. *Beep.* We are locked on. 

Now, let's talk about aperture—the adjustable opening inside your lens that lets light in. You've probably heard that wide apertures make gorgeous, blurry backgrounds. That blur is called bokeh. But there is a catch: the wider your aperture, the thinner your slice of focus. 

Let’s set our lens to f/2 using our aperture control dial. We are standing about six feet away from our model. Look closely at the simulator preview. At f/2, our depth of field—the zone of acceptable sharpness from front to back—is razor-thin. It is roughly the width of a couple of eyelashes. If you focus on the near eye, that near eye will be crystal clear. But look at the ear on the far side of the head. It's melting away into a soft, dreamy blur. And look at the tip of the nose; it might already be starting to slip out of focus. That is normal at f/2. It is a creative choice that isolates your subject from a busy background. 

Let's do a quick mental check. Are you worried that f/2 is too unforgiving? Don't be. Missing focus a few times while you learn this is just part of the journey. Every pro in Alaska misses focus sometimes. The secret is simply practice. When you use single-point AF at f/2, you are making a deliberate artistic statement: *Look right here, at this exact glance.*

Now, what happens when your portrait session goes from one person to two, or four? This is where a lot of beginner photographers stumble. If you shoot a group of three friends sitting on a couch at f/1.8 or f/2, only one person's eye will be sharp. The person sitting slightly behind them will be a blurry mess. 

So, we change our settings. When we add more people to the frame, we step down our aperture to give ourselves a thicker slice of focus. Let's dial that aperture wheel from f/2 down to f/5.6. Notice what happens on our simulator screen. The background bokeh softens up, but now the couple sitting next to each other are both entirely in sharp focus, from the tip of the nose all the way back to the ears. When you're shooting groups, f/4 to f/5.6 is your safety zone. 

Beyond technical sharpness, there is another kind of focus we need to talk about: emotional connection. The way your subject looks at the camera completely changes the story your photo tells. 

When your subject looks directly down the barrel of the lens, they are making eye contact with the eventual viewer. That creates an intense, intimate, and sometimes confrontational mood. It's powerful. It says, "I see you." Use direct eye contact when you want confidence, strength, or a direct conversation between the viewer and the subject. 

On the flip side, what happens when you ask your subject to look just past your shoulder, or down at their hands? Suddenly, the mood shifts to contemplative, candid, or lonely. They are no longer acknowledging the viewer. They are living their own private moment, and the viewer is simply peeking in through a window. Neither style is better than the other, but you need to choose it on purpose. Don't just default to direct eye contact every time. Ask yourself what feeling you want to evoke, and direct your subject's gaze accordingly.

Let's tie all of this together. First, you take control of your autofocus by switching to Single-Point AF. Second, you place that point squarely on the near eye. Third, you match your aperture to your subject count: f/2 for a dreamy solo portrait with razor-thin focus, and f/5.6 for a group where everyone needs to be sharp. Finally, you direct their gaze—either locking eyes with the camera for a bold connection, or looking away for a quiet, candid story. 

You've got this. Take these tools, open up your simulator or your real camera, and start experimenting with where your focus lands. I'll see you in the next lesson.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
**Drill: The Eyelash Test**
1. Set your camera or simulator to Aperture Priority (A/Av) mode and dial your aperture to **f/2**.
2. Position a subject (or a mannequin/test object) at a 45-degree angle to your camera position.
3. Switch to **Single-Point AF**, place your focus point directly on the **near eye**, and take a shot.
4. Review the image at 100% zoom: verify that the near eyelashes are razor-sharp while the far ear and tip of the nose fall off into a soft blur. 
5. Repeat three times until placing that focus point on the near eye becomes second nature.

## 7. RECAP
- **Single-Point AF:** Take control of your camera by manually placing your focus point where *you* want it, not where the camera guesses.
- **The Near Eye Rule:** Always focus on the eye closest to the camera; it's the anchor the human brain looks for first.
- **Aperture & Depth of Field:** Use **f/2** for a dreamy solo portrait with a razor-thin focus zone, and step down to **f/5.6** for groups so everyone stays sharp.
- **Gaze and Connection:** Direct eye contact creates bold, intimate engagement, while averted glances tell a quiet, candid story.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
1. **Question:** Why should you use Single-Point AF instead of Auto-Area AF for portraits?
   - *Answer:* Because auto modes often guess the tip of the nose or clothing, whereas single-point lets you guarantee the focus lands precisely on the near eye.
2. **Question:** If you are shooting a solo portrait at f/2, what happens to the subject's ears?
   - *Answer:* Because f/2 provides a razor-thin depth of field, the ears will fall outside the narrow focus zone and appear softly blurred.
3. **Question:** What aperture range is recommended as a safety zone when shooting a group portrait of three people?
   - *Answer:* Around f/4 to f/5.6, which provides a thick enough depth of field to keep everyone's face sharp.
