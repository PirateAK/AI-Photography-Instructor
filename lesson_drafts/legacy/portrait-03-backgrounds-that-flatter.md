## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to MuskegMan Photography’s portrait series. Today we are looking at backgrounds that flatter. If you’ve ever taken a photo where a tree branch looks like it’s growing out of your subject’s ear, you are in the right place. 

Let's start with a truth every photographer learns the hard way: your background is half of your portrait. A bad background distracts. A great background tells a story and makes your subject pop. And the best part? We control all of this in camera, before you ever press the shutter.

Let’s look at our first concept: tone and contrast. Tone simply means how bright or dark an area is in your frame. Our human eyes are naturally drawn to the brightest parts of an image. If your subject is wearing a dark blue jacket, but behind them is a blazing white house or a patch of harsh midday sky, guess where your viewer's eye is going to go? Not to your subject's eyes. 

To fix this in camera, you want your subject to be brighter than the background, or at least share a similar tonal range. If you're shooting outside on a sunny day in Alaska, don't put your subject against a sunlit gravel road while they wear a grey hoodie. Step them back into the edge of the tree line where the shadows create a darker, more even backdrop. Now, your subject's face is the brightest thing in the frame, and the viewer instantly connects with them.

Moving on to our second concept: clutter and distraction. Clutter is anything in the frame that competes with your subject for attention. Bright neon signs, telephone poles, random cars parked on the street, or text on a building. When we look at a scene in real life, our brains filter out the clutter automatically. But your camera sensor doesn't have a brain. It records everything equally.

So how do we fix clutter without packing up and moving? First, change your physical perspective. Drop down low and shoot upward at your subject. Suddenly, that messy parking lot disappears, and your background is just the clean, smooth blue sky or a canopy of birch leaves. Or take two steps to the left so that ugly electrical box is completely hidden behind your subject's torso. 

Next up is our third concept: distance and aperture. This is where the magic happens. Aperture is simply the size of the opening inside your lens that lets light in, measured in f-stops. A wide aperture, like f/2.8, lets in more light and creates a very shallow depth of field—meaning a blurry background. A narrow aperture, like f/11, keeps everything sharp from front to back.

Let's jump into the camera simulator on your screen right now. Watch what happens when I set my 85mm lens to f/2.8 and place my subject ten feet away from a distracting brick wall. The wall turns into a soft, creamy wash of color and texture. Now, watch what happens if I close down that aperture to f/11. Instantly, every single brick snaps into sharp focus, competing directly with my subject. 

Distance is the other half of this recipe. If you can't get your aperture wide open because of your lens, you can still blur that background by creating space. Move your subject further away from the background. If your portrait subject is standing two feet from a chain-link fence, that fence is going to be in focus no matter what you do. But if you move your subject fifteen feet away from that fence, and step back yourself, the distance alone will start melting that fence away into a soft blur. 

Let's put all of this together into one simple rule of thumb for your next shoot. Before you tell your subject to smile, look behind them. Ask yourself three quick questions. Is the background brighter than my subject's face? Are there distracting objects poking out of their head? And is my subject far enough away from the background to create separation? 

If you answer yes to those three checks, you are ready to shoot. You're going to get professional-looking portraits where the background supports your subject instead of fighting them. Let's head over to the recap and look at your practice drill for today.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Find a subject (a friend, family member, or even a houseplant) and place them in front of a messy background, like a cluttered room or a chain-link fence. Take one photo with your lens at a narrow aperture (like f/8 or f/11) standing close to the background. Then, use the camera simulator or your own gear to widen your aperture (to f/2.8 or the lowest number your lens allows) AND move your subject at least ten feet away from that messy background. Compare the two shots on your screen to see the instant difference in background blur.

## 7. RECAP
- Your background is half of your portrait; control it in camera before you shoot.
- Manage tone and contrast so your subject's face remains the brightest part of the frame.
- Eliminate clutter by changing your physical angle, crouching down, or shifting side to side.
- Use a wide aperture (like f/2.8) combined with physical distance between your subject and the background to create professional blur.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
1. **Question:** What does a wide aperture, like f/2.8, do to your background?
   - **Answer:** It creates a shallow depth of field, making the background blurry and soft so your subject stands out.
2. **Question:** If your subject is wearing dark clothing, what kind of background tone should you avoid?
   - **Answer:** Avoid bright, high-contrast backgrounds like a sunlit white wall or a bright sky, which will draw the viewer's eye away from your subject.
3. **Question:** Besides widening your aperture, what is the other key way to increase background blur?
   - **Answer:** Increasing the physical distance between your subject and the background.
