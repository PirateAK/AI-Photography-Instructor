# Street Photography Lesson 1 - Pre-set and wait: the street method

## 1. LEARNING OUTCOME

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to MuskegMan Photography. Today we are tackling the classic street photography method: pre-set and wait. 

If you watch movies, you might think great street photographers sprint down alleys, spinning focus rings at twenty frames per second, capturing wild, chaotic moments on pure reflex. But the truth is much calmer. The pros actually do most of their work before the subject even enters the frame. They set their exposure, pre-focus their lens, and wait for the world to walk right into their composition. 

By the end of this lesson, you will know how to lock down your settings so you never miss a shot fumbling with dials while a great moment walks right past you.

Let’s start with step one: locking down your manual exposure. Street scenes change fast. One second your subject is in bright sunlight, the next they are under a shady storefront awning. If you leave your camera on Auto, it is going to panic. It will try to make every dark shadow look like bright daylight, ruining your shutter speeds and blowing out your highlights. 

Instead, we are going full manual. Let’s dial in settings that work for a bright, open sidewalk: set your shutter speed to one-thousandth of a second—written as 1/1000—which freezes walking feet and swinging arms. Next, set your ISO to four hundred. That’s bright enough for most daylight without adding ugly digital noise. Finally, let’s choose an aperture of f-five point six. 

Now, let’s talk about aperture, which is simply the size of the opening inside your lens that lets light in. An f-number like f/5.6 gives us a nice, balanced middle ground. It lets in plenty of light and gives us a deep zone of sharpness. 

Point your camera simulator at the street scene on screen. Notice how the light hits that brick wall on the right? Meter your exposure for that bright patch of sunlight. Lock it in. Now, when your subject steps from the shadow into the light, your exposure doesn't shift at all. The highlights stay rich and detailed, and you don't waste a microsecond waiting for your light meter to catch up.

That brings us to step two: zone focusing. This is the real secret of the street. Autofocus is amazing, but it takes time to hunt and lock onto a moving target. If your camera has to think for half a second, the moment is gone. 

Instead, we are going to use manual focus and set our focus distance to three meters—about ten feet away. Switch your lens from autofocus, marked as AF, over to manual focus, marked as MF. Now, turn that focus ring until your distance scale reads three meters. 

Why three meters? Because at f/5.6, three meters gives you a massive "zone" of acceptable sharpness. Anything from about two meters to five meters away from your camera will be in focus. You don’t need to twist that ring again. Anyone walking through that three-meter sweet spot will be sharp automatically. 

Let's test this in the simulator. Look at the sidewalk marker on your screen. That line is roughly three meters from our lens position. If a person walks across that line, they live right inside our focus zone. You don’t track them with your focus ring. You just raise the camera and press the shutter the moment they cross the line. 

Step three is all about composition and patience. This is where street photography becomes an exercise in design. Find a compelling background first. Look for interesting geometric shadows cast by a building, a bright colored doorway, or a clean, minimalist concrete wall. 

Once you find your backdrop, frame it up. Lock your camera onto that spot—either on a sturdy tripod or just held firmly against your body with your elbows locked into your ribs. Decide where the edge of your frame is, and then wait. You are essentially painting a stage, and now you are just waiting for an actor to walk onto it. 

Watch the simulator screen right now. We have framed up a classic urban corner with strong vertical lines and soft morning light. The exposure is locked at 1/1000, f/5.6, ISO 400. The focus is fixed at three meters. All we do is watch the edges of the frame. 

Here comes a pedestrian. Left foot, right foot. They enter the left side of the frame. They cross our three-meter focus line. Click. Sharp, well-exposed, perfectly timed. They keep walking out the other side, and we didn't touch a single dial. 

The hardest part of this method isn't technical. It’s mental. It is fighting the urge to fidget. We are so used to constantly tweaking our gear that just standing still and watching the world unfold feels unnatural. Embrace that quietness. Let the scene come to you. 

When you get home today, load up these exact settings and give yourself ten minutes on a quiet sidewalk or even just looking out your living room window at the street. Resist the temptation to chase your subjects. Trust your pre-set zone, watch your composition, and let the moment walk right into your frame.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Load your camera in Manual mode with a shutter speed of 1/1000s, ISO 400, and an aperture of f/5.6. Switch your lens to Manual Focus and set it to 3 meters. Find a static location with interesting background light (like a doorway or pathway), frame your shot, and stand completely still. Do not adjust your settings or twist your focus ring for 10 minutes. Practice waiting patiently for subjects to walk through your pre-set focus zone before pressing the shutter.

## 7. RECAP
- Lock your manual exposure for the brightest light in your scene to prevent blown-out highlights when subjects move.
- Set your lens to manual focus at 3 meters to create a reliable zone of sharpness from about 2 to 5 meters away.
- Use an aperture of f/5.6 to give yourself a deep enough focus zone for walking subjects.
- Find an interesting background composition first, lock your position down, and let the subjects walk into your frame instead of chasing them.

- **Answer:** Because street scenes change quickly; metering for the highlight prevents blown-out details when subjects step out of the shade into sunlight.

- **Answer:** It creates a deep zone of sharpness (roughly 2 to 5 meters) so walking subjects are instantly in focus without needing to adjust the focus ring.
- **Question:** What shutter speed do we use in this lesson to freeze walking pedestrians?
- **Answer:** One-thousandth of a second (1/1000s).
