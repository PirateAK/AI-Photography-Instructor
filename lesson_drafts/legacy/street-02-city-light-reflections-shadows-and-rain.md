# Street Photography Lesson 2 - City light: reflections, shadows and rain

## 1. LEARNING OUTCOME
By the end of this lesson, the student can use harsh urban light, puddles, and deep shadows to frame high-contrast street scenes by exposing for highlights and intentionally underexposing the rest.

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to the streets. If you completed our first session, you are already comfortable with your camera body and basic settings. Today, we are going to look at the city through a totally different lens—literally. We are going to talk about city light: reflections, shadows, and rain. 

Here is the big mindset shift for today, and it is going to change how you walk around town forever: Stop hunting for subjects. Hunt for the light instead. 

When you look for a person to photograph first, you end up chasing your tail. But when you find a magnificent pool of light—a shaft of sun cutting down an alleyway, or the neon glow spilling out of a coffee shop window—and you just stand there and wait for the right element to walk into it? That is how professional street photographs are made. You set the stage, and you let the city perform.

Let us start with the architecture of urban lighting. Cities are basically concrete canyons. You get these massive, towering buildings that block out the sky, creating patches of incredibly deep, dark shadow right next to blindingly bright shafts of midday sun. This is called harsh contrast. 

Many beginners look at this high contrast and think, "Oh no, my camera can't handle this, the dynamic range is too wide." They try to balance everything out, lifting the shadows and darkening the highlights until the whole image looks flat and muddy. 

Throw that instinct out the window. On the street, high contrast is your best friend. Instead of trying to capture every single detail from the darkest alley to the brightest sunlit brick, you want to pick a side. And most of the time, you want to pick the light. 

Let us jump into the camera simulator and see how this works in practice. Picture yourself downtown at two o'clock on a sunny afternoon. You are standing next to a brick building, and across the street, the sun is blazing off a glass storefront. 

Look at your exposure triangle. If we leave our camera on auto, it is going to panic. It is going to try to brighten up the shadows in our foreground, which means the bright glass across the street is going to blow out into a blinding, featureless white blob. We do not want that. 

Instead, let us switch to manual mode. Let us set our ISO to 400 because we are moving in and out of shade. Let us set our shutter speed to a crisp one-thousandth of a second—written as 1/1000 on your screen—to freeze any sudden movement. Now, let us dial our aperture down to f/5.6. 

Look at the live view on the simulator. As we point our meter toward that bright sunlit window, we adjust our shutter speed or aperture until the highlight details are locked in. We do not care if the foreground street corner goes pitch black. In fact, we *want* it to go dark. By exposing strictly for the bright lane of light, we create a cinematic, punchy frame. The darkness in the foreground acts like a theatrical curtain, framing the action happening in the light. 

Now, let us talk about what happens when the weather turns. Rain is a street photographer's absolute favorite forecast. When the sky opens up and pours, most people run for cover. That is your cue to pull your hood up, grab your camera, and head outside. 

Rain transforms the city into a giant mirror. Asphalt turns into a black canvas reflecting neon signs, yellow cabs, and passing pedestrians. 

When you are shooting in wet weather, look down. Puddles are your playground. To capture a great reflection, you need to get low. I mean, literally crouch down so your lens is just a foot or two above the puddle's surface. 

Let us go back to our simulator to see how we handle wet pavement settings. Say it is an overcast, rainy evening. The light is dropping fast. We bump our ISO up to 1600 to keep our sensor sensitive to that dim, moody light. We open our aperture wide to f/2.8 to let in as much light as possible and blur out any distracting background elements. 

Now, focus your lens manually or use touch-focus directly onto the reflection *inside* the puddle, not the surface of the water itself. Your camera might get confused and try to focus on a raindrop sitting on the glass of your lens, so switch to manual focus if it hunts. Twist that focus ring until the reflection of the passing red bus in the puddle is razor sharp. The real bus above might be slightly out of focus, and that is totally fine. The reflection is the hero of the image. 

This brings us to our third element: shadows and silhouettes. Shadows and highlights are not just lighting conditions; they are your composition. 

One of the most powerful techniques in street photography is intentional underexposure. We are so conditioned to make sure every face is brightly lit and every shadow is filled in. But when you underexpose your scene on purpose by one or two stops, magic happens. 

Let us look at a classic silhouette scenario. You are standing in a subway station exit or under a concrete overpass. Behind your subject is the blinding, bright light of the street. If you expose for your subject's face, the background will turn into a blinding white glare. But if you expose for that bright background—let us say ISO 200, f/8, and 1/2000 of a second—your subject instantly turns into a bold, black silhouette against the light. 

All the distracting details of their clothing, the background trash cans, the messy graffiti—they all vanish into pure, clean shadow. You are left with pure shape and form. A walking silhouette, the geometric line of a handrail, the stark curve of an umbrella. 

Don't be afraid of the dark parts of your frame. Embrace them. Look for long, dramatic morning or evening shadows cast by pedestrians, lamp posts, and bicycles. Frame your shot so the shadow stretches across the pavement, leading the viewer's eye right into the frame. 

Let's review the mindset for your next walk out the door. Stop looking for a smiling face looking back at you. Look for a puddle reflecting a yellow sign. Look for a shaft of hard sunlight cutting across a gray wall. Expose for the highlights, let the shadows fall where they may, and remember that underexposing on purpose is a street photographer's greatest superpower. 

Practice this on your next walk, have fun with it, and I will see you in the next lesson.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Find a location with high contrast—such as a building overhang, an alleyway entrance, or a bright window in shade. Set your camera to manual mode. Expose strictly for the brightest highlights in the scene, letting the shadows go completely dark. Shoot at least ten frames focusing purely on how shapes, silhouettes, and light interact within that contrast zone, ignoring whether background subjects are fully lit.

## 7. RECAP
- Hunt for the light first, not the subject; let interesting light attract the action to you.
- Embrace harsh contrast in urban environments instead of trying to flatten your dynamic range.
- Expose for your highlights in bright daylight to create punchy, dramatic, high-contrast frames.
- Use rain and puddles by getting low and focusing directly on surface reflections.
- Underexpose on purpose to turn distracting elements into clean, striking silhouettes and shadows.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
- Question: When shooting a high-contrast scene with bright sunlight and deep shadows, which area should you typically expose for to create a dramatic street look?
- Answer: Expose for the bright highlights. Letting the shadows underexpose creates a punchy, cinematic look with bold contrast.
- Question: What are recommended starting settings in the camera simulator for shooting a wet street reflection in dim, rainy light?
- Answer: A higher ISO (around 1600) to compensate for low light, a wide aperture (around f/2.8) to let in maximum light, and manual focus locked onto the reflection inside the puddle.
- Question: Why is intentional underexposure considered a street photographer's superpower?
- Answer: It plunges distracting background details and clutter into clean, dark shadows while leaving your key subjects or shapes as striking silhouettes.
