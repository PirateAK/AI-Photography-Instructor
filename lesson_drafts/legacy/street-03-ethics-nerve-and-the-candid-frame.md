## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to our final street photography lesson. Today we tackle the human side of the craft: ethics, nerve, and capturing the candid frame. If you've ever felt your stomach drop right before raising your camera to a stranger, you are in good company. Every street photographer feels that knot. But I promise you, by the end of this lesson, you'll know how to turn that nervous energy into genuine, respectful imagery.

Let's start with the baseline: the rules of the road. In most public places—like sidewalks, parks, and city plazas—you generally have a legal right to take photos of what you see in plain view. But legality is just the floor. Our ethical ceiling needs to be much higher. Street photography is a celebration of everyday humanity, not an invasion of privacy. If someone looks vulnerable, distressed, or asks you directly not to take their picture, you put the camera down. Period. Respect is currency on the street. When you treat people with dignity, they feel seen rather than exploited, and the resulting photographs carry that honest empathy.

Now, let's talk about nerve. That fear of being noticed? It usually stems from acting sneaky. If you creep around corners with a telephoto lens looking like a spy, people will sense it and feel uneasy. Instead, wear comfortable clothes, keep your camera on a strap around your neck, and move with purpose. You aren't doing anything wrong. You are documenting the tapestry of your city. 

To make this work in tricky evening light, we need a reliable technical setup. Let's look at your camera simulator. As the sun dips behind the buildings, our light drops fast. Evening streets push your ISO up, so let's dial your ISO to 3200. Yes, that introduces digital noise, which is just the grainy texture of digital sensors trying to see in the dark. Grain is the honest cost of being there to capture the moment. Pair that ISO 3200 with an aperture of f/4 to keep enough of your subject in sharp focus, and set your shutter speed to 1/250th of a second. That 1/250s shutter is our safety net; it freezes the casual stride of a pedestrian walking past under the streetlights, even if your hands wobble slightly.

So what happens when you take the shot, and the person catches you? It happens to all of us. First, don't panic or sprint away like you committed a crime. That makes a normal moment look suspicious. Instead, make eye contact, offer a warm, genuine smile, and give a polite nod. Sometimes, if they look curious or hesitant, I'll walk over, show them the back of the camera, and say, "Hey, the light hit your coat just right, beautiful evening out." Nine times out of ten, you get a smile back. And if they ask you to delete the file? You smile, say "no problem at all," and press delete right in front of them. A photograph is never worth someone else's peace of mind.

Street photography thrives when we balance technical readiness with a compassionate heart. By accepting a little grain at ISO 3200, we keep our shutter fast enough to tell real stories, and by treating our subjects with kindness, we keep the streets open and welcoming for every photographer who comes after us. Let's look at your practice drill and get you ready for the evening light.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Head out to a busy public street or transit hub during the late afternoon or early evening transition. Set your camera to manual mode, lock your ISO at 3200, set your aperture to f/4, and fix your shutter speed at 1/250s. Embrace the digital grain as the honest cost of shooting in low light. Practice walking with confidence, making eye contact, and respecting anyone who signals they do not wish to be photographed.

## 7. RECAP
- Public spaces generally allow photography, but your ethical duty to human dignity always comes first.
- Open body language, a neck strap, and a confident walk eliminate the suspicion of sneaking around.
- Evening light demands compromises: accept ISO 3200 grain to keep your shutter speed locked at a safe 1/250s.
- Grain is simply the honest cost of being present in the moment.
- If noticed, a warm smile, a polite nod, or deleting an image upon request preserves community goodwill.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
- **Question:** Why do we set the shutter speed to 1/250s for evening street photography?
  - **Answer:** To provide a safe shutter speed that freezes the motion of walking pedestrians and compensates for hand shake in low light.
- **Question:** What is the technical trade-off of setting your ISO to 3200 in the evening?
  - **Answer:** It introduces digital noise or grain, which is the necessary cost to keep your shutter fast enough for sharp handheld shots in dim light.
- **Question:** How should you respond if a subject notices you taking their photo and asks you to delete it?
  - **Answer:** Stay calm, smile, agree politely, and delete the photo immediately in front of them to respect their boundaries.
