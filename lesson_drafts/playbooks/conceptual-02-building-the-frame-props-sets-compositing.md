# Conceptual Playbook Lesson 2 - Building the frame: props, sets, compositing

## 1. LEARNING OUTCOME
By the end of this lesson, the student can stage a conceptual frame using a locked camera, consistent light, and deliberate prop placement, and can execute a clean multi-capture composite where only the active element changes.

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to MuskegMan Photography’s Conceptual Playbook. I’m your instructor, and today we are pulling back the curtain on how striking conceptual images are actually made. In Lesson 1, we talked about generating ideas. But an idea in your head is just a ghost until you build a physical world for it to live in. Today, we’re looking at building the frame: props, sets, and compositing. 

Conceptual frames are rarely found sitting out in nature, waiting for you to walk by and click the shutter. They are constructed. Every single item in the frame is placed there on purpose. And sometimes, a single shot isn’t enough to hold the whole idea. That’s when we build a composite—an image assembled from multiple captures layered together. But here is the golden rule you need to tattoo on your brain right now: a composite is only as clean as its individual parts. All the capture rules from your advanced combining lessons apply here. We need a locked-down camera, consistent light, and only one variable changing at a time. If your background shifts by even a millimeter between shots, your post-processing nightmare begins. Let's make sure that doesn't happen.

Let’s start inside the frame by talking about your set and your props. When you are building a conceptual set, you are essentially directing a tiny play on a tabletop or in a corner of your room. Every object tells a story, but not all objects shout equally. You need a hierarchy. That means you need a hero prop and supporting props. 

Your hero prop is the main character. It’s the object that carries the primary narrative weight of your concept. If your concept is about isolation, the hero prop might be a single vintage pocket watch resting on an old map. Your supporting props are the ensemble cast. They don't steal the show; they whisper context. They might be a scattered pile of dried pine needles and a brass compass. They frame the hero and guide the viewer's eye. 

Here is where many beginners trip up: clutter. We think that adding more stuff makes the concept deeper. Usually, it just makes it noisy. If a prop doesn’t actively serve the idea, take it out. Let’s look at the camera simulator on screen. Notice how we have our hero prop dead center, lit by a softbox at a 45-degree angle. We’ve set our camera to manual mode: ISO 100 for maximum image clarity, an aperture of f/8 to keep the entire set sharp from front to back, and a shutter speed of 1/160 of a second. 

Now, what happens if our concept requires something impossible? Say, a floating object, or a person holding a glowing orb that they physically cannot hold in real life? That’s where compositing comes in. Compositing is simply taking two or more photos taken from the exact same physical position and blending them together. 

To do this successfully, your camera cannot move. Not a fraction of a millimeter. This means your camera goes on a sturdy tripod, and you tighten every knob down hard. No ballhead sag allowed. If you bump the tripod, your whole sequence is ruined, and you have to start over. 

Next, lock down your focus. Switch your lens from autofocus to manual focus. Why? Because if autofocus is left on, the camera might hunt for contrast between shots, slightly shifting the focal plane or the physical framing of the lens elements. Lock your focus on your prop, and leave it alone. 

Then, lock your exposure. Put your camera in Manual (M) mode. Set your aperture, shutter speed, and ISO manually. Do not use Auto ISO. If Auto ISO bounces from 100 to 200 between your background shot and your hand shot, you will have patches of digital brightness that are impossible to blend naturally. 

Let’s walk through a live capture sequence for a floating-object composite. Shot one is our empty set—just the table, the background, and the ambient room lighting. This is our clean plate. Shot two keeps the exact same framing, but now we introduce a supporting hand holding our hero prop in mid-air. We take the shot. 

When we take these into editing later, we will stack Shot two on top of Shot one. We will use a layer mask to paint away the hand, revealing the clean table underneath, leaving our hero prop magically suspended in mid-air. But this magic trick only works if the lighting on the table didn't change between frames. If a cloud rolled past your window while you were moving your hand into position, your background lighting won't match your foreground lighting, and the illusion shatters. 

This is why controlling your environment is so important. Turn off overhead room lights if they flicker or change color temperature. Pull your window blinds closed and rely entirely on artificial strobes or continuous LED lights. Consistent light equals a seamless composite. 

Building a conceptual frame teaches you to be a director, not just a passive documentarian. You control the elements, you control the camera, and you control the narrative. Take your time placing each prop. Check your corners for distracting edges of tables or stray pieces of tape. Lock down that tripod, fix your exposure, and shoot with intention. You've got this. Let's move on to the practice brief and get your hands dirty building your own set.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
- Build drill: Stage one concept with three props—one hero, two supporting—on a tripod at base ISO (ISO 100, f/8, 1/160s). Shoot your first take, then adjust the physical staging of the props (not just your camera settings), and capture a second take. Compare both arrangements to see which one communicates your concept the clearest. Restage and refine until the image reads cleanly without needing a caption to explain it.

## 7. RECAP
- Conceptual frames are built intentionally; every prop in the frame must serve the narrative or be removed.
- Establish a clear visual hierarchy with one dominant hero prop and supporting elements that provide context without clutter.
- A clean composite requires an entirely locked-down camera setup using a sturdy tripod, manual focus, and manual exposure.
- Control your light source completely—turn off fluctuating ambient light to ensure consistent shadows and highlights across all multi-capture layers.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
- Question: Why is it critical to switch from autofocus to manual focus when shooting a multi-capture composite?
- Answer: Autofocus can slightly alter the focus distance or lens elements between shots, causing micro-shifts in framing and sharpness that ruin the alignment of the composite.
- Question: What are the three core capture rules that must remain constant when shooting elements for a composite?
- Answer: A locked-down camera, consistent lighting, and keeping camera settings (ISO, aperture, shutter speed) completely manual and unchanged.
- Question: How do you define the role of a hero prop versus a supporting prop?
- Answer: The hero prop is the main character carrying the primary conceptual weight, while supporting props provide subtle context and guide the viewer's eye without creating clutter.
