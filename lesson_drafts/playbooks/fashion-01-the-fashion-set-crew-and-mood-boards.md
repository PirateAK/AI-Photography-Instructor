# Fashion Playbook Lesson 1 - The fashion set: crew and mood boards

## 1. LEARNING OUTCOME
By the end of this lesson, the student can define the core roles on a fashion set, build a cohesive three-image mood board to align vision, and direct a test shoot while respecting creative boundaries.

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to MuskegMan Photography. Today we are diving into the world of fashion, and I want to start with a little secret. Fashion photography is not a solo sport. If you walk onto a set thinking you are the entire operation, you are going to burn out fast, and your images are going to look flat. 

Great fashion images happen when a crew clicks. Today we are going to look at the people on that set, how a mood board gets everyone marching to the same beat, and how you as the photographer lead the vision without stepping on anyone's toes. 

Let us start with the crew. On a standard fashion shoot, you have four main pillars. You have the model, who brings the clothes to life with movement and expression. You have the hair and makeup artist, often called the HMUA, who sculpts the face and hair to match the era or the vibe. You have the stylist, who pulls the wardrobe, accessories, and shoes, and makes sure the clothes fit the body correctly. And then, you have you—the photographer. 

Here is the common mistake beginners make. They try to do everyone else's job. You start adjusting the collar, or telling the model how to fix their eyeliner. Don't do that. Trust your team. The stylist owns the garment. If a zipper is puckering or a hem is crooked, that is the stylist's domain. Your job is to talk to the stylist and say, "Hey, that hem is catching the light weird, can we pin it?" You direct the overall look and the frame, but you respect your collaborators' expertise. That is how you build a reputation as a photographer people actually want to work with.

So how do we get all these creative brains pointing in the exact same direction before a single shutter clicks? We use a mood board. A mood board is simply a visual collage—digital or physical—that establishes the color palette, the lighting style, the pose references, and the overall emotional tone of the shoot. 

Think of it as your visual contract. If the stylist brings a neon pink jacket, but your mood board is all moody, desaturated greys and deep forest greens, someone didn't read the board. Before every shoot, you, the stylist, and the makeup artist should sit down with that board and agree on the anchors. 

Let us build a simple three-image board in your head right now. Image one is a color palette reference—say, muted olive green paired with warm amber light. Image two is a lighting reference—soft, directional window light coming from a 45-degree angle. Image three is a pose reference—a relaxed, candid stride down an urban sidewalk rather than a stiff, formal studio stance. 

When you share those three images with your crew, magic happens. The makeup artist knows to keep skin dewy and natural, not caked in heavy studio powder. The stylist knows to lean into earthy textures like wool and leather. And when you pick up your camera, set your aperture to f/2.8 to melt away the background, and set your shutter speed to 1/500th of a second to freeze that stride, everything clicks. 

Your images will suddenly look like they came out of a magazine, not because you spent thousands of dollars on gear, but because everyone on set shared the exact same vision. That alignment is the real superpower of a fashion photographer. 

In our practice session today, you are going to build your own three-image mood board and shoot a quick five-frame test. Take your time, communicate with your subject, and let the board do the heavy lifting. I will see you in the next lesson.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
**Mood-Board Drill:** Build a three-image mood board for a single look (include one palette reference, one light reference, and one pose reference). Then, shoot five frames of a willing subject or friend styled to match your board. Have your subject or a friend review the board against your photos to see if the vision aligned.

## 7. RECAP
- Fashion photography is a collaborative team sport involving the model, HMUA, stylist, and photographer.
- The stylist owns the garment and fit; the photographer directs the overall look and frame.
- A mood board acts as a visual contract for color, light, and tone before the shoot begins.
- A three-image board (palette, light, pose) is enough to align an entire crew.
- Clear communication prevents mismatched expectations on set.
