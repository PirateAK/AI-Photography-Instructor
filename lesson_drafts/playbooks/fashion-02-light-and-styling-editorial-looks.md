# Fashion Playbook Lesson 2 - Light and styling: editorial looks

## 1. LEARNING OUTCOME

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to the studio. Today we are stepping past basic snapshots and diving into the world of editorial fashion. If you have ever wondered why some images of clothing look flat and lifeless while others make you stop and stare, the secret isn't a magic camera body. It comes down to shaped light, disciplined styling, and absolute color accuracy. By the end of this lesson, you will know how to take a single outfit and shape three completely different visual stories using light and intent.

Let us start with how we think about light in editorial work. Commercial and editorial lighting is never accidental. Every ray of light has a job to do. In our setup today, we are working in manual mode with an ISO of 100 for maximum clarity, an aperture of f/2.8 to drop our studio background out of focus, and a shutter speed of 1/160th of a second to sync cleanly with our studio strobe. 

When you build an editorial look, you start with one key light. That is your primary light source, and you place it with a specific purpose. Maybe it is feathered across the model at a forty-five-degree angle to sculpt cheekbones and reveal the weave of a wool coat. But light alone isn't enough. If light bounces everywhere, you lose your depth. That is where negative fill comes in. 

Negative fill is simply placing a solid black V-flat or foam board just outside of the frame on the shadow side of your model. What does it do? It absorbs stray bounce light from your studio walls, deepening your shadows and instantly creating rich, dramatic contrast. You are essentially carving the model out of the darkness. Suddenly, a simple studio space feels like the pages of a high-end magazine.

Now, let us talk about what happens in front of the lens. You can have the most gorgeous light in the world, but if the styling is sloppy, the image fails. Here is our golden rule on set: styling owns the wrinkles, the tags, and the fit. The camera owns the light. 

Before you ever lift the camera to your eye, you need to scan the garment like a hawk. Are there shipping creases along the hem? Are the interior garment tags tucked away securely? Is the jacket pulling across the shoulders in a way that looks unflattering rather than intentional? Fix those details with clips, pins, and a steamer before you shoot a single frame. Do not rely on fixing wardrobe malfunctions in post-production. Your time is worth more than that, and so is your client's budget.

Speaking of clients, let us talk about the financial heartbeat of fashion photography: garment-true color. This is non-negotiable. If a customer orders a mustard yellow sweater online and it arrives looking olive green because your white balance drifted during the shoot, that item is going straight back to the warehouse. 

In fashion, our white balance discipline isn't about artistic taste; it is part of the client's contract. We never leave white balance on auto. We pull out a neutral gray card, take a custom white balance reading under our studio strobes set to 5600 Kelvin, and lock it down. When you edit, you zoom in to 100 percent on the garment seams and color swatches to verify that the fabric hue matches the physical sample sitting on your styling table. If the color is wrong, the job is a failure, no matter how cool the lighting looks.

Let us jump over to our camera simulator and put these pieces together. Watch my screen here. We are going to shoot one single outfit three distinct ways. 

For look number one, our bright commercial look, we pull our key light around to the front, remove the negative fill, and open up our modifier to a large softbox. The result is clean, airy, and shadow-light, perfect for a fast-paced retail catalog. 

For look number two, our moody editorial look, we slide that key light around to the side for dramatic side-lighting and pull our black negative fill board right up to the shadow side of the model. The contrast spikes, the fabric texture pops, and the mood turns introspective and dramatic. 

For look number three, our high-contrast graphic look, we bring the key in close, feather it sharply across the face, and let the background fall away into near-total darkness. Same model, same garment, entirely different brand identity, all built purely with light positioning.

Before you wrap your session, run your garment-true check. Pop your gray card into the frame, grab a reference shot, and verify your tones. Master this control over light, styling, and color, and you will never struggle to deliver work that commercial clients love. Let us look at your practice brief for today and get you behind the camera.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
**Look Drill and Color Check:** 
Using the camera simulator (or your own setup with a model and a single light source), shoot one outfit three distinct ways: 
1. **Bright Commercial:** Front-angled key light, minimal shadows, clean and airy.
2. **Moody Editorial:** Side-slung key light with a black foam board (negative fill) placed close on the shadow side for deep contrast.
3. **High-Contrast Graphic:** Close, feathered key light with dramatic falloff. 
Finally, execute your **Garment-True Check**: place a gray card in your scene, lock in a custom white balance, take your test shot, and zoom to 100% on the fabric seams to guarantee accurate color representation.

## 7. RECAP
- Editorial lighting is intentional: you build your scene using a single key light and shape contrast with negative fill.
- Styling is your responsibility before the shutter clicks—own the wrinkles, tags, and fit so you don't waste time fixing them later.
- Garment-true color is a legal and contractual requirement in fashion; never leave your white balance on auto.
- Always use a gray card to set custom white balance, and zoom to 100% on seams to verify color accuracy.
- You can create entirely different brand aesthetics from the exact same garment simply by moving your light source.
