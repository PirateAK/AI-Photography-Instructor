# Food Playbook Lesson 1 - The food set: surfaces, props, backdrops

## 1. LEARNING OUTCOME
By the end of this lesson, the student can assemble a versatile at-home food photography kit using two boards, three linens, and basic plates, and style a subject to control its mood and story.

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to the table. Today we are talking about the foundation of every great food photograph: the food set. 

If you have ever snapped a photo of your dinner plate under bright kitchen pendant lights and wondered why it looks a bit flat or lifeless, don't worry. That is completely normal. The problem isn't your cooking, and it is usually not even your camera. It is the environment. Today, we are going to fix that by building a simple, flexible kit using surfaces, props, and backdrops that make every dish look shootable right at home.

Let’s start with the big picture: every food photo has a hero. The hero is the food itself—that stack of pancakes, the bowl of steaming soup, or the slice of tart. But a hero needs a stage. That stage is made of two main parts: the surface your plate sits on, and the backdrop behind it. 

Think of your surfaces as the emotional dial of your image. A dark, textured surface—like a weathered slate tile or a dark stained piece of wood—absorbs light and creates a moody, dramatic story. It whispers of cozy dinners, rich spices, and evening meals. On the flip side, a light surface—like a white-washed wooden board or a pale marble slab—reflects light, creating a bright, airy, and fresh story. That is your morning yogurt bowl, your vibrant salad, your breezy brunch. 

You do not need a professional studio to pull this off. You just need a starter kit. Here is all you need to get rolling: two boards, three linens, and a couple of basic plates. 

For your boards, grab a piece of plywood from the hardware store and paint one side dark charcoal grey and the other side white or off-white. Boom—you now have a reversible surface that takes up almost no space in your closet. 

Next, grab three simple linens. Linen or cotton tea towels in neutral colors—think oatmeal, soft grey, and crisp white—are your best friends. They add soft texture and fill awkward negative space on the table. 

Finally, stick to simple plates. Matte white, matte black, and earthy stoneware are timeless. Avoid heavily patterned plates because they fight with the food for the viewer's eye.

Now, let's talk about props. Props are the supporting actors in your scene. They include things like cutlery, glasses, a stray herb sprig, or a drizzle of olive oil. The golden rule of props is simple: if it didn't help make the dish or isn't going to be eaten with the dish, think twice before adding it. Don't scatter random coffee beans around a plate of pasta just because you have them. Keep your props honest to the story. If you are shooting a bowl of soup, a crusty piece of bread and a wooden spoon make sense. A vintage camera lens in the corner does not. 

Let’s jump into the camera simulator on your screen. Notice how we have a simple bowl of soup set up. Right now, it is resting on our dark, moody surface. Look at how the shadows drop off nicely around the bowl. We are shooting at ISO 200, shutter speed 1/125th of a second, and an aperture of f/2.8 to keep the focus locked on the rim of the bowl while the background melts away into a soft blur. 

Now, watch what happens when we swap that surface out for our bright, light-washed white board. Instantly, the mood shifts. The image feels cleaner, lighter, and more energetic, even though the bowl of soup hasn't changed at all. Same dish, entirely different story. 

As you build your own kit this week, remember that perfection isn't the goal. Texture, mood, and intentionality are what turn a snapshot into a mouth-watering photograph. Take your time, play with your boards, and let your food shine. I will see you in the next lesson.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
**Set Drill**: Photograph the exact same dish on a dark surface and a light surface. Use your starter kit boards or household stand-ins like a dark baking sheet and a white poster board. 
1. Set up your dish on the dark surface and capture your moody shot. 
2. Switch to the light surface and capture your fresh shot. 
3. Load both images into the app, select the **Food style**, and check how its color-honesty priorities judge each version's color cast and white balance. Notice how the mood changes the color requirements!

## 7. RECAP
- The "hero" is your food; surfaces and backdrops are the stage that sets the mood.
- Dark surfaces create moody, dramatic stories, while light surfaces create bright, fresh ones.
- Your starter kit only needs two DIY boards, three neutral linens, and a couple of simple plates.
- Props should always support the story of the dish without distracting the viewer.
- The Food style in your app helps evaluate whether your color balance matches the mood you intended.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
1. **Question**: What is the primary difference in mood between a dark surface and a light surface in food photography?
   - **Answer**: Dark surfaces absorb light to create a moody, dramatic feel, while light surfaces reflect light to create a bright, fresh, and airy feel.
2. **Question**: What makes up the basic starter kit recommended in this lesson?
   - **Answer**: Two reversible boards, three neutral linens (tea towels), and basic matte or stoneware plates.
3. **Question**: What is the golden rule for choosing props in a food photograph?
   - **Answer**: Props should support the story of the food (like ingredients used to make it or utensils you eat it with) rather than distracting or feeling random.
