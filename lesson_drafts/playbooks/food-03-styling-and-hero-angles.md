## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to MuskegMan Photography’s Food Playbook. Today, we are talking about styling and hero angles. If you’ve ever wondered why your burger looks flat in photos compared to the menu, or why your soup looks like a muddy puddle, don't worry. This is completely normal, and it is entirely fixable with a few simple tweaks to where you put your camera and how you prep your plate.

Let's start with the foundation: angles. You only really need to know three angles to cover ninety percent of all food photography. Think of them as your reliable toolkit. 

First up is the forty-five-degree angle. This is the classic three-quarter view. It mimics how you sit down at a restaurant table and look at your plate. It is the absolute best choice for bowls, like ramen or oatmeal, because it shows both the top surface of the food and the depth inside the vessel. Set your camera on a tripod, lock your height, and position the lens so it is looking down at the food at about a forty-five-degree angle. For our demo today, I am using a 50mm prime lens on a full-frame camera, sitting at an aperture of f/4, an ISO of 400, and a shutter speed of 1/250th of a second. This gives us enough light indoors while keeping the front edge of the bowl sharp and the background softly blurred.

Second, we have the overhead angle, also known as the flat lay. This is a ninety-degree shot looking straight down. It is the king of flat foods, tablescapes, and ingredients arranged on a cutting board. Think pizza slices, charcuterie boards, or a spread of breakfast plates. When you shoot overhead, every element has equal visual weight. Just make sure your camera is parallel to the table so your horizons don't tilt. If you are shooting handheld, tuck your elbows into your ribs, take a breath, and squeeze the shutter gently.

Third is the straight-on angle, which is zero degrees. This is where your lens is completely level with the table, looking right at the side of the food. You use this for anything with height, like stacked pancakes, tall burgers, or a multi-layered cake. If you shot a towering burger from overhead, you would miss the melted cheese dripping down the side and the stacked layers of bacon. Straight-on captures that vertical drama. 

Now, how do you know which angle to pick? Simple: let the food tell you. Bowls want forty-five degrees. Flat layouts want overhead. Stacks and layers want straight-on. 

Let's talk about styling. Food styling for the camera is very different from food styling for eating. On camera, we are always hunting for the "hero bite." The hero bite is the absolute most appetizing part of the dish—the melted cheese stretch, the glistening sear on a steak, or the freshest strawberry on top of the tart. 

To make that hero bite shine, you need to pay attention to three quick rules. First, place your garnish with intention. Don't just dump chopped parsley on a soup; place individual sprigs where they draw the eye. Second, add moisture right before you click the shutter. Brush a little extra oil on roasted vegetables, or pour a fresh stream of syrup over those pancakes. Third, wipe your plate rims. Stray drops of sauce or fingerprints on the white ceramic edge distract the viewer's eye immediately. Take a damp paper towel and clean the rim before every single shot. It takes five seconds and completely changes the professional look of your photo.

Finally, let's talk about action. Some of the most compelling food photos aren't still lifes at all; they are moments in time. The syrup mid-pour, the steam rising off a hot bowl of chili, or the knife cutting through a warm lava cake. 

If you try to shoot these things *after* they happen, you'll miss them. You have to shoot *while* they happen. Switch your camera to burst mode. If you are using continuous lighting or bright window light, push your shutter speed up to at least 1/500th of a second to freeze the motion of falling sauce or flying powdered sugar. Have a helper pour the sauce while you keep your eye glued to the viewfinder, your finger holding down the shutter button to capture a rapid burst of frames. Pick the exact frame where the drip hangs off the edge of the pancake. That is your winning shot.

Today’s lesson proves that great food photos aren't an accident. They are a combination of choosing the right angle for the food's natural shape, styling the details, and timing your shutter to catch the action. Now, let’s look at your practice brief so you can try this out yourself.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Grab one prepared dish or even a simple bowl of cereal. You will shoot the exact same plate using all three hero angles: 45 degrees, overhead, and straight-on. Notice how the food dictates the best angle—your bowl of cereal will look best at 45 degrees, while a spread of breakfast items will shine overhead. Once you have your favorite angle locked in, stage an action shot. Have someone pour milk into the bowl, or cut into a piece of toast, and use burst mode at 1/500s to capture the moment live. Review your captures and pick the single frame with the best action peak.

## 7. RECAP
- Use the **45-degree angle** for bowls and depth.
- Use the **overhead angle** (90 degrees) for flat lays and tablescapes.
- Use the **straight-on angle** (0 degrees) for tall stacks and layers.
- Style for the **hero bite**: place garnishes intentionally, add fresh moisture, and wipe plate rims.
- Shoot action **while it happens** (pours, drips, cuts) using a fast shutter speed and burst mode.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
1. **Question:** Which camera angle is best suited for photographing a tall stack of pancakes?  
   **Answer:** The straight-on (zero-degree) angle, because it emphasizes vertical height and layers.
2. **Question:** What shutter speed setting is recommended when capturing fast food action, like a syrup pour or powdered sugar dusting?  
   **Answer:** At least 1/500th of a second (or faster), paired with burst mode to freeze the motion cleanly.
3. **Question:** Why should you wipe the rim of your plate before taking a food photo?  
   **Answer:** To remove stray drops of sauce or fingerprints that instantly distract the viewer's eye from the hero food.
