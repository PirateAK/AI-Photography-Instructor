## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to MuskegMan Photography’s Food Playbook. Today we tackle the side of food photography that pays the bills: the food business, from local restaurant menus to stock libraries. 

If you have made it this far, you know how to style a burger, make soup steam, and light a moody table. But when a restaurant owner hires you, they aren't looking for a wildly different artistic interpretation on every plate. They are buying consistency and color honesty. 

Let's start with rule number one of commercial food work: the menu photo must match the plate the customer receives. If you make a burger look massive, golden, and towering in your photo, but the kitchen slaps together a flat, sad version for the paying customer, the restaurant owner hears complaints. Restaurants need appetizing photos, yes, but they also need accuracy. If the sauce is deep red, your edit cannot push it toward bright orange. 

Workflow is your best friend when you are shooting a twenty-item menu in three hours. You cannot reinvent your lighting setup for every single dish. You need a repeatable setup. 

Let's look at the camera settings you will lock in for a standard tabletop menu shoot. Put your camera on a sturdy tripod—never handhold a menu shoot, because framing needs to stay identical across items. Set your camera to manual mode. Dial in your aperture at f/8. That is f/8, which gives you enough depth of field—the zone of sharp focus from front to back—to keep the entire plate sharp, from the front garnish to the back rim of the bowl. Set your ISO to 100 for maximum image clarity and zero digital grain. Now, drop your shutter speed to one-sixteenth of a second, assuming you are using a sturdy tripod and a cable release or camera timer. 

Your lighting stays in the exact same spot: a large softbox at forty-five degrees to the side, with a white foam core reflector card on the shadow side to bounce light back into the dish. Once that light is clamped down, it does not move. You simply swap the plates in and out. 

Next, let's talk about pricing. How do you charge for this? Never quote a flat fee without breaking it down. Professional food pricing has three pillars: the session fee, the per-dish editing count, and the license fee. 

Let's use real numbers. Say a local café wants ten menu items shot for their new lunch menu and website. Your session fee covers your time on site, gear wear and tear, and setup: let's price that at three hundred dollars for a half-day. Then, you charge per edited image—say, twenty-five dollars per final delivered file. For ten dishes, that is two hundred and fifty dollars. Finally, you license the images. A standard commercial license for local menu and social media use adds another two hundred dollars. Your total estimate comes to seven hundred and fifty dollars. 

When you deliver those ten images, your editing in post-production must match. If image one has warm shadows and high contrast, image ten cannot look cool and flat. Use a custom camera preset—or a synchronized develop setting in Lightroom—so the white balance and tone curves are identical across the whole batch. 

Once you master this menu workflow, those exact same images can often be licensed to stock photography agencies for secondary income, provided you have a signed model release if a human hand is holding a fork, or property releases if distinctive restaurant decor is visible. 

Clients love reliability. When you show up on time, lock your exposure at ISO 100 and f/8, deliver honest colors, and hand over a clean, professional invoice, you move from being a hobbyist with a nice camera to a trusted local business partner. Let's look at how you are going to practice this right now.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
**Business Drill:** Build a small menu-shoot estimate using the Pro pricing structure we just covered: a session fee, a per-dish editing count for five dishes, and a license fee for menu and social media use. Then, go to your kitchen, set up a tripod with your camera locked at ISO 100, f/8, and one shutter speed, and shoot five different dishes or meals using the exact same light source. Edit them in post-production so the color and contrast match seamlessly across the whole set. Remember: consistency is the deliverable the client is actually buying.

## 7. RECAP
- **Color Honesty:** The menu photo must match the actual plate served to the customer to maintain restaurant trust.
- **Locked Workflow:** Keep your tripod, aperture (f/8), ISO (100), and lighting setup completely static while swapping plates for maximum speed.
- **Professional Pricing:** Always itemize your quotes into a session fee, per-dish editing count, and usage license.
- **Batch Consistency:** Synchronize your edits across the entire menu set so the visual style remains unified from the first page to the last.

- **Question 3:** Why should you use a tripod and lock your aperture at f/8 for a multi-dish menu shoot?
