## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to MuskegMan Photography’s Macro Playbook. Today we’re closing out our series by talking about macro subjects and markets. By the end of this lesson, you’ll know where to point your macro lens to build a real portfolio, and how to shoot a consistent five-image set that clients or collectors will actually buy.

Up until now, you’ve learned the tricky mechanics of macro. You’ve wrestled with paper-thin depth of field—that’s the narrow slice of your image that’s in sharp focus. You’ve mastered manual focus, tripod setup, and artificial lighting. But a great technical shot of a random bug is just a snapshot. To turn this skill into a marketable portfolio or a satisfying creative practice, you need to know *what* to shoot and *where* those images find a home. 

Let’s start with Market Number One: nature and garden work. This is the classic macro playground, and you don’t need an expedition to the Amazon to find subjects. Your back deck, a local park, or even a potted houseplant on your windowsill will do. We’re looking for flower stamens, visiting bumblebees, and morning water droplets. 

When you’re shooting nature macro, your discipline is everything. Set your camera on a sturdy tripod. Switch to manual mode: ISO 100 for clean shadow detail, shutter speed at 1/160th of a second to freeze any gentle breeze, and an aperture of f/8 to get a reasonable plane of focus across a flower petal. Use a two-second self-timer or a remote shutter release so the mere touch of your finger doesn’t blur the frame. Your goal here isn't just a sharp bug; it's clean background separation—making your subject pop away from a buttery-soft, out-of-focus background known as bokeh.

Now let’s move indoors to Market Number Two: product detail shots for makers. This is where macro photography starts paying for itself. Local artisans, jewelry makers, leatherworkers, and potters desperately need high-end detail shots of their work. Think about a hand-knitted wool sweater, the engraved clasp of a silver necklace, or the glaze texture on a handmade coffee mug. 

For product work, consistency is your best friend. Set up a simple tabletop studio using a window for natural light, or a single desk lamp with a white diffuser paper soften the shadows. Dial in your manual settings: ISO 100, shutter speed at 1/125th of a second, and an aperture of f/11 to ensure a slightly deeper slice of sharpness across the product's clasp or weave. Pay attention to dust and lint—at 1:1 life-size magnification, a single piece of floating household dust looks like a boulder on a pristine product. Clean your subjects with a blast from a rocket blower before you even touch the shutter button.

Our third market is textures and abstract prints. This is where you can let your artistic side run wild. Walk into your kitchen and open a drawer. Look at the grain of a wooden cutting board, the crystalline structure of coarse sea salt, the woven fibers of a burlap sack, or the peeling paint on an old garden shed. These macro abstracts make incredible wall art, prints, or digital backgrounds.

Here, lighting dictates mood. Use a flashlight or your phone light held at a harsh 45-degree angle off to the side of your subject. This side-lighting skims across the surface, creating deep, dramatic shadows that emphasize every tiny ridge and valley. Stick to your reliable manual tripod setup: ISO 100, aperture f/8, and check your histogram to make sure your bright highlights aren't clipped.

So how do we tie all this together? That brings us to our final practice brief. Today, you are going to build a cohesive five-image macro set. 

Your set must include:
1. One insect or flower.
2. One texture.
3. One product detail.
4. One water droplet.
5. And one free choice of your own.

Here’s the secret, and it’s the most important takeaway of this entire playbook: the consistency of your light and color grading across all five images is what makes it a professional portfolio rather than just five lucky frames. Use the same lighting modifier, keep your color temperature consistent, and edit them with a matching contrast curve. When someone looks at your set of five, they should immediately feel that one intentional artist created them all.

You’ve built incredible technical skills through this course. You’ve conquered manual focus, learned to love your tripod, and discovered that amazing subjects are hiding right in your backyard or kitchen drawer. Now it’s time to go make something remarkable. Happy shooting!

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
- **Portfolio Drill:** Build a five-image macro set following the exact brief: one insect or flower, one texture, one product detail, one water droplet, and one free choice. 
- **The Golden Rule:** Use consistent lighting and processing across all five frames so they read as a unified portfolio rather than random snapshots. Review your set on a computer screen at 100% zoom to check sharpness and dust spots before exporting.

## 7. RECAP
- Macro subjects are everywhere: find nature in the garden, products in your workspace, and textures in your kitchen.
- Use a sturdy tripod and consistent manual settings (ISO 100, appropriate apertures like f/8 or f/11) for maximum sharpness.
- Side-lighting at a 45-degree angle accentuates surface textures and adds dramatic depth.
- A true macro portfolio is defined by lighting and editing consistency across a series of images, not just single lucky shots.
