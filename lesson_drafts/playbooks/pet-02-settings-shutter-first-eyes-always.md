# Pet Playbook Lesson 2 - Settings: shutter first, eyes always

## 1. LEARNING OUTCOME

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to the Pet Playbook. Today we put Manual mode to work on our furry family members—and I promise, by the end of this lesson you'll stop fearing that control dial. 

Let's talk about the reality of photographing pets. They do not pose on command. They sprint, they pounce, they shake off water, and they blur right across your frame. If you've ever looked at a gallery of your dog or cat and found every single action shot smeared into an unrecognizable streak, I have good news. It is not your fault, and it is entirely fixable with one setting.

Shutter speed. That is the length of time your camera sensor is exposed to light. For a sleeping cat curled on the couch, 1/125th of a second is plenty. But the moment your dog breaks into a trot or chases a toy across the rug, you need to step up your game. 

Let's set our first golden rule for action: never drop below 1/500th of a second when your pet is moving. Turn your camera dial to Shutter Priority or full Manual, and dial that shutter speed up to 1/500s. If they are sprinting full tilt across the backyard, push it to 1/1000th or even 1/2000th of a second. At these speeds, you are essentially freezing time. Every single whisker stays sharp, and every bounding leap gets captured in mid-air.

Now, freezing motion requires light. And when you force your shutter to close that fast, it lets in a lot less light. That brings us to our second tool: aperture, the opening inside your lens that lets light pass through. 

To compensate for our fast shutter speed, we want a wide aperture. That means a low f-number, like f/2.8, f/2, or even f/1.4 if your lens allows it. Not only does a wide aperture let in the maximum amount of light to keep our images bright, but it also creates a shallow depth of field. Depth of field is simply the slice of your image that is in sharp focus. When you shoot at f/2.8 indoors, your pet pops out in crisp detail, while the messy living room floor, the tangled cords, and the sofa cushions melt away into a soft, creamy blur. Suddenly, your snapshot looks like it was taken in a professional studio.

Of course, even with a wide aperture and a fast shutter speed, indoor lighting in our homes is often surprisingly dim. That brings up the third corner of our exposure triangle: ISO, which measures your camera sensor's sensitivity to light. 

Here is where many well-meaning pet parents make a mistake. They try to keep their ISO locked at 100 to avoid digital noise—that speckled, grainy look in photos. But let me give you permission to let go of that fear right now. ISO is meant to be spent freely. A noisy photo at ISO 3200 can be easily cleaned up with basic editing or modern software. A blurry, underexposed photo at ISO 100 is trash. Never sacrifice a sharp moment and a clear expression to save a little bit of grain. Push that ISO up to 1600, 3200, or wherever your camera needs it to maintain that 1/500th shutter speed. 

So we have our settings locked in: 1/500th of a second or faster, wide open aperture like f/2.8, and ISO set high enough to make the exposure work. But we have one final, non-negotiable rule before you press the shutter. Focus discipline. 

The eyes carry the whole photograph. If your dog's nose is in sharp focus, but their amber or brown eyes are soft and hazy, the photo instantly feels wrong to the human brain. We naturally connect through the eyes. When you look at a portrait of a person or a pet, your gaze goes straight to their pupils. 

So here is your absolute rule: place your autofocus point directly on the eye closest to the camera, or turn on your camera's Animal Eye AF tracking mode if you have it. If you miss the eye, you miss the shot. It really is that simple. 

In a moment, we are going to put all of this into practice with a fun hands-on drill. You'll see exactly how changing your shutter speed transforms a blurry mess into a gallery-worthy masterpiece, and how nailing that focus on the eyes brings your pet to life right off the screen. Let's get your camera set up.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
**Settings Drill: Freezing Motion and Nailing the Eyes**
1. **The Shutter Test**: Grab your camera, set it to Manual or Shutter Priority, and find a willing pet or have a family member toss a toy. Take three separate test photos during play at 1/250s, 1/500s, and 1/1000s. Review your images on the back screen at 100% zoom. Note the exact shutter speed where motion blur completely disappears and the action is frozen.
2. **The Eye Rule Test**: With your aperture set wide open (e.g., f/2.8 or the lowest number your lens permits), take two close-up photos of your pet's face. On the first shot, deliberately place your focus point on the tip of their nose. On the second shot, place your focus point directly on their nearest eye. Import both photos to your computer or view them large on your screen, zoom in 100%, and compare how the eye-focused frame immediately creates a stronger emotional connection.

## 7. RECAP
- **Shutter First**: Keep your shutter speed at 1/500s or faster anytime your pet is moving to freeze action and eliminate blur.
- **Embrace Wide Apertures**: Use apertures like f/2.8 or f/4 to let in plenty of light while melting away messy indoor backgrounds.
- **Spend ISO Freely**: Never fear digital grain—push your ISO up to 1600 or 3200 so you never lose a sharp, well-exposed shot to darkness.
- **The Eye Rule**: Always place your autofocus point directly on the pet's eye closest to the camera for maximum emotional impact.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
1. **Question**: Why is 1/125s generally a poor choice for photographing a playing dog?  
   **Answer**: Because 1/125s is too slow to freeze rapid animal movement, resulting in noticeable motion blur and smeared details.
2. **Question**: If you increase your shutter speed to 1/1000s indoors, what two camera settings can you adjust to compensate for the loss of light?  
   **Answer**: You can open up your aperture (lower f-number like f/2.8) and increase your ISO sensitivity (e.g., to 1600 or 3200).
3. **Question**: Why is focusing on the eye more important than focusing on the nose in a pet portrait?  
   **Answer**: Because human psychological connection relies on eye contact; a sharp eye creates an engaging, soulful portrait, whereas a sharp nose with soft eyes feels visually incorrect.
