## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to the Sports Playbook. I'm your instructor from MuskegMan Photography, and today we are tackling the most important part of sports photography that has nothing to do with your camera settings. We are talking about access and positions. 

Here is a common frustration. You buy a fast telephoto lens, you head out to a local soccer match or track meet, and you come home with a memory card full of tiny distant figures and blocked views. You start thinking your gear isn't good enough. But I promise you, gear is only half the battle. The absolute best sports frames in the world come from one simple thing: standing in the right spot before the play even happens. 

Let's start with access. In sports photography, access is your golden ticket. Access means where you are legally and safely allowed to stand to get the shot. Depending on the venue, you might be dealing with media credentials, which are official passes given to working photographers, or you might be shooting from public spectator areas. If you are shooting youth sports, high school games, or local races, always check in with the athletic director, coach, or event organizer first. Introduce yourself, show respect for the athletes and officials, and ask where you can safely set up without blocking spectators or interfering with the game. Staying out of the way keeps everyone safe and ensures you get invited back.

Now, let's look at sports geometry. Every sport has a shape, a rhythm, and predictable choke points where the action has to go. Think about soccer. The ball moves all over the field, but where does it have to end up to score? Right in front of the goal. Think about track and field. Runners sprint down the straightaway, but the pure drama happens at the finish line. Sports geometry means studying the field of play like a map. You want to pre-position yourself near these high-drama zones rather than chasing the ball up and down the sidelines like a panicked puppy. Let the action come to you.

Let's talk about the three core shooting angles: front-on, profile, and behind the play. Each one tells a totally different story. 

Angle number one: front-on. This is the holy grail. When you position yourself slightly downfield or past the finish line, shooting back toward the oncoming athlete, you capture their face, their strained expression, their sweat, and the pure intensity in their eyes. Fans and editors connect instantly with faces. 

Angle number two: profile. This is your side-on view as athletes run past you from left to right. Profile shots are fantastic for showing the stretching strides of a runner, the lean of a cyclist around a corner, or the dynamic arc of a basketball player driving to the hoop. Shutter speeds need to be fast here—we are talking 1/1000th of a second or faster—to freeze that lateral motion sharply. 

Angle number three: behind the play. This is when you shoot from behind an athlete, looking downfield toward their target, like standing behind a soccer goalie or a quarterback. This angle puts the viewer right inside the game, showing you what the player sees, though you lose their facial expressions. 

Finally, let's talk about pre-positioning and prefocusing. Pros don't just wander around hoping for a great frame. They pick a spot, lock their camera settings—say, ISO 400, f/2.8, and 1/1600th of a second in bright daylight—and prefocus their lens manually or with continuous autofocus on the exact spot where the runner will cross or the tackle will happen. When the play explodes right into your pre-set frame, you just press the shutter. No hunting, no guessing. 

Remember, you don't need a sideline pass at a professional stadium to practice this. You can shoot a local skate park, a community soccer match from behind the fence, or runners at a public park. The principles of geometry and emotion remain identical.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
**Position Drill:** Head out to any moving subject—skaters at a park, kids playing soccer, or cars on a local street. Photograph the exact same moving subject from three distinct positions: front-on, side profile, and behind the action. Review your images and compare which position successfully shows human effort and facial strain versus just mechanical motion. Pick your favorite angle and write down one sentence explaining why that angle captured the true emotion of the scene.

## 7. RECAP
- Great sports photography is 80% positioning and anticipation, and 20% gear.
- Always respect venue access rules, safety boundaries, and credential requirements.
- Study sports geometry to pre-position yourself where action and scoring peaks occur.
- Front-on angles capture facial emotion and strain; profile angles emphasize speed and motion.
- Pre-position and prefocus your camera so you are ready when the action arrives in your frame.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
- **Question:** Why is a front-on shooting position generally preferred over a profile position when trying to show athletic emotion?
- **Answer:** Because a front-on position captures the athlete's face, eyes, and expressions of strain, which viewers emotionally connect with much faster than side-profile motion.
- **Question:** What does "sports geometry" refer to in the context of positioning?
- **Answer:** It refers to the predictable patterns, choke points, and scoring zones (like goals or finish lines) where the action naturally converges during a game.
- **Question:** What camera preparation technique helps ensure you don't miss fast-moving action in your chosen zone?
- **Answer:** Pre-positioning and prefocusing your camera settings on the exact target area before the play arrives so your camera doesn't waste time hunting for focus.
