## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to MuskegMan Photography’s Sports Playbook. Today we are diving into Lesson 2: Shutter and anticipation: peak action. 

If you have ever tried to photograph a fast-moving soccer ball or a diving volleyball player by simply waiting for them to move and then mashing the shutter button, you already know the frustrating result. You get a blurry picture of empty space, or the back of someone's head, exactly a fraction of a second after the excitement happened. Here is the big secret of sports photography: peak action is anticipated, not reacted to. Human reaction time is simply too slow to catch a fast-moving subject after it happens. Today, we are going to fix that. We will learn how to read a sport's rhythm, prefocus on the exact spot where the magic will happen, and shoot right through the play. Let us get into it.

First, let us talk about freezing motion. When athletes move fast, your shutter speed—the length of time your camera sensor is exposed to light—needs to be blindingly fast. For most field sports, your baseline shutter speed should be at least one-thousandth of a second, written on your dial as 1/1000th. If the action is exceptionally rapid, like a sprinting track star or a fast tennis serve, you might push that to 1/2000th or 1/4000th of a second. 

Let us look at our camera simulator on screen. Notice how we are in Manual exposure mode, or Shutter Priority mode if you prefer letting the camera handle the aperture. We are dialing our shutter speed up to 1/1000th of a second. If that makes your image too dark, because 1/1000th lets in very little light, you fix it by opening up your aperture—say, to f/2.8 or f/4—or by bumping up your ISO, perhaps to ISO 800 or 1600 on a bright overcast day. Do not fear higher ISOs; a slightly noisy, tack-sharp photo is infinitely better than a smooth, blurry mess. 

Next up is your autofocus system. When athletes are charging across a field, single-shot autofocus will fail you because the distance between you and your subject is constantly changing. You need to switch your camera to continuous autofocus. On Canon, this is called AI Servo. On Nikon and Sony, it is simply Continuous AF, or AF-C. This mode tells your camera lens to constantly recalculate distance and keep tracking the moving subject as long as you hold the autofocus button halfway down. 

Pair your continuous autofocus with a zone or group autofocus area. Instead of trusting a single tiny focus point—which is way too easy to slip off a fast-moving runner—a zone uses a cluster of points. This gives your camera's internal computer a larger target to lock onto and track across the frame. Think of it as a wider net catching a fast-moving fish.

Now, let us talk about the real superpower of the sports photographer: anticipation. Every sport has a rhythm. Think of it like music or a heartbeat. A basketball player is about to jump for a layup at the peak of their stride. A soccer player is about to strike the ball when it drops from the air. A runner is about to clear a hurdle. You do not wait to see the action and then react. You watch the buildup, predict the exact moment, and prefocus on that patch of turf or hardwood where the moment will happen. 

If you are shooting soccer, do not chase the ball all over the pitch like a spectator. Pick a defensive zone or a goalmouth, prefocus your camera on that goal area where the collision or header is bound to happen, and wait for the players to enter your frame. When they step into your pre-established zone, you press the shutter down.

This brings us to our final crucial concept: burst discipline. Modern cameras can shoot ten, twenty, or even thirty frames per second. It is very tempting to hold down the shutter button from the moment the players walk onto the field until the final whistle blows. Please, do not do this. If you hold down the shutter continuously for thirty seconds, you will come home with one thousand frames of junk, your memory cards will be full, and you will spend four hours deleting duplicates of a player tying their shoelaces. 

Instead, practice burst discipline. Use short, controlled bursts of three to five frames right at the climax of the action. Start your burst a fraction of a second before you think the peak happens, and let off the button immediately after. This saves your battery, respects your memory card space, and makes editing actually fun instead of an exhausting chore.

Let us review what we covered today. You now know that human reflexes are too slow for sports, so you must anticipate the play's rhythm. You have your shutter speed locked at 1/1000th of a second or faster to freeze motion. You are using continuous autofocus with a zone area to track your subject smoothly. And you are using short, intentional bursts rather than holding the shutter down endlessly. You are no longer just taking pictures of sports; you are starting to think like a sports photographer. In our next lesson, we will look at how to use panning to introduce intentional motion blur and make your background fly by. See you then!

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
**Anticipation Drill:** Pick a repeatable, predictable moment in your environment—such as a friend jumping off a low step, a dog splashing into water, or a ball being tossed up and caught. 
1. Set your camera to Shutter Priority (Tv/S) at 1/1000s or faster, with Auto ISO enabled.
2. Switch your autofocus to continuous tracking (AF-C / AI Servo) with a zone focus area.
3. Prefocus your camera on the exact spot where the apex of the action will occur (the spot in the air where the jump peaks, or the water's surface where the splash happens).
4. Do not chase the subject with your focus; keep your frame still on that prefocused spot.
5. Shoot a short, disciplined burst of three to five frames as the action passes through your spot. 
6. Review your images afterward on your screen and find *which specific frame* in each burst holds the true peak moment. Remember: that timing sense, not fancy camera gear, is the real skill.

## 7. RECAP
- Anticipate the action rather than reacting to it, because human reaction time is too slow for fast sports.
- Set a baseline shutter speed of 1/1000th of a second or faster to completely freeze fast athletic motion.
- Use continuous autofocus (AF-C / AI Servo) paired with a zone focus area to reliably track moving subjects.
- Prefocus on a specific patch of turf or court where you know the play is heading.
- Practice burst discipline by taking short, controlled bursts of three to five frames rather than holding the shutter down endlessly.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
- **Question:** Why is relying on human reaction time a mistake in sports photography?
  - **Answer:** Human reaction time takes about 150 to 250 milliseconds, which means by the time you see the action and press the shutter, the peak moment has already passed.
- **Question:** What is the recommended minimum shutter speed for freezing standard field sports action?
  - **Answer:** 1/1000th of a second (or faster for exceptionally rapid sports).
- **Question:** What camera setting should you use to keep a moving player in sharp focus as they run toward or away from you?
  - **Answer:** Continuous autofocus (AF-C or AI Servo), ideally paired with a zone or group focus area.
