## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to MuskegMan Photography’s Travel Playbook. I'm your instructor, and today we’re tackling Lesson 1: Planning travel shoots. 

Here is a hard truth about travel photography. The best shots in the world rarely happen by accident. When you see a stunning portfolio from Iceland, or Paris, or a small fishing village right here in Alaska, you are looking at the result of homework. Travel photography starts at home, long before you ever zip up your camera bag. 

Let’s start with research. When you pick a destination, you need to hunt down two things: the icons and the corners. The icons are the famous postcards—the sweeping fjords, the classic architecture, the exact angle everyone shares on social media. Go ahead and shoot those. But then, hunt for the corners. The quiet side streets, the local markets at six in the morning, the weathered hands of a boat captain tying off a line. Your job is to capture both so your gallery tells a complete story, not just a tourist checklist.

Next up is the light's direction by hour. Light is our paint, and the sun is on a strict schedule. If you arrive at a dramatic mountain overlook at noon on a clear summer day, you’ll get flat, harsh light with deep shadows. That is the exact opposite of what you want for landscape work. Instead, use a sun-tracking app to find out where the sun rises and sets relative to your specific locations. You want to know if a monument faces east for sunrise glow, or if a canyon gets side-lit golden hour light at seven in the evening. Planning your day around the light window—that sweet 60-minute block of warm, directional light—makes the difference between a snap and a keeper.

Now, let's talk about events and permits. Nothing kills a creative mood faster than a security guard telling you tripods are banned, or arriving at a festival a day after it wrapped up. Check local event calendars before you book flights. Look into permit rules if you plan on using drones or commercial flash gear in national parks or historic sites. A quick ten-minute search online saves you hours of frustration and wasted admission fees on the ground.

With your locations and light windows locked in, you need a shot list wide enough to survive bad weather. Weather happens. Fog rolls in. Rain pours. If your entire plan relies on a single sunset view from a mountain peak, and a storm hits, you are sunk. That’s why we use the three-shot habit for every single location on our list. 

The three-shot habit is simple. At every spot, you must capture three distinct frames: a wide shot, a detail shot, and a scale shot. 

First, the wide shot. Step back, use a 24mm focal point at f/8, and capture the entire scene to set the stage. Show the viewer where they are. 

Second, the detail shot. Zoom in or walk up close. Look for textures—peeling paint on a cabin, rust on an anchor, or the intricate pattern of frost on a leaf at f/4. This grounds the viewer in the tactile reality of the place. 

Third, the scale shot. Include a human element, a boat, or a tree to show just how massive or tiny the landscape truly is. If you have all three shots, it doesn't matter if the grand view gets socked in by fog. You still walked away with a complete visual narrative of that stop.

Let's pull this all together. Travel planning isn't about killing spontaneity. It is about building a safety net so that when chaos strikes—and it will—you already know your backup spots, your backup times, and your backup frames. Take ten minutes tonight to map out your next outing, and let's get you ready to shoot smarter.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
**Plan Drill:** Choose a nearby town, park, or neighborhood that you can visit this week. Write a one-page shoot plan featuring:
1. Three specific locations.
2. The exact light window (morning, golden hour, or overcast) for each location.
3. The three-shot list (Wide, Detail, Scale) assigned to each location.
Execute your plan at one of those locations this week. When you get home, grade your plan honestly: What did your research predict accurately, and what did the weather or reality change?

## 7. RECAP
- Travel photography success starts at home with thorough research before you pack.
- Balance your shot list between famous icons and quiet, authentic local corners.
- Use sun-tracking tools to align your shoots with optimal light windows.
- Always check local event schedules and permit rules to avoid wasted time.
- Master the three-shot habit (Wide, Detail, Scale) at every stop to survive bad weather and tell a complete story.

2. *Question:* What is the main purpose of "The Three-Shot Habit"?
   *Answer:* To guarantee a complete visual story—using wide, detail, and scale frames—even if unexpected bad weather ruins your primary grand-view shot.
