# Travel Playbook Lesson 2 - The three-shot habit in the field

## 1. LEARNING OUTCOME
By the end of this lesson, the student can apply the three-shot habit—wide, detail, and scale—at any travel location using a single camera and lens combination. The student will consistently capture a narrative sequence that establishes the scene, proves personal presence, and conveys physical magnitude before moving to a new spot.

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to MuskegMan Photography’s travel playbook. Today we are talking about a simple routine that will completely transform how you capture your journeys. It is called the three-shot habit. 

Have you ever returned from a trip, dumped two hundred photos onto your laptop, and realized none of them actually tell the story of how the place felt? You have fifty shots of a mountain from slightly different angles, but zero sense of what it was like to stand at the base of that trail. We have all been there. It is a completely normal trap when you are excited and moving fast. Today, we are going to fix that for good. 

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to walk up to any location—a bustling market, an empty beach, a mountain pass—and capture a complete, compelling visual story using just three intentional frames: the wide, the detail, and the scale. 

Let us start with shot number one: the wide shot. 

Your wide shot is your establishing shot. It answers the question, "Where are we?" When you arrive at a new spot, do not just lift the camera to your eye and click. Walk around for sixty seconds. Find a foreground anchor—a weathered wooden post, a cluster of wildflowers, or an interesting rock—and place it in the lower third of your frame. 

Set your camera to aperture priority, or M mode if you are feeling confident from our last lesson. Let's dial in an aperture of f/8 for good edge-to-edge sharpness, an ISO of 200 to keep things clean, and let the camera pick the shutter speed. Frame that foreground element with your main subject—say, an alpine lake—stretching out behind it. Click. That is your establishing shot. It tells the viewer where they have landed and gives the image a sense of physical depth. 

Now, do not walk away yet. Stay right where you are for shot number two: the detail shot. 

A wide shot is wonderful for setting the scene, but it often swallows the intimate textures that make a place unforgettable. The wide shot shows you the rustic wooden cabin in the Norwegian fjord; the detail shot shows you the hand-carved dragon head on the lintel, or the condensation dripping off the single-pane window. 

Switch your lens to a tighter crop, or physically step closer if you are shooting with a prime lens. Let us open that aperture up to f/2.8 to create a shallow depth of field—meaning a nice, blurry background that isolates your subject. Drop your ISO down to 100 if the sun is bright, and set your shutter speed to one-five-hundredth of a second to freeze any subtle movement. Focus sharply on the texture: the grain of the weathered wood, the rough weave of a local fisherman's sweater, or the condensation on a cold glass of cider. Click. This frame proves you were actually there, touching the environment, not just viewing it through a glass windshield. 

Finally, we have shot number three: the scale frame. 

This is the shot that makes your viewer catch their breath. When you stand before a massive glacier or a towering redwood forest, your camera flattens the scene. Suddenly, a four-hundred-foot cliff looks like a small grey wall. To fix this, you need a known reference point. You need a human element, a vehicle, or even a brightly colored tent to show just how vast the surroundings really are. 

Position your travel companion, a fellow hiker, or even yourself down the trail. Step back with your wide lens—let's go back to f/8 and ISO 200—and place that tiny human figure at a natural intersection in the landscape, like where the forest meets the valley floor. Click. Suddenly, the sheer scale of the landscape explodes off the screen because our brains compare the massive trees to the tiny person standing beneath them. 

The magic of the three-shot habit isn't just about the technical settings. It's about discipline. When you travel with one bag, one camera body, and maybe two lenses, you cannot rely on gear variety to make your photos interesting. You have to rely on your viewpoint. 

When you arrive at a location, commit to working that single spot until you have all three frames in the digital card. Wide establishes the place. Detail proves you touched it. Scale makes the viewer feel its size. Once you have your three shots, then—and only then—are you allowed to move down the trail. 

This simple rhythm keeps you from panic-shooting fifty identical frames. It slows your brain down, helps you notice the small things, and turns a chaotic vacation snapshot collection into a cohesive, professional travel story. 

In our next lesson, we will look at how to handle shifting travel light when the weather turns on you. For now, grab your camera bag, step outside, and let's put this three-shot habit into practice.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
**Field Drill:** Choose one single location in your neighborhood or local park—a rustic park bench, a historic brick building, or a large tree. Stay in that exact spot and capture three distinct frames:
1. **The Wide:** Establish the scene using a foreground anchor (like a branch or fence post) at f/8.
2. **The Detail:** Capture a texture the wide shot swallows (like peeling paint or rough bark) at f/2.8.
3. **The Scale:** Include a person, a bicycle, or a car to show true size in the environment.

*Review:* Lay the three frames side by side on your screen. Do they tell a cohesive story of what that specific spot felt like? Finally, write down one note: what hour of the day would you return to this exact spot for better, softer light?

## 7. RECAP
- The three-shot habit consists of a wide establishing shot, an intimate detail shot, and a scale shot with a human reference element.
- Use a foreground anchor in your wide shot (f/8) to add physical depth and context.
- Open your aperture up to f/2.8 for detail shots to isolate textures and local character.
- Include a person or vehicle in your scale shot to convey the true magnitude of vast landscapes.
- Working one viewpoint completely before moving keeps your travel photography intentional and storytelling-focused.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
1. *Question:* What aperture setting is recommended for the wide establishing shot to ensure edge-to-edge sharpness?
   *Answer:* f/8.
2. *Question:* Why do we shoot a detail shot at f/2.8 instead of f/8?
   *Answer:* A wider aperture like f/2.8 creates a shallow depth of field, blurring the background and isolating textures and intimate local characteristics.
3. *Question:* What is the primary purpose of the scale shot in the three-shot habit?
   *Answer:* To use a known reference point, such as a human figure or vehicle, to convey the true physical magnitude of a vast landscape that a camera otherwise flattens.
