# Travel Playbook Lesson 3 - People and culture: consent and respect

## 1. LEARNING OUTCOME
By the end of this lesson, the student can approach, engage, and photograph local people with full ethical agency, respecting cultural norms and capturing authentic character rather than treating subjects as museum exhibits.

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to the travel playbook. Today we are tackling one of the most rewarding, and frankly, most delicate parts of traveling with a camera: photographing people and culture with consent and respect. 

Let’s be honest with each other for a second. If you have ever traveled to a place where you don't share the language, where the local dress or lifestyle is different from your own, you’ve probably felt that sinking hesitation in your gut. You see an incredible face, a craftsman bent over silver jewelry, a market vendor sorting spices under a canvas tarp, and your hand freezes over your camera bag. You wonder: Is it okay to lift the lens? Am I intruding? Am I treating this human being like a tourist attraction? 

That hesitation is actually a good thing. It means you care. Travel photographs of people carry the advanced ethics lesson's full weight. When you don't share a language, a simple click of a shutter at 1/250th of a second and f/2.8 can accidentally steal someone’s dignity. But here is the good news: you don't have to put your camera away out of fear. You just need a framework for respect. 

Let's start with beat one: learning the local norms before you ever leave your hotel room. Every culture has its own relationship with the camera. In some regions, elders or religious leaders view being photographed as a bad omen or a deep violation. In others, people are warm and welcoming, but photographing children carries strict community taboos. Before you pack your gear, spend thirty minutes reading up on local customs. Know if pointing a lens at a person, a temple, or a military checkpoint is offensive. When you respect local boundaries, you stop being a intrusive tourist and start being an informed guest. 

Now, let's talk about beat two: the approach. When you see someone you want to photograph, the golden rule is simple. Ask first. But how do you do that when you don't speak the language? 

Rely on universal human communication. Put your camera down or hold it loosely at your side, not pressed against your face like a weapon. Make eye contact. Smile. Point to the camera, touch your chest with an open hand, and raise your eyebrows with an interrogative, friendly expression. You are essentially miming: "May I take a picture of you?" 

People are remarkably good at reading intent. If they shake their head, wave a hand, or turn away, you smile, bow slightly, say thank you, and walk away immediately. No exceptions. No hiding behind a telephoto zoom lens like an a-l-l-e-g-e-d spy. If they nod, smile, or gesture toward themselves, you have consent. 

That brings us to beat three: the exchange. Photography is an interaction, not a one-way extraction. When you take something—an image—you should always give something back in return. What does that look like in practice? 

It can be as simple as showing them the back of your LCD screen immediately after you take the shot. Let them see the image. Point at it and laugh together if it's a good portrait. That shared moment breaks down any remaining tension and turns a stiff stranger into a collaborative partner. 

Another powerful form of exchange is commerce. If you are photographing a market vendor, don't stand at a distance stealing photos of their goods for twenty minutes. Step up to the stall. Buy the bag of fruit. Buy the handmade wooden spoon. Pay a fair price without aggressive haggling, and *then* ask if you can take their portrait working at their craft. You are supporting their livelihood, not just feeding your Instagram feed. 

Finally, let's talk about beat four: agency in the frame. This is where technical choices meet ethical choices. When you photograph people with agency, you are making them contributors to the frame, not exhibits in a human zoo. 

What does that mean technically? Think about your focal length and your position. Shooting someone from across the street with a 300-millimeter telephoto lens turns them into an unwitting subject in a diorama. But stepping up, kneeling down to their eye level, and engaging with an 35-millimeter or 50-millimeter prime lens creates intimacy and trust. 

Set your camera up beforehand. If you know you are walking through a lively square, dial in your settings. Let's say it's bright overcast light: keep your ISO at 200, your shutter speed at 1/500th of a second to freeze subtle movement, and an aperture of f/4 to give your subject nice separation from the background while keeping their whole face sharp. When the moment comes to ask for permission, your camera is already ready. You aren't fumbling with dials while making someone wait awkwardly in the sun. 

Compose the shot so they look at the camera with pride, or let them continue working naturally while you frame them with context—their hands, their tools, their environment. Show their strength, their skill, and their humanity. 

When you treat people with this level of respect, something amazing happens in your travel photos. The stiff, guarded look disappears from your subjects' faces. You end up with portraits that don't just show what a place looks like—they show how it feels to be welcomed there. Let's put this into practice.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
**Consent Drill:** Find a willing subject (a friend or family member) and photograph them *as* a travel portrait. 
1. Approach them formally, ask for permission with a smile.
2. Take the portrait using a natural focal length (like a 35mm or 50mm perspective) at f/4 to balance subject sharpness and environment.
3. Show them the result on your screen and learn at least one specific detail about their day, hobby, or background to use as a future caption.
4. Open your positions document from the Advanced Ethics lesson and write down your personal, non-negotiable travel photography consent rule.

## 7. RECAP
- Research local cultural norms and taboos before traveling so you never unknowingly cause offense.
- Always ask for permission using universal gestures and smiles when language barriers exist; respect a "no" immediately without exception.
- Treat photography as an exchange by showing the back of your screen or supporting local commerce.
- Frame people as willing contributors with agency and dignity, not as anonymous exhibits in a tourist catalog.
- Set your camera exposure parameters in advance so you can focus entirely on human interaction when the moment arrives.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
1. **Question:** What is the rule if a potential subject declines your request for a photograph with a head shake or wave?
   **Answer:** Smile, nod, say thank you, and walk away immediately without attempting to covertly shoot with a long lens.
2. **Question:** Why is showing the back of your camera LCD screen to your subject considered a best practice?
   **Answer:** It completes the human exchange, builds trust, breaks down tension, and transforms the interaction into a collaborative moment.
3. **Question:** How does using an environmental aperture like f/4 instead of an extreme close-up telephoto lens help with ethical travel portraits?
   **Answer:** It encourages you to step closer, engage respectfully, and include cultural context rather than isolating a subject like an unwitting specimen from a distance.
