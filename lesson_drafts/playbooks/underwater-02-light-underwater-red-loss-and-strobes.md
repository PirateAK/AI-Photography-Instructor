## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to MuskegMan Photography’s Underwater Playbook. Today, we are tackling the most dramatic challenge of diving with a camera: light underwater. Specifically, why everything turns a monochromatic blue-green, and how we fix it using physics, distance, and strobes. 

Let’s start with a simple truth. Water is heavy, dense, and acts like a giant color filter. As sunlight travels down into the ocean, the water molecules selectively absorb wavelengths of light based on their color. Red light—which has the longest wavelength—disappears first. At a mere one meter deep, a good portion of the warm red spectrum is already gone. By five meters, your red lips look gray, your colorful coral looks muddy brown, and by ten meters, everything is dipped in an eerie blue-green wash. 

Red loss isn't a flaw in your camera sensor. It’s just physics. And no, white balancing in post-processing won't magically bring back reds that never reached your subject in the first place, because multiplying zero by ten is still zero. 

So, how do we fight back? Our first line of defense is the most important rule in underwater photography: the minimum-water rule. Simply put, you want as little water as possible between your lens and your subject. If you are shooting a sea turtle five meters away, your lens has to peer through five meters of water-filter. But if you get within half a meter, your lens shoots through just half a meter of water. The difference in color fidelity and contrast is staggering. Move your feet, not your zoom ring. Get close, and then get closer.

Of course, even when you are close, natural sunlight simply isn't enough to restore vibrant color at depth. That’s where artificial light comes in—specifically, underwater flashes called strobes. Strobes are external flashes housed in waterproof casings that fire a burst of full-spectrum white light, replacing the missing colors right at the subject. 

When you turn your strobes on, you have to manage your camera's manual exposure in a very specific way. Remember our baseline settings from the previous lesson? We set our camera to manual mode, ISO 200 to keep digital noise low, and a shutter speed of 1/160th of a second to freeze movement and darken the ambient water background. 

Why 1/160th? Because shutter speed controls ambient light—the natural sunlight lighting up the ocean behind your subject. A faster shutter speed like 1/160th drops the background water down to a rich, saturated dark blue. Aperture and your strobe's power output control the subject itself. 

Now, if you just point your strobes straight ahead like car headlights, you will run into a major underwater enemy: backscatter. Backscatter happens when your strobe light bounces off tiny particles, suspended sediment, and plankton floating in the water directly in front of your lens. The result looks like a snowstorm of glowing white dots ruining your frame. 

The fix is as simple as it is physical. You pull your strobe arms outward and angle the strobe heads slightly outward, about 45 degrees away from the housing. You want the inner edge of the strobe beam just kissing the edge of your subject, while the center of the beam misses the water directly in front of your lens. This lights up your subject with the edge of the light beam, keeping the floating particles in the dead zone dark and invisible. 

Let's put this all together for a macro or wide-angle shot. You spot a vibrant nudibranch on a reef wall. You swim in close—minimum water. You frame the shot with your camera locked at ISO 200, 1/160th of a second, and an aperture of f/8 to give your subject sharp focus. Your strobes are mounted on arms, pulled wide, and pointed slightly outward. You press the shutter. The strobe fires, injecting pure white light that paints the nudibranch in brilliant reds and yellows, while the ambient sunlight exposes the surrounding water as a deep, gorgeous blue. 

It takes a little practice to coordinate your body position, buoyancy, and lighting angles all at once, but once you master red loss and strobe positioning, your underwater images will transform from murky blue snapshots into professional, vibrant works of art.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
- **At-Home Drill**: Grab a bright red object—like an apple or a red toy. Fill a clear glass baking dish or a deep tub with water. Shoot the object through increasing depths of water or through stacked layers of slightly tinted glass, observing how the red color dulls and shifts toward gray-blue as distance and medium thickness increase.
- **In-the-Field Drill**: On your next dive, commit to the minimum-water rule. Take three photos of the same subject: one from two meters away, one from one meter away, and one from thirty centimeters away. Notice how color and contrast dramatically improve the closer you get, even without strobes. Then, turn on your strobes, push the arms wide, angle them outward to eliminate backscatter, and watch your reds pop against a rich blue background.

## 7. RECAP
- Water selectively absorbs red light within the first few meters, turning underwater scenes blue-green.
- The minimum-water rule states you must get as close as possible to your subject to minimize the water filter effect between your lens and your target.
- Shutter speed (set to around 1/160s) controls the ambient background blue water, while your strobes and aperture light up the subject.
- Angling your strobe heads outward prevents light from bouncing off suspended particles, eliminating unsightly backscatter.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
- **Question 1**: Which color of the visible light spectrum is absorbed first as light travels underwater?
  - *Answer*: Red light is absorbed first, disappearing within the top few meters of the water column.
- **Question 2**: Why does setting your shutter speed to 1/160s help control the background in underwater photography?
  - *Answer*: A fast shutter speed reduces the amount of ambient sunlight entering the camera sensor, darkening the background water to a rich, saturated blue.
- **Question 3**: What causes backscatter when using underwater strobes, and how do you fix it?
  - *Answer*: Backscatter is caused by strobe light reflecting off floating particles directly in front of the lens. You fix it by pulling your strobe arms wide and angling the strobe heads outward so only the edge of the light beam hits the subject.
