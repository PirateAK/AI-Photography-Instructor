# Underwater Playbook Lesson 3 - Buoyancy and technique: shooting while diving

## 1. LEARNING OUTCOME
By the end of this lesson, the student can maintain neutral buoyancy to hover motionless in the water column while operating a manual underwater camera system without touching the reef or marine life. The student can use their entire body as a living tripod to capture sharp, well-composed images from level and upward-looking angles.

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to the Underwater Playbook. I’m your instructor from MuskegMan Photography, and today we are tackling the most important skill in marine imaging. You can own the sharpest lens and the most powerful strobe on the market, but if you drift into a coral head or bounce up and down like a yo-yo, your photos will suffer. 

Here is our golden rule for today: underwater photography is a diving skill first. 

Before we ever touch a camera dial, let's talk about the non-negotiable prerequisite. This playbook assumes you are a certified diver with solid foundational skills. If you are struggling with your air consumption or your fin pivots, put the camera on the boat for one more dive and sort your buoyancy first. Safety, marine conservation, and personal well-being always come before a photograph. We never, ever touch the reef. Touching damages fragile ecosystems and ruins your shot because you are suddenly scrambling to push away.

So, how do we stay still when there is no ground to stand on and no tripod legs to lock down? Simple. Your body is the tripod. 

Let's look at neutral buoyancy—your ability to hover effortlessly at a set depth without sinking or floating. You achieve this through a mix of correct weighting, fine control of your buoyancy compensator device, and most importantly, your lungs. Your lungs are your fine-tune control knob. Inhaling makes you rise slightly; exhaling makes you sink. When you see a subject you want to shoot, you don't grab your inflator hose. You take a gentle, half-breath in or out to settle your hover, and you hold that breath for just a couple of seconds while you squeeze the shutter. 

Let's talk about body mechanics and posture. Most beginner underwater photographers make the mistake of kneeling in the sand or propping a hand on a rock. Don't do that. Sediment gets kicked up instantly, turning crystal-clear water into a murky soup, and you risk crushing marine life. Instead, trim yourself out horizontally. Keep your knees bent at a ninety-degree angle, with your fins up high away from the bottom. This frog-kick posture keeps your wash away from the reef and puts your center of gravity right where you need it. 

Now, how do you compose when you can't walk left or right? You move your whole body. 

On land, you adjust your tripod legs or step back three paces. Underwater, you are swimming your camera. If you need to move three inches to the left, you make a tiny sculling motion with one hand or give a subtle puff of air. You frame the shot with your entire physical frame. 

Let's talk angles. When you shoot downward from above, your subject blends right into the background rocks or sand. It's flat, dull, and lacks depth. But when you drop down low—sometimes right onto your belly in an open sandy patch—and shoot level or upward toward the surface, magic happens. You get gorgeous separation between your subject and the open blue water behind it. That upward angle lets the natural sunlight wrap around your subject, giving you a clean, professional silhouette or a bright, high-contrast portrait.

Let's lock in our camera settings for this kind of active environment. You are moving in a current, and your subject might be swaying, so we need settings that freeze motion. Dial your camera to Manual mode. Set your shutter speed to 1/160th of a second to freeze both your minor hand movements and any fin movement from your subject. Set your aperture to f/8 to give yourself enough depth of field—the zone of sharp focus—to keep your subject's eye and body completely sharp. Let your ISO float around 200 to 400 depending on how bright the ambient light is at your current depth. 

When you frame up your shot, remember that you are fighting water movement. Take a breath, let half of it out, lock your core, and become a statue. Squeeze the shutter gently. Don't jab it. A sudden jab moves the camera housing and blurs your image, no matter how fast your shutter speed is. 

The stillness you practice in the water column is the exact stillness that gives you razor-sharp images. When you master your buoyancy, you stop fighting the ocean and start dancing with it. In our next lesson, we will look at how to balance this natural ambient light with artificial strobes, but for now, let's focus entirely on holding your position. 

Head to the learner practice section for today's drill, and I'll see you on the next dive.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
**The One-Meter Hover Drill (Certified Divers Only, Pool or Confined Water Practice Recommended First):**
Get neutrally buoyant in a safe, open area away from sensitive marine growth. Hover completely motionless at a depth of one meter off the bottom for a full sixty seconds without touching the bottom, adjusting your BCD, or sculling with your hands. Once you achieve this stillness, bring your camera to your eye, compose a shot of a stationary object like a training weight or dive flag, and squeeze the shutter three times while maintaining your absolute stillness. 

## 7. RECAP
- Scuba diving safety and reef conservation are always the primary prerequisites before bringing a camera underwater.
- Your body is your tripod; master neutral buoyancy and use your lung volume for fine depth control.
- Adopt a horizontal frog-kick posture with knees bent and fins up to protect the reef and prevent kicking up sediment.
- Shoot from level or upward-looking angles to create stunning separation between your subject and the background.
- Set your camera to Manual with 1/160s shutter speed and f/8 aperture to freeze motion while drifting.
