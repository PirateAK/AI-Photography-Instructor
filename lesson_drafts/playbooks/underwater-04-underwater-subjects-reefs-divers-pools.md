## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to our final dive into underwater photography. Today we put everything together by looking at our actual subjects: vibrant reefs, diving companions, and the humble swimming pool. I promise, by the end of this lesson, you'll know exactly how to approach each one without stressing over your settings.

Let’s start with the golden rule of this entire genre. The reef's welfare outranks every frame. Period. No photograph is worth a broken piece of staghorn coral or a stressed marine creature. When you are shooting reef life and behavior, your buoyancy control is your number one tool. You want to hover three feet off the bottom, perfectly weighted, with your fins up and away from the delicate ecosystem. Never touch the reef to steady yourself. If you need stability, use a single two-finger touch on a barren rock, far away from any living coral. 

When you frame a reef scene, look for natural framing elements like a coral arch or a swim-through. Let’s say you are shooting a colorful parrotfish nibbling on algae in bright, shallow water at 15 feet deep. Dial your camera to ISO 200, set your shutter speed to 1/250th of a second to freeze your slight hand movements, and drop your aperture to f/8 for sharp details across the coral head. If the colors look a bit flat because water strips away red light, turn on your strobes—those external underwater flashes—and position them out wide at ten o'clock and two o'clock to light the subject without backscattering off floating sediment. 

Next up: divers in the frame. Adding a human element transforms a simple fish picture into a story about exploration and scale. A massive barrel sponge or a towering kelp forest doesn't look enormous until you place a diver next to it. When you work with a dive buddy, communication is everything. Before you drop below the surface, talk through your hand signals. Agree on what "look at me," "stop," and "move left" look like underwater. 

Let’s dial in the settings for a diver portrait. You are hanging suspended at 30 feet, and your buddy is swimming past a gorgonian fan. Set your shutter speed to 1/125th of a second to capture their motion without severe blur, and open your aperture up to f/5.6 to softly blur the background blue water, making your buddy pop. Keep your ISO at 400. Frame them slightly off-center using the rule of thirds, and wait for the magic moment when they exhale so their regulator isn't hiding their face behind a wall of bubbles. Tell a story: show them looking at a tiny nudibranch, or pointing out into the deep blue blue yonder. That human connection draws any viewer right into your image.

Now, what if the ocean is blown out by a storm, or you just want to practice your strobe angles and buoyancy without the pressure of a current? Enter the pool: the controlled studio. Pools are your secret weapon for mastering technique. You don't have to worry about surge, changing light, or wandering marine life. 

Drop into a local pool with a willing friend, a weighted dummy, or even a brightly colored waterproof toy on a stark white background tile. Treat the pool like your dry-land photo studio. Practice your minimum-water discipline—that means getting as close as physically possible to your subject to minimize the amount of water your flash has to travel through. If you are four feet away from your subject, water is absorbing most of your light and contrast. If you scoot in to twelve inches, your strobes will render punchy, saturated colors and razor-sharp details. Use this pool time to experiment with turning your strobes inward at 45-degree angles, or pulling them back behind your housing arms for a dramatic sidelighting effect. 

Mastering the pool directly translates to commercial success. Dive shops, tourism boards, and marine conservation organizations are always hunting for honest, beautiful underwater imagery. They don't want heavily photoshopped stock photos; they want authentic shots of divers practicing good buoyancy, beautiful coral ecosystems, and safe interactions with marine life. And because these organizations care deeply about their reputation, that market strongly rewards certified, careful shooters who respect the ocean. When a dive shop hires you, they know you won't crash into the reef or harass a turtle for a shot.

So, let's look at how you are going to practice this. Your mission for this week is a dedicated pool session. Treat it as your controlled studio. Set up a subject on a white pool floor or tile background, practice your strobe positioning, and relentlessly drill your minimum-water discipline until moving close to your subject feels like second nature. Then, take that confidence out into the open water. Respect the reef, tell a great story with your dive buddy, and capture the underwater world the way it deserves to be seen. You've got this. Now go get wet.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Book a one-hour session at a local swimming pool to use as your controlled studio. Place a high-contrast subject (like a brightly colored waterproof object) on a white or light-colored pool background. Practice your minimum-water discipline by positioning your dome port within 12 inches of the subject. Drill your strobe positioning—moving them outward and inward—to see how changing the angle affects contrast and eliminates backscatter in a clear-water environment.

## 7. RECAP
- The reef's welfare always outranks every photograph; never touch living coral to steady your shots.
- Use divers in the frame to provide a sense of scale and tell a compelling human story about ocean exploration.
- Treat swimming pools as your controlled studio to practice buoyancy, strobe angles, and close-focus technique away from ocean currents.
- Minimize water distance between your lens and the subject to maximize color saturation and sharpness.
- Conservation organizations and dive shops reward certified, ethical shooters who produce honest, responsible underwater imagery.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
1. **Question**: Why is minimum-water discipline important when shooting underwater subjects?
   - **Answer**: Because water absorbs light and scatters particles; getting physically closer to your subject reduces the amount of water light must travel through, resulting in sharper images and better color saturation.
2. **Question**: What are recommended starting settings for shooting a stationary reef subject in shallow, bright water?
   - **Answer**: ISO 200, 1/250s shutter speed to freeze motion, and f/8 aperture for adequate depth of field across the coral.
3. **Question**: How should external strobes be positioned when shooting a reef scene to prevent backscatter?
   - **Answer**: Positioned out wide and angled slightly outward or forward (like ten o'clock and two o'clock) so the light illuminates the subject without reflecting off floating sediment directly into the lens.
