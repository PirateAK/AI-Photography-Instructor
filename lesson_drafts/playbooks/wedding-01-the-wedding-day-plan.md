## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to the Wedding and Event Playbook. I’m your instructor here at MuskegMan Photography, and today we are tackling lesson one: The Wedding Day Plan. 

If you think wedding photography is purely about pointing a fancy camera at a couple and pressing the shutter, I have a gentle secret to share with you. A great wedding gallery is actually won hours, days, and sometimes months before you ever step foot inside the venue. It is won in the planning. 

Let's start with a hard truth. Weddings run late. It is practically a law of physics. The hair and makeup artist runs thirty minutes behind schedule, the zipper on the dress sticks, or traffic stalls the groom’s party. If your timeline is built like a house of cards, one little breeze brings the whole thing tumbling down. And who do people blame when the portraits get rushed? Usually, it's the person holding the camera. 

So, how do we fix this? We build a timeline with built-in safety cushions. When you sit down with your couple during the final consultation, do not just ask them what time the ceremony starts. Walk backward through the day. If the ceremony starts at 3:00 PM, we need guests seated by 2:45 PM. That means family portraits need to wrap up by 2:15 PM so everyone can freshen up. That means getting ready shots need to finish by 12:30 PM, giving us room to breathe and travel to the venue. Always pad your transition times by fifteen minutes. If you finish early, nobody ever complains about having a little extra time to breathe or grab a sip of water. If you finish on time because you padded the schedule, you look like an absolute rockstar.

Now let’s talk about the shot list. Every wedding photographer has a nightmare about missing *that one* photo — grandma walking down the aisle, the emotional tear during the vows, or the exact moment the ring slips onto the finger. A wedding is a live theatrical performance with zero understudies and no second takes. You cannot tap the bride on the shoulder and say, "Hey, sorry, my memory card corrupted during the kiss, can we run that back real quick?" 

To prevent this, you need a master shot list, but keep it realistic. Do not hand your clients a Pinterest board with five hundred specific poses. Instead, focus on the core narrative pillars: getting ready details like rings, shoes, and dress hanging up; candid interactions with the wedding party; the ceremony setup and key emotional beats; the reception details before guests enter; and the major milestones like toasts, cake cutting, and the first dance. Keep your must-have list locked down to twelve core items that you review the night before. Everything else is a creative bonus.

Next up is the beast that strikes fear into the heart of even seasoned professionals: family formals. Twenty people, six different family dynamics, two divorced sides who might not want to stand next to each other, and an uncle who mysteriously vanishes to the bar the second family photos are called. 

The secret to managing family groupings is not photography skill; it's crowd control. Before the wedding day, you write down a strict, linear family shot list. Start with the largest group — usually the couple with both entire extended families — and peel people away one by one. "Alright, grandparents, thank you so much, you can head to cocktail hour now. Parents, stay right here." This keeps the momentum moving. If you stand there shouting, "Does anyone want a picture with the bride?" you will lose control of the room in forty-five seconds. Assign a designated family wrangler from the couple's side who knows everyone's names and can track down wandering uncles while you stay behind the camera, ready to shoot.

Finally, we have to talk about gear, scouting, and contingencies. A professional doesn't hope things go right; a professional plans for what happens when things go wrong. Do you have a backup camera body in your bag? Yes. Do you have dual card slots recording every shot simultaneously? Absolutely. But gear redundancy is only half the battle. 

You also need to scout your venue light beforehand, or at least arrive an hour early to walk the grounds. Look for where the harsh midday sun creates brutal shadows, and find open shade where you can place your subjects. Pre-decide your contingencies for weather and schedule slips. What happens if it pours rain during your outdoor portrait session? Do you have an indoor backup nook scoped out? 

To help you lock this in, use our app’s camera simulator right now to pre-test your three riskiest frames. First, look at the backlit altar scene to nail your exposure compensation. Second, dial in your settings for the dim indoor hallway where natural light barely reaches. Third, practice tracking a moving subject in the fast first-dance simulator so you don't panic when the lights drop low and the spotlight swings. 

When you plan for the worst, you free your mind up to capture the magic. You’ve got this. Let’s get into the practice drill.

## 7. RECAP
- **Pad your timeline:** Always build fifteen-minute buffer zones into your schedule to absorb unavoidable delays like hair and makeup overruns.
- **Keep a core shot list:** Limit your unmissable list to twelve foundational narrative pillars so you never miss a vital moment.
- **Master family formals:** Start with the largest extended family group and peel people away systematically to maintain order and speed.
- **Anticipate failure with contingencies:** Scout your venue light early and have pre-determined indoor backup plans for bad weather or low-light surprises.
- **Test your settings early:** Use the camera simulator to pre-test tricky scenarios like backlit altars, dim hallways, and fast dances before game day.
