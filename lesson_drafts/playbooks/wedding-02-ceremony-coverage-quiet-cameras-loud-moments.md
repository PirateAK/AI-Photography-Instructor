## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to MuskegMan Photography’s Wedding and Event Playbook. Today we’re tackling the heart of the entire wedding day: ceremony coverage, where the cameras must be quiet and the moments are loud. 

The ceremony is the unrepeatable core. There are no second takes when the rings go on, or when the couple shares their first kiss. But shooting a ceremony often means battling difficult light, strict venue rules, and heart-pounding pressure. Don't worry, friend. Every wedding photographer has felt that flutter in their chest during the processional. We’re going to walk through how to handle it calmly and professionally.

Let’s start with where you stand. The biggest enemy at the altar isn't lack of light; it's bad direction of light. Most churches or indoor venues have massive windows right behind the altar. If you stand in the aisle and shoot straight toward those windows, your couple turns into dark silhouettes against a blinding white sky. That’s backlight, where the primary light source is behind your subject. 

Instead, you need to pre-position for the light before the processional even starts. Walk the perimeter. Find an angle forty-five degrees off to the side of the altar. Now, that window light rakes across the couple's faces, sculpting their features instead of blowing out behind them. If you must shoot head-on into the backlight, dial your exposure compensation to minus one. Exposure compensation is a camera setting that darkens or brightens your overall image. Dropping it by one full stop protects the detail in a white wedding dress from turning pure, textureless white. 

Next up: the rule of silence. Many venues—churches, temples, historic estates—strictly forbid the mechanical *clack-clack-clack* of a traditional shutter during quiet vows. If your camera sounds like a machine gun, the officiant might stop the ceremony and ask you to step outside. That’s a nightmare we can easily avoid. 

Switch your camera from mechanical shutter to electronic shutter. An electronic shutter uses the image sensor to capture the image silently with zero moving parts, letting you shoot completely undetected. But electronic shutters come with a hidden trap in indoor lighting: flickering bands from fluorescent or LED fixtures. Keep your shutter speed at one-two-hundred-and-fiftieth of a second, or drop it to one-one-hundred-and-twenty-fifth if you're working in 60Hz or 50Hz alternating current regions, to banish those ugly lines.

Now, what about the dim interior light once you've gone silent? Do not fear high ISO. ISO is your sensor’s amplification of light, and a slightly grainy photo is infinitely better than a blurry, motion-smudged photo. Let that ISO climb to 3200, or even 6400 if the venue is a dim, candlelit chapel. Modern editing software handles ISO grain easily, but it cannot fix motion blur. 

Let’s talk about framing. When the vows start, zoom in or step closer. Compose your shots tight. The vows are all about faces, tears, smiles, and micro-expressions. Wide shots show the venue, but tight shots carry the emotional weight of the day. 

When you frame tight, depth of field gets razor-thin, especially if you're shooting at an aperture like f/2.0 or f/2.8. Depth of field is the zone of acceptable sharpness from front to back in your image. At wide apertures, that zone might only be a few inches deep. That means your focus point has to be laser-accurate. 

Here is the golden rule for portraits and vows: the near eye rules. Always place your autofocus dot directly on the eye closest to your lens. If the groom is facing the bride, and she is closer to you, lock your focus on her near eye. If the focus drifts to the tip of a nose or a stray lock of hair, the entire emotional connection of the shot evaporates. 

Let’s put this all together. You’re pre-positioned at a forty-five-degree angle to dodge harsh altar backlight. Your camera is in electronic silent mode. Your ISO is resting comfortably at 3200, your shutter speed is locked at one-two-hundred-and-fiftieth of a second, and your focus point is hovering right over the near eye. You aren't just surviving the ceremony anymore. You’re capturing it with confidence. Let's head over to the practice drill and put these exact settings into play.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Simulate the altar problem using your camera simulator or your own gear in a low-light room:
1. **Set the Scene:** Create a backlit subject by placing a lamp or window behind your practice subject.
2. **Exposure Setup:** Dial your exposure compensation to **−1** to protect the highlight areas (like a white dress or shirt). Set your **ISO to 3200** and your shutter speed to **1/250s**. 
3. **Check the Histogram:** Watch the histogram guide on your screen. Verify that the graph isn't slamming hard against the right side (blown highlights) while accepting the balanced exposure trade.
4. **Focus Drill:** Walk your single autofocus dot directly onto the **near eye** of your subject. Take three consecutive test shots focusing strictly on that eye while framing a tight, emotional half-body portrait.

## 7. RECAP
- Pre-position at a 45-degree angle to window-backlit altars, or use −1 exposure compensation to protect highlights.
- Switch to electronic shutter for silent venue compliance, keeping shutter speeds steady (like 1/250s) to avoid LED flicker.
- Embrace a high ISO (3200 or higher) rather than risking motion blur or underexposure in dim indoor venues.
- Compose vows tight—faces carry the emotional weight of the ceremony.
- Always place your autofocus point on the near eye; the closer eye rules every time.
