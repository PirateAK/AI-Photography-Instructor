# Wedding & Event Playbook Lesson 3 - Reception: flash, floors and candid energy

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to the Wedding and Event Playbook. Today we are stepping into the hardest lighting challenge of the entire day: the wedding reception. 

If you have ever walked into a hotel ballroom at eight o'clock at night, you know the feeling. The lights are dim, fairy lights are twinkling, DJ lasers are cutting through the air, and your camera is struggling to find focus. It feels like a panic-inducing dark cave. But take a deep breath. By the end of this lesson, you are going to look at a dark dance floor and see a playground of creative opportunity. 

Let's start with a quick reality check. Receptions are dark, fast, and wonderfully joyful. The formal events like the first dance might be choreographed down to the final dip, but everything happening around them is gloriously chaotic. And that chaos? That is where your album lives. 

To tackle this room, we need to talk about light. You cannot rely on ambient light alone unless you want images covered in gritty, muddy digital noise. But you also cannot just blast a direct on-camera flash straight into people's faces, or you will create harsh shadows and look like a tourist at a birthday party. 

Instead, we use bounce flash or off-camera flash. Bounce flash means pointing your speedlight—that is a portable battery-powered flash unit—upward toward a white ceiling or wall behind you. The light bounces off that large surface and rains down softly over your subject, wrapping them in flattering illumination. 

Let's look at the camera simulator on screen right now. We are in a simulated dark reception hall. If we shoot at ISO 1600, f/2.8, and 1/200th of a second with no flash, the background is completely black and the couple looks muddy. Now, let's turn on our flash set to 1/4 power and tilt our speedlight head up at a forty-five-degree angle behind us, bouncing it off a white tent ceiling. Look at that instant transformation. The couple is crisp, and the room starts to fill in. 

Now, let's talk about the crown jewel of reception photography: the first dance. The biggest mistake beginners make here is freezing everything too cleanly. They shoot at 1/250th of a second, ISO 6400, and f/2.8. Sure, the couple is sharp, but the background looks dead, black, and flat. The warm glow of the fairy lights and the moody ambiance of the venue completely disappear. 

The secret weapon here is a technique called "dragging the shutter." Dragging the shutter simply means using a slower shutter speed than normal to let the ambient background light register on your sensor, while your flash fires a quick pulse of light to freeze your main subject. 

Let's test this in the simulator. Drop your shutter speed down from 1/250th to 1/60th of a second. Keep your aperture at f/2.8 and your ISO at 800. Fire the flash at 1/4 power. Look at what happens to the background. Those background lights turn into warm, glowing streaks of ambient color, and the fairy lights bloom. Meanwhile, the flash freezes the couple sharply in the center of the frame. That is how you get professional reception magic. You get sharp people and a glowing, alive room. 

Of course, dragging the shutter requires a steady hand. At 1/60th of a second, any movement of your own body will blur the background even more. If the couple spins fast and you want to lean into that artistic motion smear, let's walk our shutter speed down even further to 1/30th of a second. Watch the simulator screen. The motion blur on the dancer's dress becomes a gorgeous, painterly streak, while the flash catches a crisp ghost of their face in the middle of the spin. It is moody, it is editorial, and clients love it. 

Once the first dance wraps up, the real party begins. This is where your dance floor candids come into play. Here, you want to walk your shutter speed depending on the energy of the room. If folks are jumping up and down and sweating under strobe lights, a shutter speed of 1/60th might give you too much accidental smear. Walk your shutter up to 1/125th or 1/250th of a second. 

Let's watch how the simulator changes as we dial the shutter up to 1/250th. The ambient background gets a bit darker, but every flying hand, laughing mouth, and spilled drink is frozen in absolute crisp detail. There is no right or wrong answer here; it is an artistic choice. Ask yourself: does this moment need high-energy clarity, or does it need moody, glowing motion? You decide based on the song and the vibe.

Finally, let's talk about the spaces between the scheduled moments. The speeches are over, the cake has been cut, and everyone is mingling. Don't put your camera down and head to the bar just yet. Some of the best candid frames happen when people think nobody is watching. 

Walk the perimeter of the room with your 35mm or 50mm prime lens—that is a fixed focal length lens that doesn't zoom. Keep your camera set to ISO 3200, f/2, and 1/160th of a second, relying on the soft ambient room light without flash so you don't ruin the mood. Look for the bridesmaid laughing with her shoes off in the corner. Look for the proud grandfather watching the dance floor from a quiet table. These unguarded frames tell the true story of the night. 

Receptions can feel intimidating because they move fast, but remember: you are in control of the light. Bounce your flash, drag your shutter, and chase that candid energy. Let's look at how you are going to practice this right now.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Open your camera simulator and load the Reception preset scene. 
1. **The First-Dance Drill:** Set your camera to manual mode, ISO 800, f/2.8, and a shutter speed of 1/60s. Turn on your flash simulator and set it to bounce mode. Fire at the spinning couple to practice the drag-the-shutter technique. Watch how the background glows while the flash freezes the subject. Then, drop your shutter to 1/30s to observe how the motion smear changes.
2. **The Candids Drill:** Transition to the high-energy dance floor scene in the simulator. Walk your shutter speed progressively from 1/60s up to 1/250s. Compare the frames side-by-side and decide which shutter speed best captures the story of the room's energy without losing the ambient vibe.

## 7. RECAP
- **Bounce your flash:** Point your speedlight toward ceilings or walls to create soft, flattering, room-filling light instead of harsh direct beams.
- **Drag the shutter:** Drop your shutter speed down (around 1/60s) to let ambient room light and fairy lights glow, while your flash freezes your main subject.
- **Control motion energy:** Use slower shutters (1/30s - 1/60s) for artistic dance motion blur, and faster shutters (1/125s - 1/250s) to freeze high-energy action.
- **Hunt for unguarded moments:** Keep shooting between formal events to capture the candid, emotional storytelling frames that clients cherish most.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
- **Question:** Why do we use bounce flash instead of direct on-camera flash during a dark reception?
  - **Answer:** Direct flash creates harsh, unflattering shadows and dark backgrounds, whereas bounce flash redirects light off a large surface (like a ceiling or wall) to softly wrap the subject and illuminate the surrounding space.
- **Question:** What is the "drag-the-shutter" technique and what problem does it solve?
  - **Answer:** Dragging the shutter means using a slower shutter speed (such as 1/60s) to let ambient room light and background glow register on the sensor, solving the problem of a pitch-black background while using flash to keep the main subject sharp.
- **Question:** If you want to eliminate accidental motion blur on a very wild, fast-moving dance floor, which direction should you move your shutter speed?
  - **Answer:** You should increase your shutter speed (for example, moving from 1/60s to 1/250s) to freeze fast action, accepting a slightly darker ambient background in exchange for crystal-clear sharpness.
