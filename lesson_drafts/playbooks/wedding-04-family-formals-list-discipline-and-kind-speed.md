# Wedding & Event Playbook Lesson 4 - Family formals: list discipline and kind speed

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to MuskegMan Photography’s Wedding and Event Playbook. Today, we are tackling family formals. Let’s be honest: family formals are where weddings often slow to a painful crawl. Aunt Susan wanders off to the bar, Uncle Bob is MIA, and everyone starts looking exhausted. But it doesn't have to be that way. 

Here is our core philosophy for today: family formals are pure logistics. They are not an artistic photo shoot; they are an exercise in military-grade organization delivered with a warm smile. A standard family formal list of eight groupings should take you about twenty minutes. The written list makes it possible, and your kind speed makes it pleasant. By the end of this lesson, you will know how to command a group of twenty people without ever raising your voice or losing control of your timeline.

Let's start with your secret weapon: the family spokesperson. Never attempt to yell out family names yourself. You don't know who "Cousin Dave" is, and your face will be buried behind a camera. Before the wedding day, you ask the couple to assign one assertive, loud-voiced family member from each side—someone who knows every face and isn't afraid to shout across a lawn or a hotel lobby. Your spokesperson holds the master list and rounds up the next group while you are still finishing the current one. 

Now, how do we build that list? We always work from the outside in. We start with the largest, most complex groups—usually the couple with both immediate families, grandparents, and siblings. Then, we peel people away layer by layer. Grandparents leave first so they can sit down and rest. Extended family leaves next. Then siblings and their partners. Finally, you end with just the couple on their own. This outward-in method means the oldest and youngest guests are released early, keeping everyone happy and cooperative.

Let’s talk camera settings, because technical hiccups will kill your momentum faster than a missing uncle. When you are shooting groups of people standing two rows deep, you need enough depth of field—that's the zone of sharp focus from front to back—to keep every single face crisp. Set your camera to manual mode. Dial your aperture to f/5.6. On a standard full-frame or crop-sensor camera, f/5.6 gives you plenty of wiggle room so that the front row and the back row are both in sharp focus, even if someone leans forward. 

Next, lock down your exposure. Set your shutter speed to at least one-two-hundred-and-fiftieth of a second (1/250s) to freeze any laughing, blinking, or shifting kids. Set your ISO to match the lighting—let's say ISO 400 if you're in open shade outside, or ISO 800 if you're inside a hotel ballroom with dim overhead lights. Put your autofocus on face-detection or continuous tracking, and leave your camera settings alone for the rest of the session. You have enough to manage without touching dials.

Once the camera is locked and the spokesperson is rounding up bodies, you run a strict, four-step choreography for every single grouping: announce, arrange, shoot, and release. 

Step one: Announce. Your spokesperson calls out the next grouping clearly. 

Step two: Arrange. You guide them quickly into position. Keep your directions simple and positive. Step tall, tuck shoulders back, bring your hands together. Never grab people; use physical gestures and your voice to shape the group. 

Step three: Shoot. Take three to five frames in rapid succession. Why multiple frames? Because in group shots, blinking is guaranteed. If you take five shots spaced one second apart, I guarantee you at least one frame will have every single eyeball open and every smile looking natural. 

Step four: Release. Dismiss the people who are leaving the frame immediately, and call in the next additions. 

Here is the golden rule of the formal station: check your written list between groups, not your camera’s LCD screen. When you stop to pixel-peek at the back of your camera, you break the rhythm. The family relaxes, people start chatting, and the kids run away. Trust your f/5.6 aperture, trust your lighting, and keep your eyes on your clipboard, not your playback button. 

Let's look at a quick simulation on screen. Notice how the spokesperson herds the parents into the center, the couple stands proud in the middle, and the siblings flank the edges. My aperture is locked at f/5.6. I take four quick frames, verify everyone's eyes are open by feel and pace, and immediately call out the next line on my paper list. Twenty minutes flat, and we are done.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Write out your own eight-grouping family formal list starting from the couple outward (e.g., Couple + both extended families down to just the couple). Next, set up your camera or simulator at f/5.6, ISO 400, and 1/250s. Practice the four-step discipline—announce, arrange, shoot, and release—using household objects or mannequins for two rows of faces. Remember: check your written list between groupings, not your LCD screen!

## 7. RECAP
- Treat family formals as logistics and organization, not a spontaneous portrait session.
- Always recruit an assertive family spokesperson to manage the crowd and call out names from your master list.
- Build your shot list from the outside in, releasing grandparents and extended family members early.
- Lock your camera settings to f/5.6 for guaranteed two-row depth of field, 1/250s shutter, and appropriate ISO.
- Follow the four-step rhythm: Announce, Arrange, Shoot, and Release—and check your list, not your LCD.

- **Question:** What is the correct order for building a family formal shot list?
  - **Answer:** Outward in—starting with the largest, most complex groups (including extended family and grandparents) and ending with just the couple.

  - **Answer:** Check the written list on your clipboard, not the camera's LCD playback screen, to keep the energy and pace moving forward.
