## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to the Wedding and Event Playbook. Today we are stepping outside the traditional wedding venue and looking at corporate functions, private parties, and product launches. 

If you have spent any time shooting weddings, events beyond the altar might feel a little strange at first. There is no white dress, no first dance, and often no emotional peak moment to anchor your storytelling. But I promise you, corporate events share the exact same DNA as weddings. The difference is the pressure: here, consistency completely overrides artistry. 

When a corporate client hires you, they do not want twenty wildly different artistic interpretations of a keynote speaker. They want clean, bright, professional documentation that matches their brand guidelines, and they want it delivered on time. The brief defines the deliverables, your shot list becomes branding-adjacent, and your core job is to lock in the correct exposure and make it repeat scene after scene. 

Let us start with decoding that brief. When you get an event inquiry, look past the schedule and hunt for the non-negotiables. Is this a tech conference where sponsor banners must be sharp and legible in every room? Is it a product launch where attendees need to look engaged while holding the new item? Your shot list should mirror these priorities. Start wide to establish the room and the scale, move to medium to show human interaction, and finish tight on details like branded napkins, podium logos, and networking handshakes. 

Next up is your camera setup. Event spaces are notorious for shifting light. You might walk from a bright window-lit registration desk straight into a dark, blue-lit keynote auditorium, and then finish in a warm, amber-lit networking lounge. If you shoot in aperture priority or auto mode here, your camera will constantly get fooled by dark suits or bright stage lights, leaving you with wildly inconsistent exposures. 

This is where we go fully manual. Let us set up a rock-solid baseline for a typical ballroom interior. Pop your camera into Manual mode. Set your shutter speed to one-two-fiftieth of a second—1/250s—to freeze hand gestures and minor movement. Set your aperture to f/4. That gives you enough depth of field to keep two people in focus during a conversation without blowing out the background entirely. Finally, set your ISO to 1600. In a modern mirrorless or DSLR body, ISO 1600 gives you clean files while letting you shoot comfortably in dim hotel ballrooms. 

Now, let us talk about color and branding. Corporate clients are intensely sensitive to color accuracy, especially skin tones and brand logos. Do not leave your white balance on auto if the room has mixed tungsten pot lights and daylight spilling through glass doors. Pick a custom Kelvin value—say, 4200K—or lock your white balance to a preset like fluorescent or daylight, and stick to it across the room. If the color shifts wildly from one frame to the next, editing a thousand images becomes a nightmare. Consistency in camera saves hours in post-production.

When you are walking the floor, your pacing matters. You are not hiding in the bushes with a telephoto lens like a documentary wildlife photographer. You are a professional moving through a working space. Keep your camera at eye level, smile at your subjects, and anticipate the handshakes, the laughter, and the speaker gestures before they happen. If you see a key executive mingling, wait for a natural pause in the conversation before stepping in for a clean, direct-eye-contact portrait. 

Remember, mistakes at corporate events are normal. If you take a batch of photos under heavy magenta stage lights and realize your colors look muddy, do not panic. Take a breath, pull out a gray card, reset your white balance, and move forward. Every pro event shooter has a handful of frames from every gig where the light caught them off guard. The trick is how fast you recover and lock your settings back down.

In just a moment, we are going to look at the practice drill that ties all of this together. You are going to take your camera and lock down an event-standard shooting workflow across three different lighting zones in your own space. Let us jump into the visuals.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
**Consistency Drill:** Grab your camera and set it to manual exposure (ISO 1600, 1/250s, f/4) and a locked custom white balance (e.g., 4200K). Photograph three completely different 'scenes' in your home or office: one near a bright window, one in a dim corner, and one under mixed indoor lighting. Your goal is to hold the exact same color and exposure language across all three environments—treating your settings as a rigid delivery standard rather than a creative choice.

## 7. RECAP
- Corporate events prioritize branding and delivery consistency over pure artistic experimentation.
- Always translate the client brief into a structured shot list covering wide, medium, and detail frames.
- Lock your exposure in manual mode (e.g., 1/250s, f/4, ISO 1600) to combat shifting indoor lighting.
- Fix your white balance to a specific Kelvin value or preset to maintain uniform skin tones and accurate brand colors across all images.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
1. **Question:** Why is manual exposure generally preferred over aperture priority when shooting corporate events in mixed-lighting ballrooms?
   **Answer:** Because automatic metering modes can be easily fooled by dark clothing, bright stage lights, or background windows, resulting in inconsistent exposures that require extensive editing.
2. **Question:** What is a recommended starting manual exposure triad for a typical dimly lit indoor corporate venue?
   **Answer:** Shutter speed at 1/250s (to freeze minor motion), aperture at f/4 (for adequate depth of field), and ISO 1600 (for a clean image in low light).
3. **Question:** How does an event shot list differ fundamentally from a wedding shot list?
   **Answer:** An event shot list focuses heavily on branding-adjacent elements like sponsor logos, executive interactions, and detailed room setups, prioritizing client deliverables over emotional storytelling moments.
