## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to MuskegMan Photography’s Wedding and Event Playbook. Today we tackle the part of the job that keeps you in business long after the shutter stops clicking: the business of events. 

I know, I know. You got into this because you love capturing moments, not because you love spreadsheets. But hear me out. Great art without a business plan is just an expensive hobby. If you undercharge for an eight-hour wedding, work sixty hours editing, and deliver four thousand un-culled jpegs three months late, you will burn out. Fast. Today we fix that. By the end of this lesson, you will know how to package your services, manage your workflow, pay your second shooters fairly, and ask for referrals without feeling awkward. Let’s build a sustainable business together.

Let’s start with package architecture. Event clients get overwhelmed by endless options. They want clarity, and you want profitability. That means you build your packages around two non-negotiable pillars: coverage hours and deliverable counts. 

Deliverable counts mean the exact number of final, edited images the client receives. Never promise "all the good photos." That is a trap that leads to delivering twelve hundred repetitive shots of people eating dinner. Instead, set firm boundaries. For a standard corporate event or elopement, build an entry tier: four hours of continuous coverage, one photographer, and two hundred fifty fully edited, high-resolution digital files delivered via an online gallery in three weeks. 

Then build your premier tier: eight hours of coverage, two photographers, six hundred final deliverables, and a two-week turnaround. By anchoring your packages to concrete numbers, you protect your time. If the client wants the speeches covered past your contracted eight hours, your contract already specifies an overtime rate of one hundred fifty dollars per hour. Clear boundaries keep everyone happy.

Next, let's talk about second-shooter economics. When you scale up to multi-hour weddings or large corporate galas, you need a second shooter—another professional photographer working under your direction. 

Never treat a second shooter as cheap labor. Pay them a fair day rate—typically between thirty-five and fifty dollars an hour depending on your market, or a flat rate of three to four hundred dollars for an eight-hour day. Make sure your agreement specifies who owns the copyright—usually you license their images for your client gallery—and who carries liability insurance. Crucially, your second shooter should never deliver raw files directly to the client. They hand their cards to you at the end of the night, you ingest them into your master catalog, and you maintain complete quality control over the final product.

Speaking of quality control, let’s look at your post-event workflow. Turnaround time is where client trust is won or lost. If you promise a three-week turnaround, you must hit it. 

Here is a tested workflow to make that happen without losing your weekends. Step one: ingest and backup. The very night of the event, dump all dual-card backups onto two separate hard drives. Never format your in-camera SD cards until those drives are verified. Step two: culling. Use software like Photo Mechanic or Lightroom to quickly flag the keepers and ruthlessly discard duplicates, blinks, and test shots. For an eight-hour event yielding three thousand clicks, your goal is to cull down to about eight hundred selects. Step three: batch editing. Apply your signature color profile, adjust exposure and white balance across the entire set, and export your final selects as high-resolution jpegs. Step four: upload to your client gallery platform.

Which brings us to the most profitable five minutes of your entire client relationship: the delivery email and the referral ask. 

When you upload that gallery, your client is experiencing the absolute peak of emotional goodwill. They are reliving the best day of their year. Do not bury the delivery link at the bottom of a cold, robotic email. Write warmly. Say something like: "Hi Sarah, your gallery is finally ready! Reliving these moments brought back all the joy of your event. You can view, share, and download your high-res files right here using your PIN: 1234."

And right there, in that exact same email while they are riding the high of seeing themselves looking amazing, you make the referral ask. Keep it simple and helpful: "If you know any friends, colleagues, or family members planning an event this year, I would love to take care of them just like I took care of you. Feel free to pass along my website or forward this email." 

That simple sentence generates more organic business than any paid Instagram ad ever will. People love sharing great recommendations. You just have to give them the opening.

You’ve made it through the entire playbook. You know how to scout locations, master on-camera flash, direct nervous subjects, handle chaotic timelines, and now, run a profitable business. Take a breath. You are ready. Let's look at your final practice brief.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Build two distinct event packages from the Pro pricing lesson—defining your coverage hours, exact deliverable counts, and turnaround timelines. Next, write your personal client delivery-email template, incorporating the referral ask at the moment of maximum goodwill. ## 7. RECAP
- Anchor your event packages to two concrete pillars: specific coverage hours and fixed deliverable counts.
- Protect your sanity and timeline by promising realistic, guaranteed turnaround windows (e.g., 2 to 3 weeks).
- Treat second shooters as professional partners with clear day rates, copyright agreements, and in-person card handoffs.
- Execute your post-event workflow methodically: dual-drive backup, ruthless culling, batch editing, and cloud gallery delivery.
- Always make the referral ask in your delivery email while client emotional goodwill is at its absolute peak.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
- **Question 1**: Why should you avoid promising "all the good photos" to an event client?
  - *Answer*: It sets an undefined boundary that often leads to delivering excessive, repetitive images and creates unnecessary editing bloat.
- **Question 2**: What is the safest protocol for handling second-shooter memory cards at the end of an event?
  - *Answer*: The second shooter hands their physical cards directly to the lead photographer, who ingests them into the master catalog and verifies dual-drive backups before formatting.
- **Question 3**: When is the best time to ask a client for a referral?
  - *Answer*: In the initial delivery email, when the client is opening their final gallery and experiencing peak emotional goodwill.
