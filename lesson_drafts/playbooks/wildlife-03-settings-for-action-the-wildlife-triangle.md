# Wildlife Playbook Lesson 3 - Settings for action: the wildlife triangle

## 1. LEARNING OUTCOME
By the end of this lesson, the student can configure manual exposure and continuous autofocus for fast-moving wildlife, prioritizing a shutter speed floor of 1/1000s and accepting ISO noise over motion blur.

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to MuskegMan Photography. Today we are tackling the trinity of fast wildlife photography: shutter speed, aperture, and ISO, working together as the wildlife triangle. 

If you have ever stared at a blurry photo of an eagle taking flight or a fox darting through the brush, you already know the heartbreak of getting the composition right and the settings wrong. Wildlife runs on shutter priority in spirit, even when you are shooting in full manual. Today, we fix that blur once and for all.

Let us start with the non-negotiable floor of your exposure: shutter speed. Shutter speed is simply how long your camera sensor stays exposed to light. For static wildlife sitting on a branch, you might get away with 1/250th of a second. But the moment that animal decides to move, stretch, preen, or run, 1/250th is a recipe for disaster. 

Here is your golden rule for action: set your shutter speed floor to 1/1000th of a second. Write that down. For fast-flying birds or running mammals, bump it up to 1/2000th or even 1/3200th of a second. 

Let us look at this in our camera simulator right now. Watch what happens when I dial our shutter speed from 1/250th up to 1/1000th. The image darkens immediately because the sensor is getting less light. But look at how crisp that running wolf cutout becomes in our simulated frame. That is the sharpness we are chasing.

Now, how do we fix that dark image without lowering our shutter speed? That brings us to our second point: aperture. Aperture is the size of the opening inside your lens that lets light in, measured in f-stops. In wildlife photography, we rarely stop down to f/8 or f/11 unless we are shooting a landscape. Instead, we want to stay near our lens is widest end—f/4, f/5.6, or whatever your telephoto lens gives you wide open. 

Why wide open? Two reasons. First, telephoto lenses are expensive pieces of glass, and shooting wide open lets in the maximum amount of light to feed that fast 1/1000th shutter speed. Second, a wide aperture creates a shallow depth of field, which is a photography term for the slice of your image that is in sharp focus. A shallow depth of field melts your background into a creamy, beautiful blur, making your subject pop right off the screen. 

Let us check our simulator. We are at 1/1000th of a second, and our aperture is wide open at f/4. The background behind our simulated bear is a soft, green wash, while every hair on its snout is tack-sharp. 

That leaves us with our third element: ISO. ISO is your sensor is sensitivity to light. For years, photographers feared high ISO because older digital cameras produced ugly, colored grain called digital noise. But modern sensors—and modern editing software—handle noise brilliantly. 

Here is the absolute core philosophy of the wildlife triangle: noise beats blur, always. A noisy, sharp photo of a bear catching a salmon can be cleaned up with AI noise reduction in Lightroom or Topaz. A blurry photo of a bear catching a salmon is garbage. End of story. 

So how do we manage ISO? We set an ISO ceiling. If you are shooting in Manual mode with Auto-ISO enabled, set your maximum limit to ISO 6400 or even 12800 depending on your camera body. Let the camera float its sensitivity up as the light drops, ensuring your shutter speed never dips below that 1/1000th floor. 

Let us watch what happens in the simulator as clouds roll in over our scene. Our shutter stays locked at 1/1000th. Our aperture stays locked at f/4. The camera automatically pushes our ISO from 800 up to 6400. Yes, we get a little speckled noise in the shadows. But the soaring hawk in our frame remains razor-sharp. That is a winning trade every single time.

Of course, settings mean nothing if your camera cannot track the action. That brings us to autofocus and drive modes. You want your camera set to continuous autofocus—often called AI Servo on Canon or AF-C on Nikon and Sony. This means your camera continuously recalculates focus as the animal moves toward or away from you. 

Pair continuous AF with a zone or expanded flexible spot autofocus area. Do not use single-point autofocus for erratic wildlife unless the animal is completely still; give your camera a small cluster of focus points to grab onto fur or feathers. 

Finally, set your drive mode to high-speed continuous burst. When the eagle dives or the wolf leaps, you do not want to be timing a single click. You want to hold the shutter down and let your camera capture eight to twelve frames per second. Out of ten frames in a burst, maybe two are masterpieces, five are decent, and three miss the mark. That is professional wildlife shooting in a nutshell.

Let us pull it all together into the Wildlife Style recipe: shutter speed at 1/1000th or faster, aperture wide open at the lens is limits, ISO allowed to climb up to 6400, continuous autofocus with a tracking zone, and high-speed burst mode engaged. 

In a moment, we are going to put this into practice with our drill. Do not worry if your first few shots feel overwhelming. Dial in these numbers, trust your gear, and let us go make some sharp images.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Load the Wildlife style recipe into your camera: set your shutter speed to a 1/1000s floor, open your aperture as wide as your lens allows, and set your Auto-ISO ceiling to 6400. Now, run the dog-or-bird drill at home: find a moving pet or a backyard bird, switch your autofocus to continuous (AF-C / AI Servo) with a zone, and shoot a high-speed burst as they move. Count your sharp frames. Next, raise your shutter speed by one full stop (e.g., from 1/1000 to 1/2000) and run the burst drill again, counting your sharp frames. Notice how your hit-rate changes with a higher shutter floor.

## 7. RECAP
- Set a strict shutter speed floor of 1/1000th of a second or faster for any active wildlife.
- Keep your aperture wide open (f/4 or f/5.6) to maximize light intake and create a beautiful, blurred background.
- Embrace digital noise: noise beats motion blur every single time, so let your ISO climb.
- Use continuous autofocus (AF-C / AI Servo) paired with a zone tracking area to follow erratic movement.
- Shoot in high-speed burst mode to dramatically increase your chances of capturing the defining moment.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
- Q: Why is 1/250th of a second generally too slow for active wildlife?
  A: Because animal movements like wing flaps, head turns, or running strides happen too quickly for 1/250th, resulting in motion blur.
- Q: What does "noise beats blur, always" mean in practical terms?
  A: It means it is better to have a sharp image with some digital grain (high ISO) than a blurry image that is completely unusable.
- Q: Which autofocus mode should you choose when tracking a moving animal?
  A: Continuous autofocus (AF-C or AI Servo) with a zone or expanded tracking area, so the camera constantly adjusts focus as the subject moves.
