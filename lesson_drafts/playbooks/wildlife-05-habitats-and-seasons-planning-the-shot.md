## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT
Welcome back to the Wildlife Playbook. Today we are tackling our final, and perhaps most important, lesson: habitats, seasons, and planning the shot. 

If you spend enough time looking at incredible wildlife images online, it is really easy to fall into the trap of thinking great photographers just wander out into the woods, stumble upon a majestic bull moose in the morning mist, and casually snap a masterpiece. I am here to let you in on a little secret from years of shooting up here in Alaska. Luck has almost nothing to do with it. The stunning shot you see is almost always the result of rigorous research, careful planning, and putting yourself in the right place at the exact right moment. The animal always decides the moment; your job is simply to decide to be there when it is most likely to happen. By the end of this lesson, you will know how to build a complete wildlife shoot plan so you stop guessing and start creating.

Let us start with step one: species research. You cannot photograph an animal successfully if you do not understand how it lives. Before you ever pack a bag, you need to become an amateur biologist for your target species. Are you chasing bald eagles during the winter salmon runs? Are you tracking brown bears during the mid-summer peak of the berry season? Every species has seasonal rhythms. A golden eagle in November behaves very differently than a nesting pair in May. You need to know their breeding cycles, their migration routes, and their daily routines. Do they feed at first light and bed down by ten in the morning? Do they move along ridgelines when the wind is coming from the west? Take notes. Read local naturalist reports. Talk to park rangers. When you know your subject's habits, you can anticipate their next move instead of chasing them from behind.

Step two is mapping your location and your light. Wildlife photography is fundamentally landscape photography with a moving subject. You need to visit your location before the shoot—or at least study topographical maps and satellite imagery meticulously. Where is the high ground? Where are the natural pinch points like river crossings or narrow valleys? More importantly, you need to plan your light window. We talked about golden hours in earlier lessons—that soft, warm light in the first hour after sunrise and the last hour before sunset. But with wildlife, light direction is everything. You want the sun at your back or side to illuminate the fur or feathers, not blindingly behind your subject throwing them into a harsh silhouette unless that is the artistic choice you are intentionally making. Check your weather app, track the sunrise angle, and know your exact framing angles before you even step foot in the field.

Step three is assembling your shoot plan. This is your written blueprint for success. A good wildlife shoot plan covers six essential elements: the target species, the exact geographical location, the target season or month, your ideal light window, your approach route—keeping wind direction in mind so you do not scent-alert the animal—and finally, the specific behavior you are hoping to capture, like a fox hunting in deep snow or a loon feeding its chicks. When you commit these details to paper, your brain stops worrying about logistics and starts focusing entirely on composition, exposure, and anticipation. 

Let us talk about what happens when you finally execute that plan in the field. You have done your research, you are locked into your manual settings—say, ISO 800, f/4, and 1/1600th of a second to freeze action—and you are waiting quietly in your blind or shooting position. The animal appears. Here is the golden rule of wildlife planning: the plan gets you to the scene, but patience keeps you there. Sometimes the red fox does not cross the ridge where you expected. Sometimes the bear sleeps twenty minutes longer. That is normal, and it is never a failure of your plan. Your plan simply gave you a fighting chance to be present for the magic. When the animal does something unexpected, adapt gracefully. Breathe, keep your hands steady on your 100-400mm zoom lens, and remember that every outing teaches you something new about the habitat. 

You now have the entire playbook in your hands. Get out there, plan your next session with intention, respect your wild neighbors, and capture some incredible images. We will see you in the field.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE
Write a one-page wildlife shoot plan for a local species in your area. Your plan must include: target species, exact location, season, light window, approach route (accounting for wind), and the specific behavior you are hoping to witness. Then, execute at least one shooting session against your plan. When you return home, review your results honestly: what did your research predict accurately, and what did the animal teach you that your plan missed?

## 7. RECAP
- Great wildlife photography is built on preparation and research, not random luck.
- Study your target species' seasonal cycles, breeding patterns, and daily routines before heading out.
- Map your location and light windows in advance to ensure optimal sun angle and wind direction.
- Write a concrete shoot plan covering species, location, season, light, route, and target behavior.
- Let your plan get you into position, but remain patient and flexible when the animal dictates the real-world moment.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK
1. **Question:** Why is wind direction an important factor to include in your shoot plan when approaching wildlife?
   **Answer:** Many animals rely heavily on their sense of smell to detect predators or humans; planning your approach so the wind blows from the animal toward you (or across) prevents them from scent-alerting and fleeing before you arrive.
2. **Question:** What is the primary purpose of writing out a wildlife shoot plan?
   **Answer:** It eliminates logistical guesswork in the field, allowing you to focus your mental energy on camera settings, framing, composition, and anticipating animal behavior.
3. **Question:** True or False: If an animal does not behave according to your pre-planned expectations, your shoot plan has failed and the session is wasted.
   **Answer:** False. The plan is a tool to put you in the right place at the right time; wildlife is unpredictable by nature, and adaptation is a core skill for every successful wildlife photographer.
