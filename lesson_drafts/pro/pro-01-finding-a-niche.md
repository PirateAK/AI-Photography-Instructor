# Pro Lesson 1 — Finding a Professional Niche

Class: Pro
Prerequisite: Advanced class

## 1. LEARNING OUTCOME

By the end of this lesson, learners can describe a professional niche they could develop — grounded in their interests, strengths, potential clients, and preferred working style.

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT

**Welcome**

You can photograph anything and still need to be known for something. Clients hire the photographer whose name comes to mind for THEIR problem. This lesson finds that name — not by chasing trends, but by reading four inputs you already have.

**The four inputs: interests, strengths, clients, style**

Interests: what do you photograph when nobody pays you? Strengths: what do your best-reviewed images have in common — the personal-style audit from the Advanced class is the evidence. Clients: who needs this work repeatedly — not one buyer, a MARKET of them? Working style: do you thrive under event pressure, in quiet studio control, on location at dawn? A niche that fights your working style won't survive its first busy season.

**Comparing three genre scenarios**

Three photographers, three shapes of business. The wedding specialist: high-emotion people work, weekend-heavy, referrals drive everything. The commercial product specialist: weekday studio rhythm, business clients, repeatable briefs and deliverables. The local documentarian: varied commissions, community reputation, slow trust, long relationships. Same skills, different lives. None is better — each fits a different person.

**Where the four inputs intersect**

The niche lives where all four overlap. Interests without clients is a hobby. Clients without interest is a job you'll grow to resent. Strengths without style is inconsistent. Write the overlap in one sentence: "I make [kind of photographs] for [kind of clients] in [kind of conditions]."

**Testing a niche against reality**

Before committing, test it: does the market have enough recurring demand? Can you name five potential clients in it? Does your current portfolio support the claim — or is there a gap to fill deliberately? A niche statement you can't defend with existing work is a goal, not a niche — fine, as long as you know which one it is.

**Writing the niche statement**

One sentence, specific enough to act on: "I create natural-light portraits of families and small businesses in coastal towns." You can widen later. Starting narrow is how the first referrals happen.

**Learner practice**

Write your niche statement using the four inputs, then list five real potential clients or buyers for it. If the list is hard to finish, the niche is a wish — adjust until the names come easily.

**Recap**

Skills are generalist; businesses are specialist. Read your interests, strengths, clients, and working style; write the overlap as one sentence; test it against a market of five real names. The niche you can defend is the one that grows.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE

Write your niche statement from the four inputs, then pressure-test it:

1. List your last thirty photos' subjects (the Advanced style audit provides this).
2. Name the client type who buys that work repeatedly.
3. Describe the working conditions honestly — hours, pressure, travel.
4. Write the one-sentence niche.
5. List five real potential clients. If five names won't come, revise the niche toward the names that do.

## 7. RECAP

Clients hire the specialist. Read four inputs — interests, strengths, clients, working style — write their overlap as one sentence, and test it against a market of five real names. Narrow first; widen later.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK

- Business scenarios are illustrative shapes, not earnings claims; no income figures are stated or implied.
- Market demand varies by region and season; the five-client test is a locality exercise, not a guarantee.
- Do not present any genre as more viable than another — fit is the lesson's premise.
