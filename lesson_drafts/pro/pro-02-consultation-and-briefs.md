# Pro Lesson 2 — Client Consultation and Briefs

Class: Pro
Prerequisite: Pro Lesson 1

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT

**Welcome**

An inquiry that starts "how much for photos?" ends badly unless it grows into something clearer first. The client brief — goals, audience, deliverables, schedule, expectations — is the document that makes every later decision defensible. Contracts quote it; shoots follow it; delivery checks against it.

**The five questions every inquiry must answer**

One: GOAL — what is the photograph for? A website hero, a magazine page, a gift print — three different jobs. Two: AUDIENCE — who sees it, and what should they feel or do? Three: DELIVERABLES — how many images, what formats, what sizes, usage where? Four: SCHEDULE — shoot date, selection date, delivery deadline, and anything fixed like an event. Five: EXPECTATIONS — locations, style references, who approves, what "done" means. Vague answers are answers too — they tell you how much communication this client needs.

**Listening for the unsaid**

Clients describe outcomes awkwardly. "We want something clean and modern" is information — ask for two or three reference images and listen to what they like in them. Note the words that repeat. The brief you write back should use THEIR vocabulary, not photography jargon: "bright and airy" belongs in the brief; "high-key, low contrast" belongs in your head.

**Writing the brief back to the client**

After the conversation, send the brief in writing: your understanding of the five answers, in plain language, ending with "correct me where I've misread this." The reply loop is the consultation's real product — it converts assumptions into agreements before they become arguments.

**Demonstration: inquiry to brief**

A real-shaped inquiry: "We're a small bakery launching a website — need some photos." The five questions, asked in two friendly exchanges: goal — website and social for the launch; audience — local walk-in customers; deliverables — around twenty images, web-sized, plus a few print-worthy; schedule — shoot in two weeks, delivery before launch; expectations — warm, natural, the real shop rather than styled perfection. The written brief fits on one page. The client corrects one line — the launch moved a week. That's the system working.

**When the brief changes**

Briefs change; that's normal. What's not normal is silent change. When scope moves — more images, another location, a moved date — the brief is updated in writing and the estimate is re-checked. The document isn't bureaucracy; it's the memory both parties agreed to.

**Learner practice**

Take a real or invented five-line inquiry and conduct the five-question consultation in writing. Produce the one-page brief, send it to a friend to play the client, and let them "misunderstand" something — then fix it in writing.

**Recap**

Five questions — goal, audience, deliverables, schedule, expectations — turn curiosity into a brief. Write it in the client's vocabulary, send it back for correction, and update it in writing when scope moves. The brief is the skeleton the contract and the shoot hang on.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE

Turn an inquiry into a brief:

1. Write (or find) a five-line client inquiry.
2. Conduct the five-question consultation — in writing, in the client's vocabulary.
3. Produce the one-page brief: goal, audience, deliverables, schedule, expectations.
4. Send it to a friend as the client and ask them to misunderstand one line.
5. Correct the brief in writing. That loop is the lesson.

## 7. RECAP

Five questions — goal, audience, deliverables, schedule, expectations — convert inquiries into briefs. Listen for the unsaid, write back in the client's vocabulary, and update in writing whenever scope moves. The brief guides the plan, the shoot, the contract, and the delivery.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK

- The brief is not a contract — it informs one; say so if the relationship is discussed.
- Client examples must be fictional or consented; no real client correspondence without permission.
- Vocabulary advice ("their words, not jargon") is presented as communication practice, not a psychology claim.
- Verify the demonstration brief text is legible on camera and matches the narration.
