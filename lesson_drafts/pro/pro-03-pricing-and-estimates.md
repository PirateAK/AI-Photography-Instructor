## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT

**Welcome**

Pricing feels like guesswork because photographers quote the SHOOT and forget the iceberg around it. An estimate is a translation: the client's brief, converted into the time, rights, and costs it actually takes. This lesson builds one line by line — with example numbers, clearly labeled as examples.

**The cost side: time you forget to count**

Count everything the project consumes. Preparation: the consultation, the brief, location scouting. Shooting: travel, setup, the session itself. Editing: selection time plus per-image finishing — multiply honestly by the deliverable count. Administration: emails, estimates, invoicing, delivery. A half-day shoot is routinely a three-day project. Rate: your hourly cost of doing business — gear, insurance, software, taxes, and the months you don't sell — plus profit. Whatever shape your pricing takes, the estimate must SHOW this time, or clients will believe photography happens at shutter speed.

**Licensing: what the image will DO**

Licensing is the usage the client buys, and it's priced by reach and duration. A bakery's website images: a straightforward, small-scope use. The same images in a national advertising campaign: a different conversation entirely. The Beginner confusion to retire here: clients don't buy photographs, they buy USES of photographs — and broader, longer uses cost more. Define the usage in the brief and the license mirrors it.

**Expenses and profit**

Expenses are project-specific and pass through: travel, prints, props, an assistant, parking. Profit is not a wage — it's what buys the next lens and covers the empty weeks. An estimate that recovers only costs is a hobby with paperwork.

**Demonstration: the fictional bakery estimate**

The bakery brief from Lesson 2: twenty web images plus a few print-worthy, one half-day shoot, two weeks out. The estimate, line by line: consultation and planning (1 hr), scouting (1 hr), shoot including travel and setup (5 hrs), selection and editing (8 hrs for the deliverable count), administration and delivery (2 hrs) — seventeen hours at the example rate. Expenses: travel. Licensing: web and social use for the bakery's own channels, twelve months, defined in the license summary. Profit margin as chosen. Total presented as one number, with the line items visible — and every number here is an EXAMPLE, not a rate card.

**Presenting the estimate**

Send it itemized: scope recap from the brief, the line items, the license summary, payment terms, and the expiry date — prices and schedules change, and the estimate should say so. The itemization isn't just transparency; it's the scope-change mechanism: when the client asks for forty images instead of twenty, you both can see which line moves.

**Learner practice**

Build the bakery estimate yourself with your own rate and hours, then build one for a different brief — and compare which line surprised you.

**Recap**

Price the iceberg: preparation, shooting, editing, administration, licensing, expenses, profit. License what the image will DO. Present itemized, with terms and an expiry. The estimate translates the brief into the business.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE

Build two estimates:

1. The bakery brief from Lesson 2 — your rate, your hours, the license scope, itemized.
2. A different brief of your invention — compare which line changed most.
3. For each: present it itemized with payment terms and an expiry date.
4. Have someone read it as the client and mark anything unclear.

Write down the line you were tempted to hide — that's usually the one clients most deserve to see.

## 7. RECAP

Price the whole iceberg: prep, shoot, edit, administer — plus licensing for what the image will DO, plus expenses, plus profit. Present itemized with terms and expiry. The estimate translates the brief into the business, and its line items are how scope changes stay honest.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK

- All numbers in the demonstration are fictional examples — stated on screen — and are not presented as market rates.
- Licensing practice varies by jurisdiction and industry; this lesson teaches the structure, not legal advice — direct contract and license language questions to a qualified professional.
- Tax handling varies by region; pricing here excludes tax-specific guidance.
- Do not present day-rate or licensing models as the only valid approaches; different markets price differently.
