# Pro Lesson 11 — Working with Assistants and Creative Teams

Class: Pro
Prerequisite: Pro Lesson 6

## 1. LEARNING OUTCOME

By the end of this lesson, learners can assign roles, communicate expectations, and coordinate with assistants and creative collaborators — running a simple team briefing and keeping everyone updated through a shoot.

## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT

**Welcome**

The first time you hand a camera bag to someone else, you learn what a director does. Working with assistants and creative teams is the step from making photographs to producing them — and it runs on the same habits as the client relationship: clear roles, written expectations, honest updates.

**The roles: what each pair of hands owns**

Every helper gets a NAME and a JOB. An assistant carries, stages, and anticipates gear; holds reflectors; guards the equipment bag during breaks. A second shooter covers different frames — details, candids, the reverse angle — with an agreed boundary so no one duplicates. A stylist arranges the subject; a coordinator herds the schedule and the people. The failure mode is two people both "helping" — assign ownership, not enthusiasm.

**The team briefing: one page, one meeting**

Before the shoot, one page circulated and five minutes of meeting. The page holds: the brief in one line, the schedule's anchors, each person's role and gear, the shot list's priorities, and how updates will flow during the day. The meeting confirms everyone can say their job out loud. Twenty minutes of briefing prevents two hours of on-set guessing — the shoot-planning lesson's return on investment, multiplied by heads.

**Communication on set: quiet, clear, repeated**

On set, the director's voice is quiet, clear, and repeated. Requests go to a person by name: "Sam — reflector to the left, please." Changes are announced to everyone, not muttered to one: "we're swapping to the counter series next." And the schedule's anchors get restated at each transition: "counter done — exterior at four." Silence reads as things going fine; unannounced changes read as chaos.

**Demonstration: briefing a two-person team**

The bakery shoot, scaled up. The page: brief, anchors, roles — Sam on reflector and gear, Priya on detail frames — update flow by message at each transition. The five-minute meeting: each reads their role aloud; questions surface now, not mid-shoot. On set: quiet named requests, transitions announced, the bonus frame discussed once and assigned. After: feedback to each person — specific, honest, and including what they did better than expected. Teams re-form around people who brief well and close the loop.

**Updates and feedback: the loop after the shoot**

The loop closes after delivery: thank-yous, payment promptly, and one piece of specific feedback per person. Feedback that names behavior — "the reflector was always where I needed it before I asked" — builds the team for next time. A little loyalty among a small, well-briefed group is how production value compounds.

**Learner practice**

Run one shoot — any scale — with at least one helper: one-page brief, five-minute meeting, named requests on set, announced transitions, and closing feedback. Write down what you'd brief differently next time.

**Recap**

Name and job for every pair of hands; one page and five minutes of briefing; quiet named requests and announced transitions on set; closing feedback after. Directing is a skill — and the same habits that run clients run teams.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE

Run one shoot with at least one helper:

1. Write the one-page team brief: brief line, anchors, roles, gear, update flow.
2. Hold the five-minute meeting; have each person say their job aloud.
3. On set: use named requests; announce every transition.
4. Close with specific feedback for each helper, including one thing they did better than expected.
5. Write down what you'd brief differently next time — and whether the page needs a standing update.

## 7. RECAP

Every helper gets a name and a job. One page and five minutes of briefing prevent on-set guessing. On set: quiet, clear, repeated — requests by name, transitions announced. Close the loop with specific, prompt feedback. The same habits that run clients run teams.

## 8. ACCURACY CHECK

- Helper appearances must be consented; staged demonstrations labeled as such.
- Employment and payment practices for assistants vary by jurisdiction — the lesson teaches coordination, not employment terms; flag legal/financial specifics to a qualified professional.
- Second-shooter boundaries (no duplicating the primary's frames) are conventions, not rules; present as agreements between shooters.
- Verify the briefed roles on screen match the actual behavior shown.
