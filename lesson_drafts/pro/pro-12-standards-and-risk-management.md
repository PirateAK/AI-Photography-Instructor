## 3. NARRATION SCRIPT

**Welcome**

The Advanced class closed with ethics; the Pro class closes with risk. Same instinct, professional scale: the shoot's worst day is planned for before it happens. Risk management isn't pessimism — it's the quiet confidence of a photographer who already decided what to do when things go wrong.

**The risk walk: five categories**

Walk every planned shoot through five risk categories. People: safety of subject, crew, and public. Places: permissions, access, weather exposure. Equipment: failure, theft, damage — and what its loss stops. Data: the files' journey from card to archive. Reputation: what could this shoot do to your name if it went wrong? Five walks, five lists — written, because written contingencies are decisions and improvised ones are hopes.

**People and safety first**

People outrank photographs, always. Scout the shoot location for hazards — traffic, water, heights, unstable structures — and plan positions away from them. Weather is a people risk: heat, cold, lightning. If the plan puts anyone at risk for a photograph, the plan is wrong; the contingency lesson's alternatives exist precisely for this. Brief everyone on the hazards you identified — safety information is part of the team briefing, not a footnote.

**Permissions, insurance, and the paper shield**

Permissions: verify location access — permits where required, property owner consent, and the specific rules of venues; the ethics lesson's reasoning now wears a paperwork hat. Insurance: professional liability and equipment coverage exist, and whether they're required grows with every paid shoot; what coverage fits is a question for a qualified insurance professional in your region — not something this lesson decides. Paper shield: the agreement's cancellation, weather, and liability clauses — from the contracts lesson — are the pre-written answers when a shoot goes wrong.

**Demonstration: the scenario walked and planned**

The bakery exterior shot, walked. People: the sidewalk is public — keep gear out of foot traffic; the client's staff stay clear of the tripod's arc. Places: the shopfront is the client's — their permission, confirmed in writing weeks ago. Weather: rain ends the exterior — contingency, the interior series swaps in, already on the shot list. Equipment: a spare battery, a second card, and the insurance conversation noted with a professional. Data: the card discipline from Lesson 7 — nothing formats until delivery. Reputation: no identifiable passers-by without consent, per the ethics positions. Five walks, five lines on the plan — and shoot day carries them like the checklist.

**The written positions, completed**

The ethics lesson started the written positions; risk management completes them. The document now holds: consent practice, manipulation lines, publication rules — and the risk habits: permissions verified, insurance discussed with a professional, contingencies pre-decided. This document is the professional practice's constitution — boring on the best day, priceless on the worst.

**Learner practice**

Take a planned shoot and complete the five-category risk walk in writing, with a contingency line per category. Then update your written positions document with the risk habits.

**Recap**

Walk every shoot through five risks — people, places, equipment, data, reputation — and write a contingency line for each. People and safety outrank photographs. Permissions verified, insurance discussed with a qualified professional, clauses pre-written. And the written positions document — ethics plus risk — is the professional's constitution.

## 6. LEARNER PRACTICE

Take a planned shoot (the bakery, or a real brief) through the five-category risk walk:

1. People: identify hazards; write the safety brief line for the team.
2. Places: verify permissions in writing; note venue rules.
3. Equipment: list failure points and the backup for each.
4. Data: confirm the card discipline and backup stages from Lesson 7 apply.
5. Reputation: what could this shoot do to your name — and what's the line you won't cross?

Write one contingency line per category, then update your written positions document with the risk habits. Discuss insurance fit with a qualified insurance professional — not this video.

## 7. RECAP

Five risk walks — people, places, equipment, data, reputation — each ending in a written contingency. People and safety outrank photographs; permissions are craft, not obstacle; insurance questions go to qualified professionals; the agreement's clauses are pre-written answers. The written positions document — ethics plus risk — is the practice's constitution.
